# Chapter: Lanterns

They had won, so there was a party.

It was held at the short-legged table, and everybody did their best. Fred had got the bucket out. Seamus went round the common room with a plate of something, telling people that a win was a win, and Lee Jordan gave the last sixty feet of the dive, with both hands, for the benefit of some second-years who had seen it from the stand. Somebody had pinned the Snitch to the front of Harry’s jumper by one wing. It kept trying to leave. He sat on the arm of the sofa with a butterbeer that he had been holding for twenty minutes, and every time the talk dropped, you could hear what everyone was not humming.

It was a very good tune. That was the trouble with it. Dean had got as far as the second line, under his breath, while he was looking for a Chocolate Frog, and he stopped dead and went scarlet and looked behind him.

“Four,” said George, to nobody, turning his mug round on the table.

“He’ll be all right,” said Fred.

Neither of them looked as if he thought so. Harry looked at the portrait hole.

He had known. He had known for years; he had sat on a floor in September with a book on his knee and told somebody that he’d get it. He had known about the first goal and the second. He had even known, roughly, how the chorus went, though in the other life it had been about something else, and there had been a crown in it. Six or seven things that you could say to a person had been lying ready in his head since August, and he had gone through them in the air, on a stolen second, with the wind cracking and the whole of a green stand singing at his best friend’s back. He had thought, *I’ll tell him it gets better. It did.* Then the Snitch had gone past somebody’s boot and he had stopped thinking about Ron altogether.

That was the part he kept coming back to. He had looked across the pitch. There had been a girl up there, level with him, not singing and not stopping it, with something the matter with the way she held her left arm. There had been a corner of paper in her sleeve. He had come down sixty feet with his shoulder against hers, and he had been looking at that.

“Where is he?” said a voice at his elbow.

Ginny had a towel round her neck and her jaw set. She had flown two laps of the pitch as soon as the stands had emptied, as she had taken to doing, and she looked as though she would have liked to have somebody to do it to. She took the butterbeer out of Harry’s hand, drank some, and gave it back.

“He didn’t come in,” said Harry. “Hermione went to look.”

“I know she did. She came back. She says he’s in the shed, and he’s cleaning his broom, and he’s asked her to go away.” She looked round the room, at the bucket and the plate and the Snitch, with her eyes narrowed. “And he’s taken off his badge.”

Harry put down the butterbeer. He unpinned the Snitch. It went straight up, hit the ceiling, and started going round the lamp.

“Right,” said Ginny.

There was a lantern on a hook in the broom shed, turned low. It smelt of varnish and wet grass. Ron was sitting on an upturned bucket between the racks, in his Quidditch robes, with the mud drying on them and the new broom across his knees, going over the handle with a cloth. It was perfectly clean. He had been at it for some time, by the look of the cloth. The badge was lying on the shelf next to the tin of polish, with its pin open.

He looked up, and looked down.

“Well played,” said Ron.

“Ron—”

“No, it was. That was a brilliant dive. I watched it. I had a good view, I wasn’t doing anything.” He rubbed at something. “I’m going to tell Angelina tomorrow. She can have that third-year with his eyes shut. He’d have done better. He wouldn’t have been able to see them coming.”

Harry came in and sat down on the floor. There was nowhere else. He put his back against the rack, with a row of old Cleansweeps sticking into his neck, and he got out the first of the things.

“It’s only the first match.”

“Yeah.”

“Everybody lets in a few, the first time. Wood said he was sick before his—”

“Don’t,” said Ron.

He did not say it angrily. He went on with the cloth. Harry stopped, and looked at the other five or six things, and there was nothing whatever the matter with them. They were all perfectly true. He had worked on them. He thought of a person telling him, at fifteen, what it would be like when he was older.

“My mum knitted that scarf,” said Ron, after a while. “The one Ginny’s got. They had that in the song. I didn’t know you could get that much in. *Sieve* and *live*, I can see that, that’s just rhyming, but somebody sat down and thought about my dad. Somebody sat in a chair, with a quill. They’d got the Ford Anglia.” He turned the broom over and looked at the other side. “And I could hear all of it. It’s very clear up there. You wouldn’t know, you’re always going somewhere. You just stay in one place, and it comes to you.”

Harry didn’t say anything. It occurred to him that this was the first useful thing he had done all day.

“I got that broom for being a prefect,” said Ron. “My mum saved up. I don’t know how long. Nobody’s said. And I went out there in front of everyone in the whole school, and they looked at me, and I thought, *right*. I could feel it go. It was just like I was eleven, and that mirror.” He stopped. His big raw hands had stopped too. “You said I’d get it. On the floor. I keep thinking about that, I keep thinking what did you say that for. You weren’t even looking at me.”

There was a noise behind Harry. He looked round. Somebody had opened the door, and let in a good deal of cold and a smell of paraffin.

“Up,” said Ginny.

She had a lantern in each hand. They were the big storm kind that Hagrid hung round the pumpkin patch. Behind her, in a duffel coat with the hood up and her arms full, was Hermione. It was twenty to ten at night. It had begun to freeze.

“Go away, Ginny.”

“No. Get your gloves.”

“I’m not—”

“You let in four,” said Ginny. “I counted. So did everybody. They all went to your left. Every single one. I watched you from the stand, and you know what you did? You turned your head first. You look where you’re going before you go. You do it at chess, it’s why you win.” She walked in, kicked the bucket, gently, about an inch from his foot, and held out one of the lanterns. “It’s the easiest thing to mend that I’ve ever seen. You’d have it by Christmas, if you weren’t sitting in a shed. Get up. I’m not doing this in the morning, with people looking.”

“It’s after curfew,” said Ron. He said it in a dull voice, and he looked at the shelf. “I’m a prefect.”

Hermione put back her hood. She had gone rather pink about the nose, and she was holding a sheet of parchment, with a ruler laid along the top of it to keep it flat.

“I’ve put it on the rota,” said Hermione. “*Inspection of Quidditch hoops for frost damage, R. Weasley and H. Granger, 9.45 p.m. to midnight.* It’s perfectly in order. A prefect may inspect school property at her discretion. It’s Educational Decree Twenty-Four, page nine. I read it for somebody else.” She reached past him, picked the badge up off the shelf, and held it out on her palm. “You’ve left this,” she said. “I think you’ll want it, if we meet Filch.”

Nobody moved. The lantern hissed.

Ron looked at the badge. He looked at it for a good while, and his ears slowly went from white to something else. Then he looked at Harry, sitting on the floor with a Cleansweep in his neck.

“Are you going to say anything?” said Ron.

“I can throw,” said Harry.

He could not throw.

It was the most extraordinary discovery. He had been the best flier in the school for four years, in two lives. He had caught a Remembrall out of the air at eleven, and he had once outflown a dragon. He drew back his arm at the edge of the scoring area, in the freezing dark, with four lanterns hung on the hoops and Ginny hovering at his shoulder; and the Quaffle left his hand, wobbled through the air in a slow and thoughtful arc, and landed on the grass about thirty feet to the right of anything.

There was a silence from the hoops.

“Was that a pass?” said Ron’s voice.

“It slipped.”

“Who to?”

“Your *elbow’s* out,” said Ginny, with enormous pleasure. “Do it again. Nobody ever told me that. Four years you’ve been up there with that face on. Do it *again*.”

He did it again. It went backwards. He had a hand that didn’t close properly, was the fact of the matter, and it was the one he threw with; he had got the sleeve pulled down over it and his teeth together. He wasn’t going to mention that. The next one went straight up. The one after that came down on Hermione, who was standing below the middle hoop in her duffel coat with a lantern on a stick, reading out of *Quidditch Through the Ages* the regulations on the size of a Keeper’s gloves. She said “Ow,” and looked up, and took off her mitten and inspected the page. Somebody started to make a noise.

It came from up by the hoops, a kind of wheeze. Harry shaded his eyes. He could see the long dark shape against the stars, and it was bent over the handle of its broom, with its shoulders going.

“You—” said Ron. He couldn’t get it out. “You throw like—like Neville’s *gran*. Like somebody getting rid of a cat—”

“I’d like to see you catch one!”

“I’d have to *find* it first!”

It went on for rather a long time. He couldn’t have said, afterwards, what was so funny. It was very cold. He sat down in the frost with his legs stuck out, because he had got a stitch; Hermione was laughing and holding her head; and Ron had come down about twenty feet without appearing to know it, hanging on to the broom with one hand, wiping his eyes with the other, and shouting something about a cat.

Ginny waited. She sat there on the air with the Quaffle under one arm, and when it had gone on as long as she thought it should, she leaned forward, and went.

She came in from the left, flat and very fast, with her red hair tied back and a lantern swinging behind her. Her arm came over. Ron had got one hand on the broom and his mouth open. He didn’t turn his head. He hadn’t had the time. He went out along the handle, all arm, and the Quaffle hit the heel of his glove with a smack like a door shutting, and went away over the stand.

For a moment nobody said anything. Then Ron looked at his glove.

“Oh,” said Ron.

“*There*,” said Ginny. “That’s all it is. Now, again. And if you look at me, I’ll put it through your teeth.”

They kept at it until the lanterns started to burn low. Ginny threw and Ron stopped them or he didn’t; Harry fetched. It turned out that that was what he was for. He ran about in the dark after a large leather ball, with his breath going up in clouds and his bad hand under his arm, and Hermione kept the tally with a mitten on, in two columns, by the light of a lantern on a stick. Eleven out of thirty. Fourteen out of thirty. At some point after midnight she told them that the frost damage had been found to be within acceptable limits, and nobody took any notice.

He had not thought about anything for two hours.

He noticed that on the way back, going up the long slope, with the castle black against the stars and a window or two still lit. His feet had gone. Ginny had walked on ahead with the lanterns, and Hermione had taken Ron’s broom, for some reason, and was carrying it in both arms. She was telling Ginny something about the Statute of something. Ron dropped back. He had got the badge in his hand. He stopped, and pinned it on, in the dark, with his big cold fingers, and it took him three goes.

“Thanks,” said Ron.

“I fetched.”

“Yeah, you’re brilliant at that.” He blew on his hands. They went on for a bit. There was frost on the grass, and it creaked. “Nineteen, I reckon, by the end of the week. I’ll tell Angelina I’m not going anywhere. She’d only shout.”

“Good.”

Ron walked on for a while without saying anything. He looked up at the castle. Over to the left, down by the black water, there was nothing to see at all, and he looked at that.

“She was right above it,” he said.

Harry’s feet stopped hurting. He couldn’t have said why.

“Malfoy. All through the match. I looked, when I wasn’t doing anything else. She could’ve stopped that. Hermione says, she says she did it on the train in about a minute. She was just up there, like a—” He shook his head. He didn’t sound angry; he sounded tired, and rather puzzled, as you would about a sum. “You were up there as well. You’d have been about level. I’m not saying anything. You got the Snitch, that’s the job. I’m just saying. She’s your— She used to be. You could’ve said something to her, mate. You could’ve flown over. It wouldn’t have taken a minute.”

The frost creaked. A long way ahead, a lantern went in at the doors.

He had a number of answers. It was the sort of thing he was good at. He thought of a window on the sixth floor, and the point of her chin. *Eighty-five*, he had told her a week ago, with his arms round her, and he had thought it was the funniest thing that anybody had ever said. He thought of a corner of paper in a sleeve, and how long he’d looked at it. He had been able to tell you, to the inch, which way her wrist was turned.

“Yeah,” said Harry. “I could.”

Ron nodded. He gave Harry a shove with his shoulder, not hard, as you’d shove a door that sticks, and he went on up the hill.
