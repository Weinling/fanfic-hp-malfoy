# Chapter 97: Under Owing

The book had forty-one headings, and Vanessa Crabbe had ruled every one of them herself.

She kept it in a long narrow book with a stiff black spine, bought in Diagon Alley in the August before her fourth year for a Sickle and nine, out of money that she had been given for gloves. *Almonds. Butter. Coal. Eggs (duck). Eggs (hen). Flour.* Each had its page and its column, in the small round hand that nobody had ever called anything but clear; and down every margin, in pencil, a great deal larger and slower and rounder, went the other one. *Good. More nutmeg. Ness says no. Ness says no AGAIN.* On the page for *Marble* there was a drawing of a slab with rays coming off it, and underneath, in the round hand, the words *one day*.

There was no heading for what one did when half of it stopped.

She sat on the end of the bench in the dormitory, in the first week of January, with the book on her knee and a stub of pencil behind her ear, and went through it from *Almonds*, as she had on every one of the last twenty nights; and it did not come out. It had not been made to; it had been made for two people, one who knew what an oven wanted and one who knew what that cost, and one could move the figures about until the lake froze over, and at the foot of every page there was still somebody missing, who would have done the baking.

Eight Galleons a quarter. A year in advance, in two lots: sixteen down. She had eleven Galleons, four Sickles and a Knut, in a stocking, which was two years of not buying anything; and she had one Galleon more that was not hers, polished, with a square of card tied to it by a thread. *As an investment. I expect nothing.* She had found it in the pocket of an apron.

She shut the book. The bed by the wall had been stripped on the Tuesday before Christmas and made up again with clean sheets, very flat. It no longer gave at the corner. Nobody sat on it. On the far side of the room, behind green hangings, somebody was lying on her right side with the lamp out, not asleep, and Vanessa knew that as she knew the price of suet; she had been listening to that person not sleep since they were eleven.

She did not look at the hangings. She had stopped looking on the third of December. One could, it turned out, go on knowing where a thing was for a long time after that.

The letter had come on the second of January, by a grey owl with a bad wing.

*Miss Crabbe. I am told that my daughter kept a book of receipts and that it is with you. I should be glad to have it. I have her clothes, and I find that they are no use to anybody. G. Goyle (Mrs).*

The writing covered one side of a small sheet, in a nib that wanted mending, and had been folded while it was wet. Vanessa read it standing up. Then she went to her trunk and took out the other book, the fat one, with the grease on its cover and the Muggle pencil still in the spine, which had been propped against a salt-cellar in the kitchens at six every morning for fourteen months; and she sat down with it, and a new exercise book, and a good nib.

She did not send it at once. She was sorry for that, afterwards, when she thought of the woman waiting. But it was the only one there was.

It took her seven nights. Greer had not written recipes as other people did. She had written what she’d done, and then what she ought to have done, and then, across both, at an angle, what had happened. *Walnut. 9. It wants more of something. WHAT.* There was butter on the page for butter. Under *Pastry (rough)* a very small hand had been drawn round in pencil, with the fingers spread, and the words *D.M., flour to here*. Vanessa copied that too. She drew round her own hand, and wrote beside it that it should be smaller.

She found on the second night that she could no longer make a capital *M*.

She had made it the same way since she was six: down, up, and a small hitch before the second leg, where the nib caught its breath. She had never known that she did it. Now she saw it every time. There are a great many of them in a book of receipts. *Mace. Marzipan. Milk, a pint, warm.* She would get to the top of the first stroke, and there it was, the hitch, on a sheet of cream paper in a folder that said *My friend*, in a dead woman’s desk; and her hand would stop in the air. She tried doing it another way. It came out like somebody else’s. In the end she did them as she had always done them, one after another, with her teeth together, because the book was Greer’s and Greer had been used to her *M*’s.

*Suet* came on the fifth night. There were dumplings, and a roll with jam, and a pudding for Christmas with the quantities for forty. And in the space after the pudding, where another person would have left a line, there was this.

*L. is horrible. Has been since the train. I know that. Writing it down so nobody thinks I didn’t.*

And beneath it, in a slightly different pencil:

*Bacon one, for L. White, thick. Crusts OFF, she won’t, she never has. Cold is all right. Don’t ask her does she want it. Put it down and go away.*

Vanessa read that for some time.

She had cried once, when she was twelve, and had been told by the person in the next bed that it was a cold. She did not suppose that this was the same thing. It was only that the page had gone, and the lamp with it, and something very large had sat down on the middle of her chest, as somebody might who meant it kindly and did not know her own weight. She put the nib down so that it should not blot. She waited. She could not see the lines again until a quarter past one, and when she could, she copied both entries, word for word, with the capitals; because Mrs Goyle had asked for the book, and that was what was in it.

At six in the morning the kitchens smelt of bread, as they always had.

She went down on the Thursday because there were things of Greer’s there still. An apron on a nail, a rolling pin that nobody else was allowed to use, a tin with *G.G. NOT YOURS* scratched in the lid: she had thought that she would take them and go. She came in past the pear, and the whole long room stopped, a hundred pairs of eyes at about the height of her knee; and then it started again, more quietly, and somebody very small put a stool behind her without being asked, as though she had only been away for the holidays.

It was the right stool, the one where she had always sat, at the end of the scrubbed table, with the book, saying numbers.

Somebody was at the table already. She had a great deal of brown hair, tied back with what looked like a bootlace, and her sleeves pushed up, and a long pink scar on one arm; and she was sorting paper into three piles with the expression of somebody doing a thing because it could at least be done. She looked up. She had been crying at some point in the last hour and had made no arrangements about it.

“I’ve got a rota,” said Hermione Granger.

Vanessa sat down on the stool.

“I made it in November. I never showed her. We’d had a— She said they could come and help on their half-day, in the shop, if they liked, and she’d give them pie, and I said that that was exactly the difficulty, that one couldn’t build a business on people being *given pie*, and she told me that I’d put too much sugar in the custard.” It was coming out rather fast. “So I went away and did it properly. Hours and wages. It’s in three colours. There are eleven of them who want to. I was going to bring it down at the end of term, when I’d checked the arithmetic.” She pushed a sheet across the table. Her hand was not quite steady. “I don’t know whom it belongs to now. I thought it might be you.”

Vanessa looked at it. It was very good work. The sevens were all the same.

“You’ve got them at a Sickle an hour,” she said.

“It’s the least that one could—”

“The shop won’t clear a Sickle an hour for a year and a half. I’ve done it both ways.” She took the pencil from behind her ear. “You could have them at four Knuts and a share. A share’s better. They’d be owed something.”

Granger stared at her. Then she pulled the sheet back and began, without a word, to cross out a column; and for about a quarter of an hour the two of them sat at the end of a kitchen table at six in the morning, a Gryffindor and a Slytherin who had not exchanged ten sentences in five years, and moved figures. They did not become friends. Vanessa would have noticed.

A boy was at the big stone sink, in an apron, with his back to them.

He was long and mild and had a good many spots, and he washed up with his left hand and the edge of his right forearm, having worked out how. He had been coming down at six since the middle of December. Nobody had asked him. He did the tins. The first morning, an elf had tried to take the brush from him, and he had said *No, thank you*, quite gently, and gone on; and Vanessa, on her stool, had looked at that right hand, with the words standing white on the back of it, and at the pencil in her own.

*The third of November, last year: Wayne Hopkins’s list.* She had it written down. She had told it to somebody in a dormitory because she had thought that somebody would be pleased.

He did not turn round. He never did. He rinsed a tin and set it on the rack, exactly square.

“There’s a boy gone,” said Granger suddenly.

She had her quill in the air. She was frowning at a line near the bottom, in green.

“The small one. With the bow tie. He used to come at six and hit the dough; she’d put him down for Saturdays, look, there’s a *D.* with a ring round it. He hasn’t been at a meal since we got back.” Her eyes came up, brown and level and adding. “Where is he?”

“At home,” said Vanessa.

“Why?”

“I don’t know.”

That was true. She knew only what the whole of Slytherin knew: that Lyra Malfoy had come in at the great doors on Sunday with her neck bare and a line down it like a bootlace, and stopped in front of six hundred people to say that her family had other arrangements. She had said it in the pleasant voice. Vanessa had heard that voice explain a great many things since the first of September, 1991, and had given up believing it at about the same rate as she had given up looking.

On the rack behind them, under a cloth, something round and dark was cooling, with a shine on it. The elves made one every Thursday now. They had it from the fat black book, which they’d had the reading of before she had. Nobody had told them to, and nobody ate it in the kitchens; it went up to the Slytherin common room at nine at night on a board, by itself, and was set down on the green baize in front of an empty chair.

He marked it. He had not missed a week.

The game went on in the corner by the fire, because Slytherin did not know how to stop a thing once it had been going three years. Nott dealt. Montague lost. At nine the cake would be there, and Blaise Zabini would lay his cards face down and align them, and take up the fork. He ate one mouthful with his eyes shut. Then he took the pencil from behind his ear and a square of card from his top pocket, and wrote on it, on his knee; and he put the card back in the pocket without showing it to anybody.

Parkinson had asked him once, in the second week, what it had got. Zabini had turned his head and looked at him, as if at something on the sole of a shoe, until he went away.

On such a night, with the cake cut and going round, Nott put the lease down in front of her.

She had not known that he had it. She had left it in the long book, and the long book on the arm of the bench, for the time it took to fetch a pen. He had read it. He had his long dull lawyer’s face on, and he laid one finger on the second paragraph and another on the fifth.

“She can’t have both,” said Nott. “A year in advance and a right to enter at a week’s notice. Either you’ve paid for the year or you haven’t. And there’s no chimney in the schedule. If it isn’t in the schedule it isn’t yours, and it’s the only reason that you want the place.” He took his fingers away. He did not look at her; he looked at the lease, as he had looked once at a horse that he was thinking of describing. “I shouldn’t sign it like that. I’d send it back. I could write the letter, if it would be a convenience.”

She had never heard him make so long a speech. He had gone pink along the cheekbone by the end of it.

She looked at the second paragraph, and the fifth. He was quite right. She ought to have seen it. She had been reading that paper for a month with her mind on a sock.

“It’s sixteen down,” said Vanessa. “I’ve got twelve. That’s counting his.”

Nott felt in his pocket. She had the words ready; she had been ready with them since the summer her father was taken. He put four Galleons on the baize, one on top of another, and on the top of those a chess piece: a black knight, rather worn about the ears, which had come out of that pocket once before, in a corridor, and gone back.

“It’s my grandfather’s set,” said Nott. “There was a man in Knockturn Alley at Christmas who gave me four for the other one and thought he had robbed me. I don’t play any more. One wants somebody to play.” He pushed the pile an inch across the cloth. “It isn’t a present. You’d owe me four Galleons. You’d put me down. You can have that to keep the place.”

She had forty-one headings. She ran through them, standing there. There wasn’t one for this.

“I’d have to rule a page,” she said.

“Yes,” said Nott. “I thought you would.”

She took them. The knight was warm from his pocket. She sat down at the end of the bench with the long book, and turned to the first clean leaf, and ruled it: a line across, a line down. She thought about what to write at the head of it. In the end she wrote *Owing*, with a capital, and underneath, *T. Nott, 4 Gall. One knight, held*; and when she looked up he had gone back to the cards and was dealing, a little unevenly, and Zabini was watching the two of them over the rim of a glass with no expression whatever.

It was the next night that the paper came.

She had gone up early. The dormitory was empty, with the lake going over the ceiling in slow green bars, and she had the fat book done up in brown paper on her knee, addressed, with the copy she was keeping beside it. She was trying to write the covering letter. *Dear Mrs Goyle.* She had done that much nine times.

The door opened. She knew the step. She did not raise her head.

It came across the boards and stopped at the foot of her bed, where she herself had stood on a night in December with a sheet of Potions notes in her hand. It said nothing. She could smell the soap that was sent from France. Then a hand came down into the lamplight: the right one, thin, with ink on the second finger, and laid on the blanket beside the parcel a single sheet of the good paper, folded once.

Vanessa looked at the hand. It was shaking. In five and a half years she had seen it hold a wand, and a Snitch, and a teacup under Dolores Umbridge’s nose, and she had never seen it do that.

She unfolded the paper.

The writing was not hers. She had been afraid, for an instant, that it would be. It was the other: the upright, narrow, beautiful hand that had headed every list in Slytherin since they were eleven, the best in the House and well aware of it; and across the top, underlined twice, it said:

*Not one word aloud, Vanessa. Read it and write.*

She very nearly spoke, to spite it. She had been told what to do by that hand for a long time. But the rest was there underneath, and her eyes had gone down before she could stop them.

*Sunday the 15th of December, 1996. There was a box in my trunk. It held twenty-four chocolates, and they were poisoned. It was given to me in July to carry to the Headmaster on the last night of term, and I knew what it was. At ten past three I left it open on my bed for five minutes. When I came back Greer had it on her knee. She thought that I had been keeping them from her. She took one.*

*I was in the doorway. I told her to put it down. I did not tell her why. I could have. There was time to say it, and I had the word in my mouth, and I did not say it. She was dead in a little more than a minute. She offered me the next one.*

*I put the box away before I called anyone. Professor Snape said that it was her heart, and I let him. It was not her heart.*

*I am not asking you for anything.*

*L. Malfoy. The 10th of January, 1997.*

The lake went over the ceiling.

She read it again. She found that she was reading the figures. *Ten past three. Five minutes. A little more than a minute.* It was like her; she had wanted the times right. Vanessa had come to that door, that afternoon, with cold air on her and a book against her chest, and had stood on the threshold and looked at a pair of boots and two wet marks and a big pale hand lying open on the floor; and she had thought: *her heart*. She had gone on thinking it for twenty-six days. She had written to a man in Tutshill to say that the marble would not be wanted.

*She offered me the next one.*

She had her pencil in her hand, and did not remember taking it from behind her ear. Under the signature was a space about three fingers deep, and she wrote in it, in the plainest hand in the world. The *W* gave her no trouble.

*Why didn’t you shout.*

She held it out without looking up. It was taken. She heard a pen, not for long; and when it came back there were four lines, and the beautiful hand had gone all over the place.

*Because it would have been heard. Not by her. My mother is alive on condition that certain things are not said near me, and so is Draco. I did the sum. I had got to the bottom of it by the time she had swallowed. It came out.*

*Heard.*

Vanessa raised her eyes at last.

She was standing very straight, in the square-necked grey, with the lamp on one side of her. It ran down from under her ear, white and thick, and in at the top of the sling. She had put her right hand over her left wrist, below the black silk, and was holding it, hard, as one holds something shut; and her face was the one that Vanessa had seen once before in her life, at twelve, in a corridor, when she had picked a wand up off the floor and given it back.

She had known what was under that cuff since September. Everybody’s father had one. She had not known that it listened.

She thought of a dormitory with the lamp out, on a Friday in December, and a slow voice saying *Budge up*. *There’s nobody in there, Greer. She’ll have you carrying something.* There had been somebody behind those hangings, a yard away, with her arm on the blanket. And there had been somebody else.

She could have done a number of things then. She saw them quite clearly, set out as on a page. She could have got up and gone to Professor McGonagall, whose door she had stood outside on the last day of term for the length of a lesson. She could have added one line to a letter. *Dear Mrs Goyle. It was not her heart.* She looked at the parcel on her knee. A woman in the north, with a husband in Azkaban and a wardrobe of clothes that were no use to anybody, had asked for a book of receipts; and for a very little ink she could be given the truth along with it. Vanessa could think of nobody who had a better right.

And then there would be the sum. She could do sums. A woman she had curtsied to at eleven, and a boy who had been taught to hit dough. She did not know how it was arranged, or by whom, and she did not need to; she had seen that hand shake.

She did not understand it. She wished that to be clear, if only to herself. She could see how the figures went, and that they came out, and she still did not understand how a person stood in a doorway and did them. She thought that she probably never would, and that there was nothing in that to be ashamed of.

“You counted,” said Vanessa.

Neither of them said anything else. It was not loud. She saw it go in, and she saw that it was worse than anything else that she could have found, and she was not sorry.

She took the paper back. She wrote under the four lines, slowly, with the hitch in the *M* where it had always been.

*I am keeping this. It goes in my book. If anybody else ever eats anything that comes from you, it goes to Madam Bones’s niece the same day, and after that I don’t mind who has it. V. Crabbe.*

She let her read that. She waited until she had seen her get to the end. Then she opened the long book at the new leaf, where there was one line already, about a knight; and she laid the sheet in, flat, under the heading, and shut it.

The other one stood a moment longer. Her mouth had started to go. She did nothing about it; she did not put up her hand, or turn her face out of the lamp, and it ran down and into the corner of the scar. Vanessa watched it, as she had watched everything else. She did not say that this wasn’t anything.

When the door had closed she took up her pen again.

*Dear Mrs Goyle. Here is the book. I have kept a copy, which I hope you will not mind, as she meant me to have the use of it. She wrote things in it that are not receipts. I have left them in. Yours respectfully, Vanessa Crabbe.*

There was room for another line. She looked at the place for a long time. Then she blotted it, and folded it, and put it under the string.

The lease came back on the twentieth, with the chimney written in and the second paragraph struck out, and a note from the landlady to say that she had never been spoken to like that by a schoolboy and would he care for a position.

Vanessa signed it at the table in the common room, with Nott for a witness. She sent sixteen Galleons by the school owl, in a stocking. The key was iron, and as long as her hand.

She painted the board herself, on the dormitory floor, on a Sunday, with a pot of green from Filch and a ruler. She had the lettering from the lid of a tin. There had been an argument once about what went on it. *Ness wants Goyle and Crabbe. She says that people like to know who they’re suing.* It had got a laugh, in a pub, from somebody who very seldom laughed; and a pencil had started up again, and an ear had gone pink.

She gave it one name. It took her most of the afternoon to get the apostrophe where it belonged.

On the Monday she went down by the kitchens, with her bag. She did not go in. She went on along the passage to where the barrels were, stacked in their recess, and stood there, in the smell of onions, in a place where eighty people had stood in December beside a chair with a plate on it. Her cousin had told her which barrel. She had not asked what one did to it. She waited, as she was used to doing, with her back to the wall and her feet together; and at ten to six the lid of the middle one swung out, and a square pale girl with a plait over her shoulder came through it, with her left hand in her pocket.

She stopped. She had sat down next to Vanessa at breakfast, once, on a Monday, and been told no.

Vanessa took a sheet out of her bag: a page of Herbology, done in the fourth year for somebody who had not cared to take her own. She held it out.

“It’s my hand,” she said. “On your aunt’s letters. The *M*. I knew it when you showed me, and I said I didn’t.” Her voice was low, and quite reasonable, and she could hear it shaking. “I didn’t write them. I’ve never written to your aunt. But it’s mine, and I thought that you ought to have it from me.”
