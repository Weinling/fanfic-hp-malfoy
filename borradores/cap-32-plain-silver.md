# Chapter 32: Plain Silver

“I choose my partner, Headmaster.”

Viktor Krum said it from the door of the cabin, with snow melting on the shoulders of his furs, in the flat, heavy English he used for everything from Quidditch to the weather. He had been sent for. He had come in, and stood, and listened to his headmaster explain to him in Lyra’s hearing what an honour was being done him, and what a pair they would make, and how pleased Mr Malfoy would be; and when Karkaroff stopped for breath, he said that.

“Viktor—”

“I have asked already.” Krum did not look at Karkaroff. He looked at Lyra, quite steadily, without any embarrassment at all. “She says yes.”

Then he nodded to her, as one nods across a pitch to a Seeker one has nothing against, and went back out into the snow.

Lyra put down the cup she had not been drinking from.

She had come down to the ship because the Ball was in three days and Viktor Krum had not said one word to her in eleven. Slytherin had begun to notice. Montague had asked at breakfast, with great innocence, whether Krum meant to ask her by owl, as they did in Bulgaria, and Lyra had said that in Bulgaria they did not need to ask, since everybody understood; and she had gone down to the lake straight afterwards with her face very calm, because a thing said in front of the whole of Slytherin had to come true, or else she was a liar, and Lyra Malfoy was a great many things in that common room but she had never once been caught out in that.

“Miss Malfoy.” Karkaroff had gone a curious colour over his beard. “A misunderstanding. The boy is young, he has his head full of— But we are not without resources! There is Poliakoff. A very good family. His father and your father have done business together for—”

“Do I look,” said Lyra, “like something off a sale table, sir?”

She did not wait for the answer. She went out on to the deck, past three Durmstrang boys who had very obviously been listening at the porthole and who straightened up as she passed as though they might be in with a chance, and down the gangplank, one wet step at a time.

At the bottom, on the shingle, Krum caught her up.

He did not hurry about it. He came down the gangplank behind her with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders hunched, and stopped a yard off, frowning at the lake.

“Headmaster says many things,” he said. “He says them in your name. I did not know. I am sorry for this.”

“You needn’t be,” said Lyra. “I know whom you asked.”

He looked at her then, sharply, and she saw him remember the library, and the girl with the hair, and a question asked at the edge of a Quidditch pitch.

“You tell me she is not Potter’s,” said Krum slowly. “You tell me true?”

“I generally do,” said Lyra.

Krum considered that, as he had considered everything she had ever said to him, as though it were a broom that pulled to one side and he meant to find out which. Then he nodded once, and went back up the gangplank to his ship.

Lyra walked up the slope.

She did not mind that Viktor Krum was taking somebody else to the Ball. She told herself so twice, and the second time it was true. She had never wanted him. She knew exactly whom he was taking, and she wished them a very pleasant evening, and a great deal of hair.

What she minded was having been refused.

Nobody refused her. It was not a thing that happened. Boys asked, and she said no, kindly or otherwise, and they went away and asked Daphne; and today she had swallowed every scrap of pride she possessed and walked up a gangplank in the snow to let it be known, in the right quarter, that she would not be displeased; and she had been turned down, politely, in front of a man with yellow teeth who would very likely write to her father about it.

She had done it because Potter had asked her to. *Only go with Krum. Or look as if you are.*

He had a plan. He had said so. She did not know what it was, and he had not told her, and she had walked up that gangplank anyway; and now she wanted him to come round the corner of the greenhouses with that infuriating face and explain it to her, very pleased with himself, so that she could be extremely cold to him for about a minute and a half and then forgive him.

He did not come round the corner of the greenhouses.

Lyra stopped at the top of the slope, out of sight of the ship, and put her hand in her pocket and took hold of the ring, and for once she did the looking. She held it in her fist and thought, quite hard, of a boy on a desk with his feet on a chair, and waited for it to warm towards him.

It did not warm. It did not pulse. When she opened her hand it was lying in her palm exactly as it had come out of the box in Zonko’s: plain silver, and cold, and pointing nowhere at all.
