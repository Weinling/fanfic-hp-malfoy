# Chapter 1: Hostage

The night the Chamber of Secrets was closed, Harry Potter took Lyra Malfoy into an empty classroom, put her father’s diary on the desk with a hole punched through it, and informed her that she was now his hostage.

Until then, she had been having a perfectly good year.

Lyra Malfoy had many virtues, and she saw no reason to be modest about any of them. She was beautiful, for a start. She came from one of the oldest pure-blood families in Britain, and one of the busiest.

“Board of Governors, half the Ministry, the parents’ committee, and oh, look, there’s one in Slytherin as well. How are they *everywhere*?”

And she was clever and quick and very, very good on a broom, which was the part that seemed to upset people most.

Lyra took remarks like that as compliments. As far as she was concerned the school had a natural order, and she sat very comfortably near the top of it. Her real smiles she saved for people who had earned them, which at present meant her mother, her father, her little brother when he was behaving, and, on particularly good mornings, her own reflection.

There was, however, one flaw in the arrangement, and it wore glasses.

Lyra had taken against Harry Potter before she even knew who he was. He had been standing on the stool next to hers in Madam Malkin’s the summer before first year, a scrawny boy in clothes several sizes too big for him, and she had made perfectly friendly conversation about Quidditch and Houses and the sort of people one ought not to mix with, and he had answered in mumbles, edging away along his stool as though she might be catching. Then, on the Hogwarts Express, she had found out his name, gone along to his compartment, and offered him her hand in front of the Weasley boy. He had looked at it and told her he could work out the wrong sort for himself, thanks.

Nobody had ever refused Lyra Malfoy’s hand before. She had not enjoyed the novelty.

In fairness, and Lyra was always fair when it cost her nothing, he wasn’t bad-looking. Without those dreadful round glasses he might even have been called handsome, which was a purely objective assessment and said absolutely nothing about her. His hair was a lost cause. The rest of him was merely wasted.

What she couldn’t forgive was everything else. He had been raised by Muggles, and it showed: he wandered round the castle with his shirt untucked and his tie at half-mast, eating as though he expected somebody to take the plate away. Nobody called him harmless, though; Potter repaid grudges patiently and in full. His marks were respectable, which was entirely down to Granger, that know-it-all Mudblood, and since Granger had spent the last month lying petrified in the hospital wing, Lyra had been watching Potter’s marks with great interest.

Then there was Quidditch. This year Lyra had joined the Slytherin team as Seeker, opposite Potter, and her father had very kindly equipped the entire side with Nimbus Two Thousand and Ones, which had nothing whatsoever to do with anything. Granger had suggested otherwise, which was why Lyra had called her what she had called her, and why Weasley had spent that afternoon belching slugs. The November match she did not discuss: not the Snitch, which had apparently hovered beside her left ear for several seconds while she was busy telling Potter exactly what she thought of him, and not the broken arm he had caught it with, which people talked about as though it were heroic rather than simply careless.

And he kept ending up at the centre of everything. Last June he had snatched the House Cup out from under Slytherin’s nose, and Lyra had bitten a nail almost to the quick before Crabbe pulled her hand away from her mouth. This year half the school had called him the Heir of Slytherin, until he announced over dinner that they were welcome to, provided the Slytherins started kneeling; and Lyra, who knelt to nobody, had found herself in the absurd position of defending him.

Hogwarts without him, she sometimes thought, would be very nearly perfect. Quieter. Better run. Considerably less likely to involve giant snakes.

The news reached the dungeons a little before midnight, the way news always reached the Slytherin common room: sideways, in pieces, and from somebody’s cousin.

Ginny Weasley was alive. Dumbledore was back. Something enormous had been killed underneath the school, and Potter and Weasley had been involved, because naturally they had. A fifth-year said basilisk, and everybody laughed, Panther Parkinson so hard that he fell off the arm of his chair. Then Professor Snape appeared in the doorway with the expression of a man who had been ordered to be festive and was taking it personally, and announced a feast, now, in whatever they happened to be wearing. Nobody laughed about the basilisk after that.

Lyra refused to go up with her hair the way it was, and took ten minutes she didn’t really have in front of the mirror. When she came down, in her dark green dressing gown with the silver embroidery at the cuffs, she looked, if not her best, then at least deliberate. Her hands had not been entirely steady with the brush. The dungeons were always cold at night.

Her father had been in an excellent mood all year. At Christmas he had written, in the careful way he wrote anything that might be read by the wrong eyes, that she should keep her head down and let events take their course; she had asked what events, and he had answered with three pages about her Transfiguration marks. Lyra was not stupid. She knew he despised Arthur Weasley and wanted Dumbledore gone. She had been standing beside him in Flourish and Blotts last August when he picked the youngest Weasley’s battered Transfiguration book out of her cauldron, sneered at it, and dropped it back in, and she had been at school a few weeks later when the first message appeared on the wall. She had not asked herself any questions. Questions were for people whose fathers weren’t Lucius Malfoy, and her father had spent her entire life making sure that consequences happened to other people. Whatever had gone wrong tonight could be tidied up. It always was.

Crabbe and Goyle were waiting at the foot of the dormitory stairs in nightdresses that had clearly been bought with growth in mind, and they fell in behind her without being asked. The three of them had just reached the entrance hall, which was full of noise and candlelight and Hufflepuffs in nightcaps, when someone stepped out of the shadow beside the marble staircase and said, “Malfoy.”
