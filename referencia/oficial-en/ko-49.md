The moment Cedric's hand closed around the Triwizard Cup.

His body lurched upward. He grabbed the Cup in a panic, clinging to it as it spun him round and round, then shot him skyward—

—and set him down in front of the judges' table.

Cedric blinked at the panel in bewilderment. Dumbledore nodded with a benevolent smile. Karkaroff and Madame Maxime regarded him with expressions of profound disappointment. Barty Crouch Sr. wore the same rigid blankness he always did—whether that was simply his natural face or a deliberate performance of impartiality, it was impossible to say.

"The winner of the Triwizard Tournament is—!"

Ludo Bagman muttered the words with all the enthusiasm of a man delivering bad news.

"—Cedric Diggory! Please, congratulations. Would he come up to the podium."

With each word, the deflation and despair on his face grew more pronounced—the look of a man for whom Cedric's victory was deeply, personally unwelcome.

But no one was paying attention to Bagman's face. Everyone was shouting Cedric Diggory's name—Hogwarts' Champion, Hogwarts' winner—and the roar of it swelled across the grounds.

The Hogwarts students gave Cedric Diggory a thunderous ovation.

A winner had been crowned. Professor McGonagall moved through the crowd herself to collect Harry. She looked him over, and then—quite unlike herself—she offered him something approaching comfort.

"You gave everything you had. A fourth-year, taking down one of Hagrid's creatures alone and fighting through the bog trap—that alone is a remarkable achievement. Don't be disheartened. You are Gryffindor's pride."

Harry's conscience gave a small, uncomfortable twinge. He was too tired to speak, or at least he made a convincing show of it.

Following McGonagall to the judges' table, he found Viktor Krum and Fleur Delacour waiting. Krum had blood on his lip; Fleur was moving one arm with the careful stiffness of someone whose shoulder hadn't quite forgiven her yet.

"Our other Champions have arrived..."

Bagman shot Harry a brief, reproachful look. Harry thought of the large sum Bagman had wagered on his victory and smiled back pleasantly.

One of the minor benefits of surrendering the win. Harry could now be Bagman's saviour—and his sole creditor.

Bagman forced himself to finish.

"...Though they did not take the victory, let us applaud our Champions for competing with everything they had."

Another wave of enthusiastic applause broke over the grounds.

With that, Bagman left the judges' table. The other judges were occupied consoling their own schools' Champions. Karkaroff threw Dumbledore a look of profound displeasure and disappeared somewhere. Only Dumbledore remained, watching Harry with quiet, searching eyes—no comfort, no encouragement, just that steady gaze.

Then someone entirely unexpected stepped in front of Harry.

Barty Crouch Sr.

Head of the Department of International Magical Cooperation. A man who had never once sought private contact with any of the Champions—and yet here he was, extending his hand to Harry for the first time.

"An impressive performance, Potter. I commend your courage."

Harry shook his hand, puzzled.

"Thank you, Mr. Crouch."

Barty Crouch had been a Triwizard judge, but he was a stranger to Harry in every meaningful sense. He had never cultivated any personal relationship with the Champions—no private conversations, no appearances beyond the tasks themselves, arriving only to score and departing immediately after. His marks had been scrupulously fair, never generous to one and stingy to another.

Harry had found him strange at first, but it hadn't taken long to realise that Crouch was simply what a proper judge looked like.

It was Karkaroff and Madame Maxime—openly favouring their own students while sitting in judgment—who were the aberration. It was Bagman, quietly cheering for whichever Champion caught his fancy, who was the aberration.

When the normal man is surrounded by the abnormal, he's the one who looks out of place.

If a judge wished to show support for a particular Champion, the proper way was Crouch's way: wait until every task was finished, then approach. That was how it ought to be done.

"You entered first, which meant you encountered more traps than anyone else. The right to go first is both an opportunity for victory and an invitation to hardship—that was the intent when we wrote the rules. And yet."

Crouch examined Harry's injuries.

"Looking at you now, I cannot shake the feeling that the traps were too punishing on the Champions. Had you not been worn down fighting through them, you would have reached the Cup first. A pity. In future, we ought to design obstacles that test without leaving lasting damage."

"Well. That's just how it goes sometimes."

"When the luck required to win exceeds the skill required to win, the result feels hollow." Crouch paused. "In any case, well done. You are an exceptionally capable and gifted wizard. In you, I find... reason to hope..."

His voice trailed away.

He was said to be a ferocious workaholic—and looking at him now, Harry could believe it. Crouch looked exhausted, hollowed out. How tired did a man have to be to lose the thread of his own sentence mid-thought?

"Mr. Crouch?"

Crouch snapped his head up and said:

"In any case—that creature that pierced your leg and drew your blood. What is it? Extraordinarily powerful and vicious. I have never seen anything like it."

He gestured, without preamble, toward the Blast-Ended Skrewt.

Harry was caught off guard twice in quick succession: once by the abrupt change of subject, and once by the realisation that Hagrid's illegal hybrid had been noticed. He did his best to look innocent.

"I honestly couldn't say. Maybe someone brought an ancient creature out of some ruins somewhere?"

"I'll need to have it taken in for examination. It may be an illegally bred hybrid..."

"Wait. You're taking it—the Skrew— I mean, that thing? Why?"

Barty Crouch answered as though he'd prepared the response in advance.

"Potter. I work in the Department of International Magical Cooperation, and I am obligated to provide visiting foreign wizards with detailed information regarding all aspects of the Triwizard Tournament. They have expressed considerable interest in the obstacles set by the British Ministry of Magic, and I have been unable to give them satisfactory answers. At the very least, I owe them an identification of that creature. Is that sufficient reason?"

"Ah. I see."

Harry had heard things about Barty Crouch in passing. A man of rigid principle. A former Ministry prosecutor who had pursued Dark wizards with relentless severity. A workaholic of near-legendary dedication.

The fact that he'd kept his distance from the Champions, skipped the Yule Ball, and was now curious about the Skrewt—all of it fit neatly into that portrait.

He was, without question, exactly the man people said he was.

"Though I'm not sure why you're asking me—I can't tell you much. You'd be better off speaking to Professor Dumbledore, or to Rubeus Hagrid, the Care of Magical Creatures professor."

"...Quite right. You're a Champion, not a member of the organising committee."

He murmured it as though something had just occurred to him. Then, abruptly, he went still—staring at Harry with his mouth slightly open, as if he'd only just noticed where he was.

"...Why did I come to you...?"

Barty Crouch's eyes went briefly unfocused. For just a moment—a fraction of a second—Harry saw something in those clouded pupils: fear, and shock.

Then Harry blinked, and it was gone. Only the rigid, composed face of Barty Crouch remained.

"Are you all right?"

"Ah—yes, thank you, Potter. I've been working rather too hard of late. I think I need to return to the house."
That much was obvious. Harry was almost more puzzled by the fact that Crouch had stopped to speak with him at all, rather than going straight home.

Crouch turned and called out:

"Weatherby! Come here!"

What a name, Harry thought, and glanced over. Percy Weasley came hurrying across the grounds. Apparently Barty Crouch had the wrong surname entirely—or perhaps, with so many Weasleys in existence, he'd simply given up trying to keep track.

Percy adjusted his glasses.

"You called, Mr. Crouch!"

"Yes. Contact the Department for the Regulation and Control of Magical Creatures. I want that creature examined. Subdue it carefully—don't injure it—and bring it to my house. Cross-reference it against foreign magical creature registries, and if there's no match, investigate its origins and assign it a provisional danger classification."

It was unfortunate for Hagrid, but it seemed his pet was about to come under Ministry scrutiny. Harry felt a genuine pang of sympathy for the grief Hagrid would feel—and simultaneously, a sincere hope that the Ministry would lock the creature away from the world permanently.

Fighting something that tried to tear into you with pincers and drink your blood was one time too many. Not that he hadn't had similar experiences before.

"Well. Good evening, Potter."

Crouch said it quietly and walked away. Harry watched him go with a vague, nagging sense of wrongness, then turned and made his way toward the Gryffindor stands.

"You did brilliantly, Harry!"

Hermione was the first to reach him.

"You went around the same spot three times! If it hadn't been for that, you'd have won—I can't believe it! You, with your memory, walking past the same place and not even noticing!"

It was difficult to tell whether she was consoling him or scolding him.

Harry was still searching for an excuse when Ron answered for him.

"Can't be helped. Anyone who came face to face with a Blast-Ended Skrewt would lose track of where they were."

"But if he'd turned left there, he'd have avoided four more traps! Cedric even bypassed two of the obstacles Harry had already cleared! You practically handed Cedric his victory!"

"Cedric was always going to win. The universe wanted it that way."

Harry couldn't bring himself to admit he'd lost on purpose. He kept his silence, and the others charitably assumed it was exhaustion.

Ginny Weasley pushed through Ron and Hermione to get to him.

"Hold on—both of you—Harry needs to go to the hospital wing first! Look at his leg, he's bleeding!"

"Oh, he is!"

Hermione and Ginny fussed over him and steered him toward the hospital wing. Harry let himself be led, and somewhere along the way he glanced back.

Carina was nowhere to be seen.

It wasn't that he'd missed her. Harry always found Carina wherever she was—she was impossible to overlook, and every instinct he had seemed calibrated to locate her. If she wasn't there, it meant she'd left long ago.

Harry turned away, as if shaking something loose.

He still loved her. And the only way he could go on loving her—the only way any of this could end the way he needed it to—

Was to kill Voldemort and put every last Death Eater in Azkaban.

That was all.

Harry received a great many visitors in the hospital wing. The Weasley family descended on him and collectively worried enough for a lifetime. The Gryffindors came through in ones and twos, each wanting a moment with him.

Cedric Diggory arrived with his parents. Amos Diggory told Harry it was a shame, then spent most of the visit subtly emphasising his son's triumph. Harry briefly regretted not simply winning.

A fair number of Hufflepuffs came as well. Professor McGonagall appeared—a rare occurrence—bearing a box of biscuits. Everyone who celebrated Cedric's victory seemed equally sorry about Harry's loss.

Even a few younger Slytherins made the trip. One small girl crept in, left her get-well gift on the pile with a nervous twitch, and fled before anyone could speak to her.

By the time the light outside had faded to dusk, the ward was empty except for Harry. Dumbledore and Sirius arrived together.

"Why did you lose on purpose, Harry?"

Dumbledore asked it with eyes bright with curiosity. Sirius, who had been commiserating over the defeat, startled at the question.

"Is that true, Harry? You threw it to Diggory deliberately?"

Harry nodded.

"I don't need gold or glory. If I can trade those for something I actually want, that's a good deal."

"And what is it you want?"

"People who owe me something. People who'll listen when I speak and act on what I say."

Cedric Diggory and Ludo Bagman. Two debts, collected in a single evening. One emotional, one financial.

Harry had lent Bagman money on the condition that the Weasley twins were repaid first—a sum just short of what Bagman needed to clear his debts entirely. From now on, Bagman would be paying very close attention to whatever Harry said.

Dumbledore spoke quietly, with a note of gentle reproach.

"Every contest has its own meaning, Harry. Cedric Diggory fought with everything he had to win. You didn't meet him at full strength. That victory could have been something truly precious in his life—and you've diminished it by letting him have it."

"He still won. He can afford to lose a little of the shine. What he wanted was the victory, and he got it."

Harry said it without particular concern.

"You know what Cedric is. He's ambitious. He might not have been when he was Sorted into Hufflepuff, but living among those students shaped him. Hufflepuff—the house everyone dismisses and looks down on. He wanted to prove they could win. He wanted to give them something to believe in."

"And your deliberate defeat is that belief?"

"Cedric earned it. If the tournament had been... normal... he would have won anyway. He deserved it."

It wasn't wrong, exactly. Harry's scheme had bent the rules of fair play without quite breaking them. Still, Dumbledore exhaled a quiet, sorrowful breath.

"I worry that you've looked so far ahead that you've stopped living in the present. Perhaps I should have delayed telling you everything—about Voldemort, about the prophecy. I never wanted your only years at school to become preparation for war."

"No. I was living in the present. Until two weeks ago."

Sirius knew what had happened two weeks ago. That was the day Carina Malfoy had ended things with Harry. Since then, the easy smiles and the casual mentions of his girlfriend had simply stopped—there was no way Sirius could have missed it.

"I lost the present because of a future that was coming regardless. So I started thinking about the future beyond that. Professor Moody would be pleased—it's exactly what he warned me about."

"...I suppose so. I'm hardly in a position to criticise, given that I couldn't stop Alastor."

"But I agree with him on one thing. Until Voldemort is dead, I won't have room to breathe."

Harry said it with cold, level eyes.

"He's coming back soon. Even though the protection in my blood still holds."

"Sooner than expected—but he was never simply waiting. Even without a body, he's been preparing. Slowly, carefully. As we have."
"Which means he's not stupid, unfortunately. But..." Harry glanced at Sirius and allowed himself a small smile. "We're still ahead of him, aren't we?"

Sirius grinned with undisguised satisfaction and pulled something from his pocket.

It was Hufflepuff's Cup. Where there had once been gleaming gold, there were now ugly black gashes, carved deep into the metal.

Once a symbol of glory. Once a vessel for Voldemort's soul. Now it was only the ruin of what it had been.

"That's right. While you were in the maze, we destroyed the Horcrux. There's only one left—something of Ravenclaw's!"

"Ravenclaw's Diadem."

Harry corrected him.

"You've already identified it?"

"Yes. Ravenclaw's own descendant told me directly, so it's reliable."

"Her descendant? You mean the Grey Lady—Ravenclaw's ghost?"

"You know her?"

"Of course I do. The ghosts have lived in Hogwarts longer than anyone, and they know extraordinary secrets—and they talk. Drop a few sympathetic words in the right ear and they'll tell you everything. We were the ghosts' best friends. Three of the secret passages on the Marauder's Map came from information the ghosts gave us."

Sirius said it with evident pride. Harry made a mental note to seek out the ghosts himself one day. Moaning Myrtle had rather poisoned his impression of the castle's spectral residents.

"The Grey Lady was always the easiest to talk to. Did she tell you?"

"Very clearly. She said Voldemort came to her and asked where the Diadem was hidden."

"Then everything is falling into place!"

Harry agreed. Things were moving well. It might take time, but the path was clear: find the Diadem, destroy it, then kill a Voldemort stripped of his Horcruxes.

As long as the protection in Harry's blood held, Voldemort couldn't touch him. A single brush of Harry's skin would be enough to shatter whatever body he'd managed to cobble together...

Sirius turned to Dumbledore.

"All right, Dumbledore. It's time to be honest."

"About what?"

"Who put Harry's name in the Goblet of Fire. It was you, wasn't it? You wanted to give Harry a chance to compete. Who else could it have been?"

Sirius seemed fairly confident in his deduction. Dumbledore denied it without hesitation.

"I do not bend the rules, Sirius. If I had put Harry's name in the Goblet, I would have ensured he won. Cheating in that fashion is not my way."

Sirius scratched the back of his head, looking sheepish.

"Then who did it? Harry didn't win—well, he chose not to, but he came within reach of winning. He wasn't seriously hurt either."

"I don't know. Alastor has been investigating tirelessly and has found nothing. The intent is impossible to read. It's possible the whole thing was simply meant to divide our attention."

"That would make sense. It kept you and Moody tied to Hogwarts."

"If so, the plan only half succeeded. Lupin and Tonks held things together admirably."

While Sirius and Dumbledore talked, Harry's eye drifted across the pile of get-well gifts on the bedside table—and snagged on something unfamiliar.

A small, neatly wrapped package: petal-shaped chocolate biscuits and sunflower-shaped sweets. An ordinary enough get-well offering. Except that hanging from the bottom of the wrapping was a ring Harry knew very well.

He reached out and took it. The familiar weight, the faint warmth—it was Carina's ring. The one that had been a pair with his.

She'd returned it.

The meaning was unmistakable. Harry's expression went still.

At the base of the ring, a small note had been folded tight. Harry opened it. Fine paper, neat and elegant handwriting.

[Congratulations on second place, Potter. Not a victory—but you got what you wanted, so I imagine you're satisfied.]

The faint scent of it stirred something that felt uncomfortably like longing. But the letter's message was a clean, merciless farewell.

[This is where we end. The time we had wasn't entirely dull. It simply wasn't meant to go further than this.]

Whatever small warmth had been in him settled and went cold. Harry stared at the letter. Staring didn't change the words.

On an impulse he couldn't quite name, he pressed his wand to the paper—half-hoping, half-praying that the words might shift into something else.

That if she'd written I miss you somewhere, he'd find it. That it wasn't too late. He pressed the wand against the parchment with something close to desperation.

And then—remarkably—letters began to surface at the bottom of the page. Hidden text, rising to meet him. Harry dropped the wand and read as the words appeared.

[Now that it's all over, I'll tell you. The person who put your name in the Goblet of Fire was—]

Something he had never expected. Something he would never have thought to find in a letter from Carina.

Harry crushed the note in his fist and turned.

"Barty Crouch."

Dumbledore and Sirius looked at him.

"It was him. Barty Crouch. He tampered with the Goblet of Fire and put my name in it."

Of course. Barty Crouch had been the only one who was normal. He had behaved exactly as everyone expected him to behave.

Rigid. Minimising private contact. Absorbed in his work, never drawing attention to himself.

And in those final moments, when he'd stood before Harry, he had shown every sign of a man under the Imperius Curse—without any attempt to hide it. The glazed eyes. The occasional bewilderment at his own actions. The fear. The shock.

"Him? But why would he—"

"He was under the Imperius Curse. That's why he avoided any private gatherings entirely. If he'd spent time around people who knew him well—Professor Dumbledore, Mad-Eye Moody—they might have noticed. And he was the one person no one questioned for skipping the Yule Ball!"

Dumbledore drew a sharp breath of realisation.

It had been Crouch who brought the Goblet of Fire from the Ministry. Crouch who had insisted the Goblet's decision was binding. He'd had every opportunity to interfere, at every stage.

He was simply too senior, and too impeccably correct in his conduct, for suspicion to land on him. And yet Barty Crouch Sr. had been the person with the greatest responsibility—and the greatest access—of anyone involved in the Triwizard Tournament.

"And his purpose..."

If he had truly been under the Imperius Curse, then whatever he had done last—whatever he had moved toward at the very end—was the real objective.

Harry's jaw tightened.

"To get my blood. The blood the Skrewt drew with its sucker!"
