# Chapter 15: Show Your Working

Lyra caught a cold in Hogsmeade, and by Monday morning the whole of Slytherin House had been told so.

She had told them herself on Saturday evening, in the doorway of the common room, with the rain still running out of her hair and her eyes, as she was perfectly aware, pink to the rims. She had gone out without a hat, she said; she thought she was coming down with something. Then she drew the hangings round her bed and stayed behind them all Sunday, and coughed whenever she heard somebody on the stairs, so that the cold would have something to stand on.

It was a good cold, and by Monday she very nearly believed in it herself. She came down to breakfast with her voice pitched a little low and sat at the far end of the Slytherin table, where she could see the doors and the staff table and none of Gryffindor at all.

That was the arrangement. It had come to her on Sunday, somewhere in the long grey middle of the afternoon, that she knew where Potter sat in every room in the castle: a third of the way down the Gryffindor table with his back to the wall; the second bench from the front in Charms; on her right in Potions, whenever he could get away with it. She had never once set out to learn any of it. So she would sit where she could not see him, and walk where he did not walk, and in a week or two the knowledge would go off like milk.

Crabbe sat down opposite her and put a small stoppered bottle beside her plate.

“Pepperup,” she said. “I went up to Madam Pomfrey last night. She says it’s very good for colds.” She reached for the toast. “If it’s a cold.”

Lyra looked at the bottle. Everybody knew what Pepperup Potion did. It cured a cold in a quarter of an hour and left whoever had taken it smoking at the ears for the rest of the morning, and nobody over the age of eight took it unless they were made to. A little way down the table, Panther Parkinson had stopped chewing to watch.

There was no way out of it that did not go through an admission. Lyra unstoppered the bottle and drank the whole of it, without taking her eyes off Crabbe.

“Thank you, Vanessa,” she said. “Much better.”

Crabbe nodded and buttered her toast, and did not say another word, and that was worse than anything she could have said.

The smoke began almost at once, out of both ears at a gentle, determined rate, and hung about her head in the cold air of the Hall.

“Merlin, Malfoy,” said Parkinson. “You look like the Hogwarts Express.”

“And you look like the back end of it,” said Lyra, “but I was brought up too well to say so.”

Goyle, beside her, observed that you could probably boil an egg on her, and Lyra laughed. It was a loud, high, delighted laugh, much too big for the joke, and two Ravenclaws at the next table turned round. Lyra stopped as suddenly as she had started and ate a piece of toast she did not want. She had been doing it since Saturday, going from nothing to far too much and back again without any warning, like a broom with a warp in its tail, and she could not seem to find the place in between where she usually lived.

She left breakfast early, trailing smoke, and went up to Arithmancy by the back stairs, because the back stairs were the long way round and Potter had never taken the long way round to anything in his life.

He was on the fourth floor, in a window bay halfway along the corridor, with Granger.

They had their heads together. Granger had a book clutched against her chest and was talking fast and low, and Lyra had come round the corner close enough to hear the end of it before either of them saw her.

“—isn’t a toy, Harry, it’s—”

Granger stopped. She had seen Lyra over Potter’s shoulder, and her mouth shut on whatever came next and stayed shut.

Potter had his back to the corridor, and he did not turn round. He must have known who it was: Granger’s face would have told him, or the silence, or for that matter the smoke. He stayed exactly as he was, hands in his pockets, looking out of the window at the rain, as though there were nothing behind him but a stretch of stone floor that anybody at all might walk along.

So Lyra walked along it, at an ordinary pace, with her ears steaming, and Granger watched her the whole way and at the very last, for less than a second, looked at the smoke.

So that was where it all went, everything he would not tell her. Granger, in a window bay, on the inside of something, with her book and her low quick voice. Whatever was not a toy, Granger had been shown it. Granger had very likely known about the dog since September.

The word came up in her as it always had, smooth and ready to hand, and for once there was a great deal of comfort in it. A Mudblood, standing exactly where Lyra had been told there was no room.

She went round the next corner and stopped with her back to the wall, and listened. Behind her the corridor stayed quiet; they did not start again. She stood there long enough to be ashamed of herself, and then a little longer. Then there were footsteps going away in the other direction, two sets, and she went on up to Arithmancy, took her seat by the window, and laid out her quill and ink and her copy of *Numerology and Grammatica* in a perfectly straight line, and smoked gently at the ears.

Granger came in on the bell, rather out of breath, and sat in the front row where she always sat, and did not look round.

Professor Vector did not believe in end-of-term treats, and said so every December, and every December she gave one anyway.

“Parlour Arithmancy,” she announced, writing it across the blackboard in large, unhurried capitals. “The numbers of names. Page three hundred and two, under *Methods, Discredited*. The method is crude, the results are meaningless, and you will all enjoy yourselves enormously. One value for each letter, one to nine and round again; add them up; reduce any total of two digits by adding the digits, unless you arrive at eleven or twenty-two, which the old arithmancers left alone for reasons they never explained. Every step shown. You will work in pairs and check each other’s working. Mr Boot, Mr Goldstein—”

She went down the rows. Lyra, at the window, was not listening particularly, until she heard her own name at the end of the list, attached to another.

“—Miss Granger and Miss Malfoy. Neither of you ever needs checking, so you may check each other. Miss Malfoy, you may move to the front. Mind the smoke.”

Lyra gathered her things and went down to the front row, and sat at the far end of Granger’s desk, as far along as the bench allowed. Granger moved her inkpot two inches to the left. It might have been an accommodation, or a border.

They had never sat at the same desk before. Since September they had divided the top of every Arithmancy list between them, a mark up one week and a mark down the next, without exchanging a word that was not about the homework, and very few of those. Lyra knew the back of Granger’s head a great deal better than she knew her face.

They wrote each other’s names at the top of their parchments without looking up.

Lyra did Granger first, to get it over with. HERMIONE came to fifty-one and GRANGER to forty-three: ninety-four, thirteen, four. She turned to the table at the back of the chapter and ran her finger down it, and found that she was enjoying herself for the first time in two days.

“Four,” she said. “The square. Order, labour, endurance.” She read on with pleasure. “‘The number of those who carry the load.’ Well. There we are.”

“It says *builder*,” said Granger, without looking up.

“A little further down. After the load.”

Granger finished her own sum, checked it once with her lips moving, and looked at the answer for a second longer than she needed to.

“Eleven,” she said.

“Naturally.”

“It doesn’t reduce.”

“I heard her the first time, thank you.”

Granger turned to the table in her own book and read it out in a carefully neutral voice. “‘Eleven. The number that will not be reduced. Insight, pride, an exacting standard.’” She paused. “‘Given to nerves.’”

A small fresh puff of smoke came out of Lyra’s left ear. Granger’s eyes followed it up towards the ceiling and came down again, and she said nothing whatsoever, very pointedly.

“The method is meaningless,” said Lyra. “Vector said so. It needs a control.”

“A control.”

“Somebody we already know.” She pulled a scrap of spare parchment towards her and wrote PANTHER PARKINSON across the top of it, and the two of them bent over it together. Thirty-seven. Forty-five. Eighty-two. Ten. One.

Granger found it in the table. “One,” she read. “‘The leader. The first. The one who goes before.’”

They looked at the parchment. Lyra thought of Parkinson, who in their first year had been sick on the Hogwarts Express before it had even left the platform, going before. Beside her, Granger made a small sound in her nose and stopped it at once; and Lyra, who had not laughed at anything properly since Saturday, felt something come up in her throat that was nothing like the noise she had made at breakfast, and only just got her hand over her mouth in time.

It lasted perhaps three seconds. Then Granger remembered who she was sitting next to, and so did Lyra, and they both looked at the blackboard with great attention.

“That proves it’s nonsense,” said Lyra.

“Yes,” said Granger. “Swap.”

They swapped. Lyra went through Granger’s sum for her own name, found nothing wrong with it, and resented that slightly. Granger went through Lyra’s as though it were evidence at a trial, a line at a time, checking each letter against the chart although she obviously knew them all by heart. It was going to take her a while.

Lyra had nothing to do. The scrap of parchment with Parkinson on it lay by her hand, with most of a clean side left.

She dipped her quill and wrote on the back of it, quite small, a name she had no need to look up the spelling of. Another control, she told herself. You could not establish a method on a single case, and a boy who broke every rule in the castle ought to come out as something to do with breaking rules. If he did, the method had that much in its favour.

HARRY: eight, one; seven, seven, seven. POTTER: seven, six, two, two, five, seven.

Her quill went quickly. Thirty. Twenty-nine. Fifty-nine. Fourteen. Five.

She found Five in the table. *Change. Liberty. The one who will not keep to the path; the breaker of rules.* It suited him exactly, so the method was sound. Lyra sat back with the satisfaction of a sum that had come out, and looked from the five to Granger’s parchment, where LYRA MALFOY stood in Granger’s small, neat hand with an eleven beside it. They had nothing whatever in common, which was entirely as it ought to be. She pressed the nib down on the five until it went through to the wood.

“Your R’s are sevens,” said Granger.

She had finished, and her eye had fallen on the scrap, and she was looking at it with the face of somebody who has spotted a spelling mistake on a public notice and cannot, in all conscience, walk past it.

Lyra put her hand over it. She was a little too late, and they both knew it.

“R is nine,” said Granger. “You did it right on mine.” She could no more have stopped herself than stopped breathing. “It’s the eighteenth letter. One and eight.” Before Lyra could do anything, she had leaned across with her own quill and written a neat small nine over each of the three R’s, and was adding under her breath, quick and perfectly sure of herself: “Thirty-four. Thirty-one. Sixty-five—”

She stopped.

Six and five. Neither of them needed to do it. They sat side by side at the front of the classroom and looked at the eleven beside Lyra’s name on one parchment and the eleven on the scrap, both in Granger’s hand, while the smoke from Lyra’s ears went on rising very gently between them.

“Another control?” said Granger.

“Obviously.”

“On somebody random.”

“Entirely random.”

Granger looked at her a moment longer, and visibly decided against every one of the things she might have said.

“Right,” she said.

“Time,” said Professor Vector from the back of the room. “Parchments to me, please. Names and working.”

Lyra closed her fist on the scrap. On her way back from Vector’s desk she passed the wastepaper basket and did not put it in.

Professor Vector wished them a happy Christmas and went out with the parchments, and the classroom emptied as classrooms do in the last week of term, noisily and in a hurry. Lyra packed her bag one thing at a time. At the front, Granger did the same, more slowly, because she had a great deal more of it.

Then it was the two of them, and the rain on the windows.

“Well?” said Lyra.

Granger looked up. “Well what?”

“What do you want for it?”

“For what?”

“Don’t be stupid, Granger. You aren’t, whatever else you are.” Lyra came down between the desks and stopped at a carefully judged distance. She had watched her father do this across a hundred dinner tables: you found out the price and you paid it, and then the thing could not be held over you. It was owing that was dangerous. “Either you tell him, or you don’t and you want something for it. I’d rather know which now.”

“I’m not going to tell him,” said Granger.

“Of course you are. You tell him everything.” It came out harder than Lyra had meant, and a little too fast. “Window bays and all.”

Granger did not deny it. She looked at Lyra for a second, and then went back to fitting books into her bag.

“What I tell Harry is my business,” she said. “This isn’t mine. So I won’t.”

“And what do you want for that?”

“Nothing.”

“There is always something.”

“Then you’ll have to go on looking for it.” Granger forced a last book in, and the seam of her bag gave an ominous creak. “I’m not doing it for you, Malfoy, if that helps. I just don’t give away things that belong to other people.”

*I didn’t do it for you.* Lyra had said that herself, in the library in October, with Potter at the table listening. Granger had evidently kept it.

The word was right there, as it always was: smooth and ready to hand, the one sure thing to throw when nothing else would do. There was nobody to be careful of. Potter was four floors away, or forty, and on Saturday he had made it perfectly clear that nothing she did was any concern of his. Granger stood alone at the front of the classroom with her shoulders set, as if she knew exactly what was coming and had decided in advance not to flinch.

Lyra opened her mouth.

“Granger,” she said.

It was not the word she had reached for. The other one had simply failed to arrive, the way it had stopped arriving in October whenever Potter might be in earshot; only Potter was nowhere, and it had not come anyway, and there was nobody in the room to have stopped it except Granger, who had not said a word.

Granger was waiting.

“Your bag’s going to split,” said Lyra.

It split. The seam went all at once with a sound like a tablecloth tearing, and the whole of Granger’s education went out across the flagstones: *Numerology and Grammatica* and *Unfogging the Future* and *The Standard Book of Spells, Grade 3*, and a fat brown volume called *Home Life and Social Habits of British Muggles*, and a great many quills, and a sheet of parchment that skated over the floor and came to rest against Lyra’s shoe.

Lyra picked it up, since it was there.

It was a timetable, the same grid they had all been handed in September; but where Lyra’s had one subject to a square, Granger’s had been written over and over until some of the squares were nearly black. Monday, nine o’clock: *Arithmancy*. And beneath it, in the same square: *Muggle Studies*.

Lyra looked at it. She had sat in Arithmancy that morning from nine o’clock until the bell, and so had Granger, in the front row. Granger had not been anywhere else. She could not have been.

She raised her eyes. Granger was on her knees among the books, looking at the timetable in Lyra’s hand, and her face had gone careful. It was a good careful face. Lyra had been wearing one very like it since Saturday, and knew the work it took.

She could feel a great many things lining up to be said, each one useful. Knowledge was power, her father liked to say, and here was a piece of it, still warm from Granger’s bag, and Lyra had had nothing to buy with all week.

She held it out.

“I didn’t see anything,” she said.

Granger took it. “There’s nothing to see.”

“Then I didn’t see it twice.”

Granger knelt there with the timetable pressed flat against her chest, and Lyra watched her understand it.

“I didn’t ask you for that,” Granger said slowly.

“I know. That’s what makes it square.”

Granger pointed her wand at the bag and said, “*Reparo*,” in a voice that was not quite steady, and the seam drew itself shut, neater than it had been to begin with. It was a very good Reparo. Lyra noted it, against her will, along with everything else.

“You’re very strange, Malfoy,” said Granger, getting up.

“I’m a Malfoy.”

“Yes,” said Granger. “I know.” She hitched the bag on to her shoulder and considered Lyra as though she were a sum that had not come out the way the book said it would. Then she went to the door, and there she stopped. “For what it’s worth,” she said, without turning round, “I wasn’t counting.”

“Somebody has to,” said Lyra, but the door had already shut.

She stood in the empty classroom with the last of the Pepperup thinning out above her ears, and after a while she sat down and smoothed the scrap of parchment flat.

HARRY POTTER, in her own hand. Three neat nines, in somebody else’s. Sixty-five; eleven.

Lyra did not believe in Parlour Arithmancy. Vector said it was meaningless and Granger said it was nonsense, and she agreed with both of them. But she had never in her life let anybody else’s figures go unchecked. So underneath his name, in the smallest letters she could make, she wrote her own and worked it through, slowly, with every R a nine.

Forty-seven. Eleven.

Granger had been right. Lyra sat and looked at the two of them for some time, one above the other, as alike as two numbers could be.

Then she opened *Numerology and Grammatica* at the back, where the tables of values were, folded the scrap very small and slid it in behind them, next to a strip of paper in her mother’s hand that said *You may tell me anything*.

Potter did not come near her all week.

She noticed it as one notices a clock that has stopped. Since the first of September there had been exactly one week in which he failed to turn up somewhere he had no business being, and that had been her own fault, in October, in the library. This was the second. In Potions on Thursday he sat with Weasley on the Gryffindor side of the dungeon and was so quiet that Snape looked at him twice, and at Lyra once, for rather longer. Lyra did not look at Potter at all. It took a good deal more out of her than looking would have.

By Saturday her cold had been declared cured, and she stood in the Entrance Hall in her travelling cloak, with her trunk gone ahead to the station, and waited for the carriages to come round.

Goyle sniffed.

“You’ve got a sugar quill,” she said. “I can smell it.”

“No, I haven’t.”

Goyle looked at Lyra’s left-hand pocket with deep and simple longing, and Lyra kept her hand on it. The quill had been in that pocket, or the one before, for seven weeks. She could not have said what she was keeping it for. Nobody else was having it in the meantime.

Parkinson came and stood beside them, rocking on his heels, which meant he had news and was waiting to be asked for it. Nobody asked. He told them anyway.

“Potter’s staying,” he said. “I saw McGonagall’s list. Him and Weasley.” He left a pause for effect and looked sideways at Lyra. “And Granger.”

“How thrilling,” said Lyra. “You should have it set to music.”

“I only thought you’d want to know.”

She was spared an answer by Granger herself, who came out of the Great Hall with an armful of books, bound for the library on the first morning of the holidays, and passed within a yard of them on her way to the stairs. Parkinson nudged Lyra, quite unnecessarily.

Granger did not stop. But she looked at Lyra as she went by, and went on looking for a good deal longer than it took to recognise her. It was not a friendly look, and it was not an unfriendly one. Her eyes went to Parkinson, standing there with his mouth half open and his news going stale in it, and came back to Lyra; and Lyra understood that Parkinson knew nothing, and that Granger wanted her to see as much.

Lyra inclined her head, very slightly, which committed her to nothing.

Granger went on to the foot of the marble staircase. And Lyra, who had kept the arrangement for five days, through fifteen meals and a double Potions, broke it in the Entrance Hall, in front of Parkinson.

Potter was on the first landing, leaning on the banister, waiting. Granger went up to him and said something, and he took half her books off her without being asked, and the two of them went on up the stairs together and round the turn and out of sight. He did not look down into the Hall, not once, not even at the turn.

“Well,” said Parkinson, with tremendous satisfaction, “that’s—”

“Carriages,” said Crabbe, who had not said a word all morning, and took Lyra by the elbow and walked her out through the front doors.

The carriages stood in a line in the rain, drawn by nothing anybody could see. Lyra climbed into the first and sat by the window, and Goyle climbed in after her, still faintly hopeful, and then Crabbe. She was going home: to her brother, who had not written in eight weeks, and the man in the east cottage, and a family that Potter, a week ago, had shown her he could break.

Behind her the castle would stand nearly empty for the whole of the holidays, with Potter in it, and Granger, and Weasley, who did not count, and fifteen days to go before the train came back. Lyra leaned her head against the cold glass and did not think about it at all, and by the time the carriage reached the station she had worked out, without in the least meaning to, that it came to three hundred and sixty hours.
