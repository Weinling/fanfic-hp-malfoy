# Chapter 51: Four Hours

Hermione locked the door, put a chair against it, and took eleven sandwiches out of her bag.

“Sit down,” she said.

It was a quarter to ten on Saturday morning, in a classroom at the top of the west side of the castle that nobody had used in years. There were nineteen desks in it, half a diagram on the blackboard, and a window that looked down on the Quidditch pitch. She had found him on the stairs and asked him to help her carry something. He had not slept. He had come along without thinking about it, which he was beginning to suspect had been the idea.

“What needs carrying?” said Harry.

“Nothing. Sit down.”

He looked at the door. She was putting her wand away. She had gone rather pink, and she had the look she got on the last page of a very long book, when it turned out that she had been right.

“I’ve got to—”

“You haven’t. Angelina’s got Ron down there until lunch, and Ginny’s throwing for him. I’ve checked. I’ve got nothing until the rota at two.” She sat down at a desk, and took out a notebook, and squared it to the edge with two fingers. It was a fat red one, with an elastic band round it and coloured tabs down the side. He had seen it about for years. He had thought that it was Arithmancy. “What happened on the sixth floor?”

“Nothing.”

“Right,” said Hermione.

He had said that word to somebody, twice, this year. He had not known what a horrible word it was.

She took off the elastic band and opened the notebook at the first tab.

“January, first year,” she said. “I can’t give you the date. I didn’t start writing things down until March, so the beginning’s from memory. You stopped laughing at Ron’s jokes.”

Harry looked at her.

“That’s what I put, and it’s wrong, I’ve crossed it out. You went on laughing. You started laughing *early*. About half a second, before he’d got to the end.” She ran a finger down the page. “I thought that you’d got cleverer. That’s what I thought, I was eleven. Then you stopped asking what things were. You used to ask me everything. You stopped getting lost. You stopped treading on the trick stair on the way to Charms, from about February, you just stepped over it, while you were talking. Ron trod on it until third year. Ron and I spent three weeks in the library looking up Nicolas Flamel, and you came along and carried the books.” She turned a tab. “Second year, you told me never to go to the library on my own, and you wouldn’t say why. I’ve got that one underlined. Third year, Professor Trelawney saw the Grim in your teacup and you went on with your biscuit. Fourth year—”

“You’ve been writing this down for four years?”

“Somebody had to.”

It went up the back of his neck before he had had any chance to look at it. He had slept perhaps three hours in two nights. He had held his right hand under the tap in the prefects’ bathroom that Ron had lent him the password for, until it stopped feeling like anything, and it had started again. He knew exactly what he was going to say. It was the one thing he had always been able to do, in either life: he knew where everybody kept things.

“Haven’t you got anything better to do?” said Harry. “You’ve got a badge. You’ve got a rota. Go and sit on the stairs with Ron and hold his list. You don’t want to know what’s the matter with me, Hermione, you never have, you just want to be *right*. Four years you’ve been marking me, like an essay. Does he know you do it? Or is it just something you do with people who haven’t got anybody?”

It crossed the room, and it hit her where he had sent it. Her chin moved, once. She looked down at the notebook, and her hands were not quite steady, and she took hold of the next tab, a yellow one, and turned to it.

“August,” said Hermione. She had to stop and start again. “I did the washing at Grimmauld Place, on the Friday, because Mrs Weasley had the drawing room to do. You always leave things in your pockets.”

There was an envelope stuck in at the yellow tab. She took it out, and laid what was in it on the desk between them, one, two, three, and squared them up. They had been through the wash. They had gone soft and furry at the edges, and the print had faded to the colour of old dishwater, but you could read it. *Odeon. Holloway Rd.* And the Thursday. Two of them had been torn across at the door. The third had not.

Harry looked at them.

“You bought three,” said Hermione. “You stood on the stairs and told Ron to get the Cleansweep. You said *well done*, you’d practised it, I could see you’d practised it, and you went and stood by the stove with your toast. Then you went with Sirius, and I thought that was nice. And you’d bought *three*, Harry. You never said anything.” Her eyes had filled. She was looking straight at him, and it was running down beside her nose, and she did not do anything about it at all. “I’ve been trying to work out how to say sorry since August. I can’t. I’ve tried it a lot of ways. So I’ve brought sandwiches.”

There was a very long silence. Far down below the window somebody blew a whistle.

He did not know what was happening to him. In seventeen years and four, nobody had ever kept anything of his. People had kept him: that was quite different. He had been kept in a cupboard and kept safe. He had been kept alive for a purpose by a succession of people who were very fond of him. He looked at three bits of soft grey paper, on a desk, squared to the edge. He had thought that they had gone in a fire.

He sat down.

“Which ones have got cheese in?” said Harry.

It took about an hour to get as far as the Chamber.

He told it no better than he had told it in the Owlery. She did not say that it was impossible. He had expected her to, and he had a number of proofs ready, and she never asked for one. She said “Go on,” and “When?”, and she wrote, very fast, in a small neat hand, and twice she stopped him and sent him back for a date. When he got to the corridor, and Quirrell, she put down her quill and looked at the first page of the notebook for a moment. Then she picked it up again.

“And Malfoy?”

“She wasn’t there. It was Draco.”

“*Draco?*”

“He was all right. I mean, he was foul. He called you— He was foul for seven years.” He looked at his sandwich. “I don’t know why she’s here. Nobody does.”

“How does it end?”

He had not meant to look out of the window. It faced the wrong way, in any case; you could see only the edge of the Forest from there, the first dark trees beyond the pitch.

“Can’t,” said Harry.

Hermione looked at him, and then at the window, and she made a small mark in the margin and did not say anything. He remembered, suddenly and completely, what it had been like to have her on your side.

She had stopped writing. She was rolling the quill between her finger and thumb.

“What happened to us?” she said. “To Ron and me. In the other one.”

He got up and went to the window, because it was easier. A long way down, a very small figure on a broom was hanging in front of the left-hand hoop, with its elbows out. A smaller one with red hair drew back her arm. The Quaffle went past the Keeper’s left ear and through the hoop, and even at that distance you could see him say something to himself.

“You got there in the end,” said Harry. “You took your time. It was the middle of a war. You’d both got your arms full of basilisk fangs, and he said something decent about the house-elves, and you dropped the lot.”

He heard nothing behind him at all. He looked round.

She had gone scarlet from the neck of her jumper to the roots of her hair. She had put her fingers over her mouth. She was looking past him, out of the window, at a boy who had gone to McGonagall with a graph, with an expression he had seen once before, over the answers at the back of a book: pure terror and pure joy, and a very strong desire not to have looked.

“Don’t tell me that,” said Hermione, through her fingers.

“You asked.”

“I know I asked! Does he— No. Don’t. He hasn’t got the—he thinks I’ve got a *cold*, Harry.” She took the fingers away and pressed both hands against her cheeks. Then she started to laugh, and it got away from her, and it went on for some time. He found that he was laughing as well. It hurt, rather; he had not been using those muscles. He got it almost right. He got it about half a second early.

She heard it. She stopped.

“Eat something,” said Hermione. “Then tell me about the Chamber.”

She did not raise her voice. He had thought that she might.

He told her all of it: Flourish and Blotts, the book in the cauldron, and that he had known, and had stood there with Mrs Weasley’s hand on his shoulder and let it happen. Why he had let it happen. He told it as he had told it in the Owlery. It did not sound any better.

Hermione had gone a long way away. She sat with her hands flat on the notebook, and the colour had drained out of her face and left it small and plain and very tired, and for the first time since he had known her in either life she looked as if she did not want to know the next thing.

“I was Petrified,” she said. “For a month. You knew that as well.”

“Yes.”

“You told me not to go to the library on my own.”

“Yes.”

“You came and sat by my bed. Ron said. You used to read me the Quidditch results, for a joke.” She nodded slowly, as if he had confirmed a date. “You knew I’d be all right.”

He did not say anything. There was nothing to say to it that was not *yes*.

“She didn’t speak for a fortnight, afterwards,” said Hermione. “Ginny. I sat with her. She was *eleven*.”

“I know she was.”

“Everyone keeps telling you that, I expect.”

“One other person.”

She got up, and went to the window, and stood where he had stood. Down on the pitch, the small figure with the red hair had flown in close to the hoops and was showing the Keeper something with her hands, and he was nodding.

She stood there for a long time. He did not say anything. It occurred to him that there was nothing at all he could do to stop her unlocking that door and going down there.

“You’re never to tell him,” said Hermione.

“I—”

“I mean it. Not when it’s over, not when you’re old. Not ever. It would finish him. He’d come after you, or he’d go to pieces, and I don’t know which is worse, and either way she’d have to know why.” She did not turn round. Her voice was perfectly clear. “If he asks me, I shall say I don’t know. I’m going to go on sitting next to him, and I’m going to look at him, knowing that, and I’ll lie. I want you to understand that I’m not doing it for you.”

“I know you’re not.”

“I want it written down.”

She came back, and sat, and wrote it down. It took two lines. She put a date beside it, and blotted it, and looked at it for a moment. Then she turned back through the notebook, past the yellow tab, a long way, until she was at the beginning again; and she sat frowning at the first page, with the place where she had crossed something out.

“There’s something that I don’t understand,” said Hermione.

“Only one?”

“Who was he?”

“Who was who?”

“Before.” She put her finger on the page. “You said January. In the corridor. You came back, or it came back, or however it was. But you’d been here since September. I met you on the train. You’d got Spellotape on your glasses, and you’d no idea what anything was. You had a whole term.” She looked up. “That wasn’t you, was it? Not all this. He hadn’t got any of it. He was just a boy from Surrey. So who was he?”

He opened his mouth.

He could remember the train. He knew what had happened on it, as he knew the date of a battle. He knew what they had eaten. He looked for the boy who had eaten it, and he went on looking, for a moment longer than he had ever let himself before, and it was like putting your foot out in the dark for a step.

“I don’t know,” said Harry.

“But you remember him.”

“I remember it. It’s like— It’s like something I read.”

“Well, where did he go?”

“He didn’t go anywhere. I’m here. I’m—” He stopped. “I don’t know.”

Hermione looked at the page. She was not really talking to him. She was going back through it, as she would go back through anything, to see where the mistake had got in; and she was smiling, very slightly, at something she had found there.

“I liked him,” she said. “He was so pleased about everything. He thought that Chocolate Frogs were the best thing he’d ever seen. He used to ask me what words meant, and he’d say them over to himself. I’d only got him for four months, I suppose, and I was horrible to him for two of those.” She glanced up. “I don’t think he’d ever had—”

She saw his face. She stopped, and shut her mouth, and very gently she closed the notebook on her finger.

“I’m sorry,” said Hermione. “That wasn’t— I’m sure it’s not like that.”

“No,” said Harry.

He picked up a sandwich, and looked at it, and put it down.

It was gone one by the sun on the desks. Nobody had come. The Keeper had been called in, and the pitch lay empty, with a thin cold wind going over it. They had got through six of the sandwiches. Between them, on the desk, the three grey tickets lay where she had put them.

“What happened on the sixth floor?” said Hermione.

He had thought that she had forgotten. He ought to have known better. He looked at his right hand, which was lying on the desk, and it looked just the same.

“I saw the jar,” she said. “Everybody saw the jar, they just didn’t know what it was. I saw you come in last night. Ron says you stood in the bathroom for an hour; he thinks you’re sickening for something. And Malfoy’s stopped taking points.”

“She told on Hopkins.”

“I know she did. I worked that out on Wednesday.”

“She sat in that room. All three nights. She ate a—” He stopped. “I went to find her. I thought I was going to shout. And she told me why, and it was—it was me. He’s going round a table asking who talked, because of me, and I hadn’t known, and she’d done that to him so he’d stop looking at her, and she stood there with her book and she said he was nobody.”

“And?”

He was still looking at the hand.

“I had her against the wall,” said Harry.

Hermione did not move.

“By the wrist. I didn’t—I don’t remember doing it. She was over there, and then I’d got her. I said *take that back*. I said it just like—I’ve done it before. In first year, in a corridor. I’d forgotten. She hadn’t.” It was very odd. His voice seemed to have gone somewhere up behind his nose, and it was doing the words one by one. “She went white. You’ve never seen her. She’s not frightened of anything, she’s never been frightened of anybody, she’d tell *Dumbledore* his robes didn’t fit. She just stood there. She couldn’t breathe. She looked at me, and I’ve seen that, I’ve seen people look like that, I know what it is, I’ve seen them look like that at—”

He stopped.

“It’s the same hand,” said Harry. “It’s the one with the—you don’t know what’s on it. She put it back together. Nine days. She did it with her *fingers*.”

There was something the matter with the desk. It had gone blurred, and there was a small dark spot on it, by the edge of the tickets, and then there was another. He looked at them for some time before he understood. He had not done this at Sirius’s table, or on a swing, or at three in the morning on the floor of a bedroom. He had not done this, as far as he could recall, in the other life, at the end, when there had been every reason. He tried to stop, and it turned out that nobody was asking him. It simply went on coming, without any noise, down his face and off his chin, like something that had been left running in another room.

Hermione got up.

He heard her chair. He did not look. He heard her walk away down the room to the blackboard, and stop, and stand with her back to him; and after a moment there was the small dry sound of somebody with nothing to rub out rubbing out half a diagram, very carefully, a little at a time.

She went on doing that until it was finished. It took quite a long while.

When she came back she did not say anything. She put down a clean handkerchief and the last of the cheese sandwiches, and sat, and looked out of the window with her chin on her hand.

“I’m going to tell her,” said Hermione.

He wiped his face. “Tell her what?”

“That I know. Not about the wrist. About the rest. She ought to hear it from me, and not find it out. It’s only fair.”

“She’ll kill you.”

“She’s had five years.” She took off the elastic band, which she had put round her wrist, and looked at it, and she stretched it over the notebook. “She’s the worst person I’ve ever met, Harry. I want that written down too. She took a boy’s hand. And you love her, and I’ve got that written down, it’s under the green tab; it’s been there since the Yule Ball. What did she say when you told her?”

He laughed. It came out wet and rather horrible.

“Hold still,” said Harry.

Hermione shut her eyes.

“Yes,” she said. “She would.”

She picked up the three tickets, and put them back in their envelope; and then, after a moment, instead of putting it back in the notebook, she held it out to him across the desk.

It was ten to two. She had the rota.

They met her on the fourth floor.

There was no way of not meeting her. It was the top of the stairs, with the corridor empty in both directions, and she came round the corner with her book of rounds under her arm and her hair very neatly pinned, by herself, which she never used to be. Crabbe was not with her. He had time to see that. He had time to think that she looked as if she had not been eating, and then she had seen him, and she stopped.

She looked at his eyes.

He knew what they looked like. He had seen them in a window on the way down. She looked at them for a moment, and he saw her take it in, red rims and all; and then she looked past him, at Hermione, who had come out on to the landing with a bag and a good many crumbs, at two o’clock on a Saturday, from wherever the two of them had been.

Something happened to her mouth. It went small and thin, and her nostrils narrowed. He had never seen it before. He had only heard about it, from Hermione, in connection with a spoon.

For about a second and a half it was the best thing that had happened to him all week.

Then she saw where he was looking. He had not meant to. His eyes had simply gone there: to her left cuff, buttoned to the wrist, on a mild day, with the sleeve drawn well down over the heel of her hand.

She moved it behind her back.

She did it quite easily, with no change of expression. She might have been shifting her book. He would not have noticed, in anybody else; he had seen a great many people do it. He stood at the top of the stairs and watched Lyra Malfoy put her hand out of his reach.

She went by on the far side of the corridor, with her eyes in front of her, and she did not hurry.

Hermione touched his sleeve. “Come on,” she said.

Ron was in the common room with his boots off and his feet on the fender, looking pleased with himself. He had let in nineteen out of forty, which he said was a personal best. He looked up, and looked again.

“Blimey,” said Ron. “Where’ve you been? You look awful. Have you got Hermione’s cold?”

“Yes,” said Harry.
