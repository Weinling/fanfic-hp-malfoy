# Chapter 44: Prefects

“I’m counting on you,” said her father, on the platform, with the steam going up white round the pair of them and Draco already halfway into the train.

He said it lightly, and he did not need to say it any other way. Lyra kissed her mother, and let her father kiss her forehead, and watched her mother put one hand on Draco’s head as he went past, quite flat, as though she were measuring him, and keep it there a moment after he had gone.

Then the whistle went, and they were moving.

Draco had found Crabbe and Goyle before the train had cleared the platform, or they had found him: Lyra came past their compartment on her way to the front of the train and saw him through the glass, sitting between the two of them with his legs not reaching the floor, explaining the Wronski Feint with both hands and a Chocolate Frog. Goyle was listening with enormous seriousness. Crabbe was watching Draco, and then the door, and then Lyra in the corridor beyond it; and she lifted one hand an inch from her lap, which was a great deal, from Crabbe.

Every so often, in the middle of a sentence, Draco put his hand flat against the front of his shirt, where something folded small lay against his chest, and pressed it, the way one presses a pocket to be sure of a ticket. Then he went on talking.

Lyra went on to the front of the train.

The prefects’ carriage was at the very front, behind the engine, with a brass plate on the door. She had walked past that door four years running and wondered what went on behind it. The answer, it turned out, was a long table with a green cloth on it and a plate of sandwiches, and the Head Boy at the end, with a list.

The Head Boy was Cedric Diggory.

He looked up when she came in. He had a faint white line at his hairline, on the left, where something in a maze had caught him in June, and the badge on his chest, and the same open, handsome, decent face that had made half the school forgive him for being good at things. He looked at her, and at the P on her robes, and the decent face stopped being interested.

“Malfoy,” he said.

“Diggory.”

“Congratulations.” He did not say on what. He looked down at his list. “You’re with Zabini. Slytherin, fifth year. Rounds on the train every half-hour, front to back, Zabini takes back to front. Anything you can’t deal with, you come to me.”

“I imagine I shall manage.”

“I imagine you will,” said Diggory, without looking up, and she understood that she was being punished, very courteously, for a thing that had been done to him on a podium in June by somebody else, and that she was going to be punished for it all year.

She sat down beside Zabini, who was eating a sandwich and appeared to find the whole occasion faintly comic.

The Gryffindors came in last. Granger first, with her badge already on and a stack of books under her arm, as though she had been wearing it since July, which she very likely had; and behind her, filling the doorway, with his own badge pinned crooked on a jumper his mother had certainly knitted, Weasley.

Lyra had not known about Weasley. She looked at the badge, and then at him, for long enough that he went pink.

“Something to say, Malfoy?”

She had a great many things to say. She had been not saying them since a Hogsmeade afternoon in March, when they had argued about the Chudley Cannons like two old men on a bench, and she had sat at a table in the Three Broomsticks between Weasley and Potter with her hand held under it, and been happy. She had liked that afternoon. That was the trouble with it.

She was on the other side now. She had said so in an Owlery, to the only person whose opinion of her had ever mattered, and she had meant it: all the way, as herself. And there was no use in being on the other side of anything by halves.

“Only that I’m glad for your mother,” said Lyra pleasantly. “She must be so relieved. Something on your chest that she hasn’t had to knit herself.”

Zabini stopped eating.

Weasley’s ears went first, and then the rest of him. “You—”

“And I’m sure the Ministry will be glad, too,” Lyra went on, in the same voice, the one her mother kept for tradespeople. “Your father’s been working there, what is it, thirty years? Thirty years in an office in the basement, and still the same robes. It must be such a comfort to him that one of his sons is going to be *somebody*, in a small way. Before it all comes down.”

“Before what comes down?”

She looked at him.

“Whatever’s coming. You’ve heard Dumbledore. You must have. And when it does, Weasley, I should think very hard about which side of a house you want to be standing on. Your family has never been very good at that.”

The carriage had gone quite still.

“You were *his*,” said Weasley. His voice had gone low and rough, and he was not pink any more; he was white. “All last year. In front of everybody. You sat in the Three Broomsticks and argued about the Cannons. And now you’re back to being your dad’s.” He looked at her, at the badge, at her face. “That was quick.”

It went in. She kept her eyes on his badge.

“I was never anybody’s. You just weren’t paying attention.”

Granger had said nothing at all. She stood beside Weasley with her books against her chest and looked at Lyra across the green tablecloth, not angrily. Lyra had expected angrily. She had been ready for it. She had not been ready for Granger to look at her as she looked at an Arithmancy problem with a mistake in it somewhere, a long way back, that would have to be found.

“Right,” said Diggory, into the silence, in the voice of a man who had chaired worse meetings. “Rounds. Weasley, Granger, you’re the next pair. Let’s try and keep the House stuff out of the corridors, shall we? There are first-years on this train.”

She was on her second round, with fields and sheep rolling past the windows, when a door slid open beside her and Potter stepped out into the corridor.

He had been waiting. She could tell from the way he stood: not leaning, not casual, his hands at his sides. He looked thinner than he had in Diagon Alley, and older, and very tired round the eyes, and his hair had not recovered.

“Malfoy.”

“I’m on rounds, Potter. Go and sit down.”

“Parkinson. And Montague. Fourth compartment from the back. They’ve got a lot of Ravenclaw third-years in there, and they’re talking about who’s back. Loudly. With names.”

She stopped.

“What names?”

“Not his. Theirs.” He looked at her. “Whose dads went out, the night of the Third Task. Montague’s. Parkinson thinks it’s funny.”

She was already moving. *A careless word in the wrong corridor*, her father had said, *could cost us a great deal*. She had promised to keep them quiet, and proud; and the first careless word of the year was being said, loudly, by Panther Parkinson, in front of a lot of Ravenclaw children whose parents would hear it by Monday. And it was Potter who had come to tell her.

She stopped at the end of the corridor, with her hand on the door to the next carriage, and did not turn round.

“What is it to you?”

“You’ll shut them up, and nobody else can.”

“You might have told Granger. She’s a prefect. You tell her everything, I expect.”

It came out sharper than she had meant. She heard it go, and could not have it back.

There was a pause behind her.

“No. I don’t.”

She did turn round then. He was standing exactly where she had left him, in the swaying corridor, with the fields going by behind him through the glass; and he looked at her, and said, quite quietly, as if he had been carrying it since August and had only now found somewhere to put it down:

“You were laughing. At Fortescue’s. With Draco. Before you saw me.”

She had been. Draco had said something about the Hufflepuffs, with ice cream on his chin, and she had laughed, out loud, on a terrace in Diagon Alley in the sun, for the first time all summer. And somebody on the other side of the street had seen her do it, and had crossed the whole of the road, and had talked about a bow tie.

She walked back down the corridor. There was a compartment behind him with its blind down and nothing in it but somebody’s owl. She put her hand flat on his chest and walked him backwards through the door, and slid it shut with her heel.

“That,” said Lyra, “is what you came to say.”

“Yeah.”

“In August.”

“Yeah.”

“You had a street and a fortnight and four hundred pages. Did you read that book?”

Something went across his face, and his ears began, very slowly, to change colour.

“Some of it,” said Potter.

She looked at him. She looked at the ears. She had had no quill that day, and no time, and he knew that, because he was not a fool; and it came to her, whole, as such things did, a boy on a floor in the middle of the night with his wand lit, going through the tables of values one margin at a time.

“There was nothing in it,” said Lyra.

“No. I know.”

“Every page?”

He did not answer. It was quite the most pathetic thing she had ever heard of. She kept her face where it was, and underneath it something that had sat up on a dungeon staircase in June sat up again and asked to be told the whole thing slowly.

“I am allowed to laugh, Potter. It’s not against any law. He wanted to know whether Hufflepuff could be cured.” She took a breath. “What would you have preferred? Should I have been crying? Would that have suited you better?”

He looked at the owl. He looked at the floor, and then he looked at her, and she saw him decide.

“A bit,” said Potter.

The train went over some points. Neither of them said anything.

It was a horrible thing to say to a person. He knew it was; he had gone white round the mouth saying it. Nobody who was any good would have said it, and nobody who had any sense would have asked. She ought to have been a great deal angrier than she was.

“You are perfectly vile,” said Lyra.

“Yeah.”

“Good. I have thought of nine better answers to what you asked me in June, and you shan’t have one of them.”

That went in. She had waited ten weeks to see it, and she found that it was not at all as satisfying as it had been on the ceiling.

“I know what I asked,” said Potter, very low. “I shouldn’t—”

“No. You shouldn’t.”

His collar had got folded in under the neck of his jumper. Mrs Weasley’s jumper, by the look of it; maroon, and much too big. She had put it straight and turned the point of it down before she noticed that she was doing it, and his hand came up and closed round her wrist, not hard.

“Don’t,” he said. His voice had gone rough. “That’s twice. You can’t keep doing that and sending me tins.”

She looked at the hand. After a moment he took it away.

“Sit down, Potter,” said Lyra.

She opened the door. The owl watched her go.

She went on through the connecting door, and it slid shut behind her, and she walked the whole length of it before she let herself do anything with her face; and then she found the fourth compartment from the back.

She heard Parkinson before she saw him. He had the voice he used in the common room for things he had heard first, and it carried through the door as if the door were not there. “—no, *properly* back, I’m telling you, my dad says Montague’s went out that night and didn’t come home till breakfast, and his arm was—”

Lyra opened the door.

There were seven of them in a compartment built for six: Parkinson by the window, with his feet up; Montague beside him, grinning; and five small Ravenclaws in a row on the opposite seat with their eyes very round, one of them holding a Chocolate Frog halfway to her mouth and not eating it.

“Panther,” said Lyra.

He took his feet down. Montague stopped grinning.

“I didn’t hear the end of that. His arm was what?”

Nobody said anything. The Ravenclaw with the Chocolate Frog put it down on her knee.

“No? Never mind.” Lyra looked at the five of them on the opposite seat, one after another, and smiled at them, very kindly. “Panther tells stories. He’s famous for it. He told the *Daily Prophet* last winter that I was going to a ball with somebody I had no intention of going anywhere with, and they printed it, and he has been very sorry ever since. Haven’t you, Panther?”

“Yes,” said Parkinson, to his knees.

“So you won’t repeat anything he said, I expect,” said Lyra to the Ravenclaws, “because you’d only look silly. And your parents would only worry. And there’s nothing at all to worry about, is there, Graham?”

“No,” said Montague. His ears had gone a dull red. “Nothing.”

“There. Third-years, aren’t you? You’ll want to change into your robes before Hogsmeade. There’s a compartment free at the back.”

They went, all five, in a scurry, the girl with the Chocolate Frog last, looking back at Lyra over her shoulder with an expression that was not quite fear and not quite admiration and would, Lyra thought, be reported in full in a Ravenclaw dormitory by midnight. That did not matter. What mattered was what would not be.

She shut the door behind them, and turned round, and looked at the two boys who were left.

“We are a great House,” said Lyra, quite quietly. “The greatest in this castle. We do not tell tales on our own fathers to children in a railway carriage for the pleasure of watching their faces. If you have something to be proud of, Graham, be proud of it. With your mouth shut. Do you understand me?”

Montague, who was two years older and a head taller, looked at her for a long moment, and then, slowly, nodded.

She went out, and slid the door shut, and stood in the swaying corridor with her hand still on the handle.

She had kept her father’s secret. Potter had sent her to do it.

The first-years came across the lake in the boats, as they always did, and in at the great doors in a long nervous crocodile, and up the middle of the Great Hall behind Professor McGonagall to where the stool stood with the Hat on it; and Draco was eleventh in the line, between a girl with plaits and a boy who looked as though he might be sick.

Lyra watched him come up the Hall.

He was trying very hard to look as though he did this every day. He walked exactly as their father walked, unhurried, with his chin up, and every two steps he forgot and looked round at the ceiling, the stars and the floating candles, with his mouth open, and remembered, and shut it. When he came level with the Slytherin table he looked for her, and found her, and grinned, with the gap where the tooth had been; and she did not smile back, because the whole table was watching, and then did, a little, because he was eleven.

The Hat sang. Lyra did not hear a word of it.

“*Malfoy, Draco.*”

He went up the three steps and sat on the stool, and McGonagall put the Hat on his head, and it came down over his eyes and rested on the bridge of his nose; and nothing happened.

Nothing went on happening.

Lyra had been Sorted in about the time it took to sit down. So had her father, he said, and his father before him, and every Malfoy back to the Conquest; the Hat had barely touched their hair. She sat at the Slytherin table with her hands in her lap and watched the brim of the Hat not move, and counted, and got to ten, and to twenty, and somewhere along the table Parkinson said “What’s *it* doing?” and nobody answered him. Draco’s hands were gripping the edge of the stool. His feet did not reach the floor.

At thirty she stopped counting and waited for the Hat’s mouth to move.

She did not know what she was afraid of. It was absurd. He was a Malfoy; he was her brother; he had their father’s chin and their mother’s eyes and a list from an old man in a cottage folded against his heart. There was nowhere else in this castle the Hat could possibly put him. And it went on sitting there, on her brother’s head, thinking about it, as though it had found something in him it had not expected and wanted to be sure.

“*SLYTHERIN!*”

The table went up round her in a roar. Draco came down the steps with his face scarlet and blazing and ran, actually ran, the last few yards, and Montague hauled him on to the bench beside him and thumped him on the back; and Lyra let out her breath all in one piece, and found that her hands had gone white in her lap, and unclenched them, one finger at a time, under the table, where nobody could see.

Across the Hall, at the Gryffindor table, Potter was not clapping. He was looking at Draco, with an expression she could not read at that distance; and then, as if he had felt her looking, at her.

It was at the end of the feast, when Dumbledore was on his feet with the plates cleared, that the woman in the pink cardigan coughed.

Lyra had noticed her at the start of the meal, at the staff table, in the chair where Mad-Eye Moody had sat last year: a short, squat woman with a broad pale face and a little black velvet bow in her hair like a fly that had landed there, who had smiled throughout dinner at nobody in particular. Dumbledore had said her name. Professor Umbridge. Defence Against the Dark Arts. Nobody had clapped much.

“*Hem, hem.*”

Dumbledore stopped in the middle of a sentence. It was the first time in five years that Lyra had seen anybody make him do it.

Professor Umbridge rose. She was not much taller standing than sitting. She thanked the Headmaster for his kind words of welcome, in a high girlish voice like a child reciting, and then she made a speech; and it went on for some time, and most of the Hall stopped listening to it about a third of the way through, and began to whisper. It was about progress for the sake of progress, and tradition, and pruning; about practices that ought to be prohibited. It was very dull.

Lyra listened to every word of it. She had grown up at a dinner table where speeches like that were made over the port by men from the Ministry, and she knew precisely what one sounded like when it was saying, under all the cotton wool, *the Ministry is here now, and it is not leaving*.

She looked across the Hall at the Gryffindor table.

Most of them were whispering, or yawning, or looking at the ceiling. Granger was frowning with her whole face, as at a sum she did not like. And Potter was sitting very still with his elbows on the table, watching Professor Umbridge, and on his face there was no surprise whatever.

She knew that face. She had watched him wear it at a start-of-term feast once, when a man with a wooden leg came in out of a storm. He was looking at Umbridge as though he had met her before, and had not liked her, and was already working out exactly what it was going to cost.
