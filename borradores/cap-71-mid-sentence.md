# Chapter 71: Mid-Sentence

Harry had been looking forward to the History of Magic examination for four years and five months.

He got up at seven and cleaned his teeth. It was a white, still, airless morning, with the sun already hot on the dormitory floor, and he stood at the basin and looked at himself and felt, for the first time in his life, like the only person who has read the last page. The bed next to his had its blanket pulled straight. He tied a bit of parchment to Hedwig’s leg, at the window, as he had done every morning for twelve days. *Still bored? H.* He gave her a piece of bacon. She went off north over the Forbidden Forest with her shadow going along under her, and that was everything he had to do.

He knew where Sirius was. That was the beauty of it. On page 71 of Dudley’s road atlas there was a place on Rannoch Moor, a long way from any of the thin red lines, with a small grey square on it that the Ordnance Survey had marked as *Bothy (ruin)*. He had got him there in a week. He had written to say he’d found something, that it was to do with the seven, that somebody had to sit on that hillside until the nineteenth and watch who came, and that it couldn’t be anybody in the Order, because one of them was talking, and that it couldn’t be mentioned to Dumbledore. All of that was rubbish. Sirius had been shut up in his mother’s house since January, and he had gone like a dog out of a car. *FINALLY*, he had written, in letters an inch high. And every day since, Hedwig had come back with a scrap of something tied to her leg, smelling of peat.

*Day 9. Have counted the sheep. 41. One of them is looking at me.*

*Day 11. It’s raining in here but not outside. Tell Moony he owes me a Sickle. Nobody has come. Are you sure about this?*

Yes, thought Harry. He was very sure.

He had put a square of parchment into Hermione’s hands to make certain. *He’s here*, and the reference, six figures. *It’s for the 18th. There’ll be a dream, in History, that they’ve got him. It’s a trap and it isn’t true. Whatever I say, whatever I look like, don’t let me go.* Nobody in the other life had thought of that. There was a boy of fifteen going to get up from a desk this afternoon, howling for his godfather, and he was the only one who knew it, and he’d left instructions.

And he’d be there, in the Great Hall, with a sharpened quill. Today, for a single afternoon, he was going to know exactly what would happen next. He’d sit in his chair. He’d get the dream and look it in the eye. He would go on with the question.

At breakfast Parkinson dropped his fork.

Harry looked up, with everybody else. Malfoy had come in. She walked the length of the Slytherin table with her book under her arm and her nose in the air, in the ordinary way, and there was a slight, spreading, disbelieving quiet going along the bench beside her, like something being poured. She sat down. She reached for the toast, with her left hand. She took some time over it. There was a ring on the fourth finger, a plain silver band, and it was as pink as a shrimp. She buttered a corner. Then, for about the length of a breath, across forty feet of hall and two hundred upturned faces, she raised her eyes and looked at him, with such a level, dry, private satisfaction that he nearly choked on his tea.

*Well*, said the look. *I’ve seen to it. Haven’t you?*

He had no idea what she meant. He grinned at her. He couldn’t have stopped himself if you’d paid him.

They went in at two. The four long rows of little desks had been set out again, and the sun came in through the high windows in great solid slabs, and it smelt of hot wood and ink and fear. *You may begin*, said the old wizard with the egg-timer. Two hundred sheets of parchment turned over.

She was four rows in front of him and one to the left. He could see the back of her head.

He knew a surprising amount. He did Bagnold. He spelt her right. He gave the three causes of the Goblin Rebellion of 1612. His quill went along with a dry, even, contented sound. It got to be twenty to three, and three; a bluebottle was walking on the rim of the inkwell, and he felt nothing at all but a little sleepy. He had begun to think that it might not come. He thought he would be rather annoyed. *Question 9*, said the paper. *Explain why the International Statute of Secrecy was thought necessary in 1689, and why*

He dipped his quill.

*The wizarding community feared that Muggle persecution would*, wrote Harry, and his forehead split open.

He had forgotten. You couldn’t remember it, that was the thing. You thought that you could, but it didn’t keep. He saw the sheet of parchment go a long way off. He saw the nib slide away from the *d* of *would*, trailing a thin black line. Then the floor of the Great Hall was coming up, or he was going down, and it was dark and enormously cool, and he was walking. He was tall. There was a lot of him, and he was pleased. His hand was long and white and he didn’t need to look at it. He went down some stone steps, at his leisure, with people on either side of him pressing themselves against the benches. There was a pit, and in the middle of the pit, on a raised place, there was an arch with something stirring in it.

He knew that room. He had known it for a while. He would know it till he died.

*All right*, thought Harry, from a long way up, in a small clear voice. *All right, here we go. There you are.*

It was different. He noted that. It should have been the shelves, with all the little glass balls, and the rows going by. He wasn’t bothered; it was the same road. There was Bellatrix Lestrange on the top step, with her dirty finger in her mouth and her eyes shining. She was laughing without any noise. She had something by the scruff. She gave it a shake, and it dropped, and it lay across the bottom of the arch, breathing in great wet pulls: a big black shaggy dog, with one foreleg going the wrong way, and its tongue on the stone.

It was very good. It was extremely well done. You could see the burrs in its coat. *Nice try*, thought Harry. He’d got his teeth together. He felt the long white hand lift a little, and the wand turn over in the fingers, and he felt how much it was enjoying itself; and the thing he had been looking out of stopped.

It turned its head.

No one had told him about this. It had not done that before. It looked up and round, with its slit red eyes, without any hurry, past the benches and the pit. It looked at the place he was looking from. It looked straight at him, as you’d look up at someone on a landing.

“Come and see,” it said.

The voice was light and courteous. It was addressed to him personally.

“Mr Potter? Mr Potter, can you hear me?”

There was a bluebottle. There was a smell of floor polish, against his cheek. He was lying on his side in the aisle, with a lot of faces and a very old wizard bending down, and somebody’s sandal, and his head was full of broken glass, and he’d been sick, a little, on his sleeve.

He found he was smiling.

It was the most tremendous relief. He lay and looked at the sandal, and it went through him like a hot drink. *That’s it. That’s all. That’s the lot.* It had come. It had come at exactly ten past three on the eighteenth of June, in the middle of the paper, and it was a lie, and he’d known it was a lie. He had lain there and looked at it, and he’d said *Nice try*. Four and a half years. He had got his foot on it. “I’m fine,” he said. “Sorry. Too hot.” They got him up. He saw a pale pointed face turned round, four rows on, with a pen stopped in the air, and white as paper; and he gave her, he actually gave her, a small thumbs-up. He let them walk him down the aisle to the doors. “You may finish in the Hospital Wing, Mr Potter, I’m sure in the circumstances—” “It’s all right, sir. I’ll just get some air. I’ll come back.” He’d left his quill on the desk. He had left a sentence with no end on it. He was going to go and sit on the front steps for ten minutes in the sun, and then he’d go in and finish it.

The steps were hot through his robes. There was nobody out there. The lawn ran down white and shimmering towards the lake, and there wasn’t a breath of air.

He had been sitting for perhaps five minutes, with his head in his hands and his heart coming slowly down, when something brushed the top of his hair, and there was a scrabble of claws on his shoulder.

“Hello,” said Harry. “You’re late.”

She shuffled along his arm and held out her leg, and looked at him. She’d come a long way; she was panting, with her beak a little open. He undid the string, not looking, with his thumb. It was rather a nice knot. He’d got quite good at that knot.

He looked at it.

He looked at it for some time, without any particular thought. It was a bit of parchment, folded in four. It was perfectly dry. It didn’t smell of peat.

He unfolded it.

*Still bored? H.*

Someone was running. There was a lot of echo. He went up the marble staircase, and he remembered the gargoyle on the second floor, because he hit it. “Sherbet lemon,” he said. “Cockroach Cluster. Fizzing Whizzbee, *please*.” It looked over his head. The Headmaster had gone off at noon, said a portrait with its wig on crooked, he’d had a note from the Minister. He went down again. He went down four flights and along the dungeon passage. He felt the third flagstone rock, and he beat on the door with both his fists. It was locked. Nobody came. He stood there with his forehead on the wood.

*Think.*

She didn’t leave her post. She had never in her life come back with a note undelivered. She’d have gone to the bothy. She’d have gone round it, and sat on the wall; she’d have waited, hours, she always waited. And he hadn’t been there. He had been there yesterday. He’d been there every day for twelve days, counting sheep. There was nowhere for him to go. Nobody knew. *Nobody knew.* There were two people in the whole world who knew about that hillside, and one of them was Hermione. He had made sure of that. He had seen to it.

He had looked at that dog, with its leg going the wrong way.

It was a dream. It was a false one; he knew it was. He knew exactly how it was done. They’d sent it on purpose. He had given them the idea himself, on a rug, with a quill behind his ear.

So where was he?

You could not have told anyone what that was like. He tried, later on. He’d had five years of it, the most frightful certainty, going down the days like a man going down the rungs of a ladder in the dark, knowing each one; he had hated it, he’d have given anything to be rid of it. He had been so tired. And he put out his foot, and it wasn’t there. There was no next rung. There was only the dark, in every direction, with nothing written on it, and it occurred to him that this was what it was like for everybody. They got up every morning and walked out on to this. He’d never known. He didn’t know how anyone could bear it.

If he’d only known what would happen next. He’d have given his hand.

Hermione came out at ten to four. She’d finished early. She came through the doors of the Great Hall with her bag on her shoulder and her eyes on the floor, and she stopped, because he was standing in front of her, and she looked at him for the first time in twenty-four days.

He held out the bit of parchment. He didn’t seem to be able to say anything. She took it. She read the three words on it, and she looked at the outside, and then at Hedwig, who had come in after him and was sitting on the banister with her feathers on end.

She went very still.

She undid the elastic band. She found the place, and took out the small square, and she opened it and held it up in front of his face. Her hand was steady. She’d got the look of someone holding a ruler to a child’s knuckles and hating it.

“‘Whatever I say,’” said Hermione. “‘Whatever I look like.’”

“He’s not there.”

“It’s the eighteenth. You got the dream. You told me you would. I saw you fall down, Harry, I saw you put your thumb up. It’s a trap, it says so, it’s in your writing.”

“I know it’s a trap! I *know!*” It rang all up the stairwell. “I know it’s not true, I know what it is—he’s not *there*, Hermione. He’s not at the place. I put him there. I told him nobody would come. If they haven’t got him, where is he? He wouldn’t go, he’d have said, he’s had an owl every day— There’s nobody else knew. There’s nobody. What if it’s true? This time? What if that’s what it is, what if it’s all the same, and they’ve got him, and they got him because I took him out of the one house on earth with Moody’s charms on it and I stuck him on a *moor?*”

She didn’t answer. He saw her go through it, with her lips pressed white. She went down it, line by line. He had seen her do that with a Potions essay and with the whole of his life. She looked at the note in her hand, and the other note, and she looked at him; there was blood on his knuckles, he didn’t know from what.

“Sit down, Harry,” said Hermione.

He looked back at her.

“I promised,” she said. Her voice shook, once, and steadied. “I’m saying it. I want that understood. Sit down.”

“No,” said Harry.

For a little while neither of them moved. He saw her hand go to her wand. It was what he had asked for, in writing, with a reference. He saw it stay there, and he saw her think of it. Then it came away. People had started to come out past them, in twos and threes, blinking and comparing dates. Then something went out of her shoulders. She folded the square of parchment in half, and in half again, and put it in the notebook and snapped the band.

“No,” said Hermione. “I didn’t think so.” She shut her eyes and she opened them. “It’s Sirius. I’m coming. I’m not coming with you, I want you to know the difference.”

She turned, and walked straight across the Entrance Hall. Ron had come out with Dean Thomas, and he stopped dead when he saw who was walking at him. She said something, about a dozen words. Harry saw his ears. He saw him look down at her, and for a moment he thought he was going to look up, over her head, towards the stairs. He didn’t. He gave one nod. He dropped his bag where he stood, and he went off along the corridor towards McGonagall’s office, at a run, with his big feet slapping on the stone.

That was the Order told, then. Somebody would come. It would take them an hour, and he hadn’t got one.

Neville was the next out. Harry got hold of him by the front of his robes. He had meant to say something sensible.

“Bellatrix Lestrange,” said Harry. “At the Ministry. Now.”

Neville looked at him. He had a blot on his chin. He looked for about a second and a half, with his round mild anxious face; and then he bent down and put his bag very carefully against the wall, so that it shouldn’t be in anybody’s way.

“All right,” said Neville.

There wasn’t anyone else. He thought that, going up the stairs, with the blood banging in his head; and he came round the corner of the first-floor corridor and there was a tall boy leaning against the wall opposite Umbridge’s door, with his arms folded and his Head Boy’s badge in the sun.

“You came out of that exam on your feet, Potter,” said Cedric Diggory, “and you’ve been running ever since, and Granger’s got a face like a coroner. I saw you at the gargoyle. What’s going on?”

He told him. It took three sentences. Cedric listened, with no expression. He looked at the door, and at Neville, and at Hermione coming up behind them with her wand already out.

“Fireplace?” he said.

“It’s the only one they’re not watching. It’s been empty since March.”

“You’ll want somebody who can hold a shield.” He pushed himself off the wall. He took out his wand, and he looked down at Harry, from a height, for a moment. “I’ll go in front,” said Cedric Diggory. “I don’t care to have you behind me.”

It smelt of old violets. Nobody had been in there since the Aurors. There was dust on the lace and dust on the little table; a square of paler pink on the carpet showed where the desk had stood, and all round the walls the kittens looked out of their plates and mewed, in a thin, hopeful, rusty way, as if somebody might at last have come with the milk.

The pot was on the mantelpiece. Cedric took a pinch, and threw it, and the flames went up emerald. “Ministry of Magic,” he said, in a clear hard voice, and he stepped in and turned, and was gone. Neville went next. He said *Thank you* to the fire. Harry held out the pot to Hermione, and she took some; and that was when he heard feet.

They were coming along the corridor very fast. They had hard heels, and they were not used to running. The door hit the wall.

Malfoy stopped on the threshold, with one hand on the frame.

He had never seen her look like that. She had no bag. Her hair had started to come down on one side, and she was fighting for breath, and there was a long streak of ink on her wrist. She must have been all over the castle. She looked at the green fire, and at the pot in his hand, and he saw it reach her. Her left hand had closed on her wand. On the fourth finger, the ring had gone the colour of a fresh cut.

“No,” said Malfoy.

She crossed the room. She did it in about four steps. She got in between him and the mantelpiece and turned round, with her back to the flames and the green light all down one side of her, and she was so out of breath that it hardly made a sound.

“You can’t go. Potter. Listen to me. Don’t go, you mustn’t, you’re not to leave the— He isn’t there. He isn’t anywhere near. It’s a trap, you said so yourself, you *told* me, he isn’t—”

“I know it’s a trap.”

“Then what are you—”

“He’s gone.” It came out quite quietly. “He’s not where I put him. Get out of the way, Malfoy.”

“I know he’s gone! That’s what I’m— Listen. Oh, will you *listen*. It’s my—”

She stopped.

It was only for an instant; she had looked past his shoulder, at Hermione, standing with her hand full of powder. She had run out of air. That was all it was. He would go over that, a very great number of times. She breathed in. But he had made a study of that face. He had been at it for five years, and nobody living knew it as he did, and he saw it happen. He saw her eyes go down the column and stop. He saw her decide what she was going to say.

*I know he’s gone.*

Everything got slow and quite bright.

He could see each of the kittens. He could see the wand in her hand, and the fire. She had looked at him across the Great Hall that very morning, over the toast. *I’ve seen to it.* He’d thought it was a joke. She’d looked at him just like that, with her chin up, when she was thirteen, and fourteen; she had looked like that in a classroom on the third floor, and she’d said *No*. And there had been five months of Sundays, in her best hand. *He dreams of his godfather’s death.* Nobody knew about the moor. Nobody knew where he was; and here she stood, between him and the only thing he’d got left, with a Malfoy’s wand in her hand, telling him she knew.

He didn’t believe it. He would swear to that. He never for one moment believed it, not with the part of him that had got up on a Sunday and held a coat.

But it went through his head. There was time for that. It went through quite slowly, and it looked about, and it didn’t seem to be a stranger.

She had taken a step towards him. She had put out her hand; the left one, with the ring. Her mouth had started to shape the word.

“Potter, he’s with my—”

*Don’t go*, she had said. He’d said that once. He had said it to her, with his hand round her arm.

He noticed that his own arm had come up. It had come up very smoothly, from the shoulder, and it was quite steady. It wasn’t going to need any help this time. He didn’t raise his voice. There wasn’t any need; he felt extraordinarily calm. He felt as if he had been standing for years in a very long queue and had got, at last, to the front of it.

“*Petrificus Totalus*,” said Harry.

It took her in the middle of the word. He saw it go across her face, from the hairline down. Her mouth stopped, a little open. Her arms snapped in against her sides, and her heels came together, and she stood for a moment bolt upright, with the green fire behind her, like something that had been set up there. Then she began to tip. She went over backwards, all in one piece, as a plank goes, towards the corner of the fender.

He caught her.

He hadn’t thought about it. He’d dropped the pot. He got one arm behind her head before it reached the iron, and he went down on one knee and put her on the pink carpet, in the pale square where the desk had stood, and drew her clear of the hearth. He took the wand from between her fingers and looked at it. He put it in his pocket. Her hands were wrong. It seemed important to get that done properly: he set them straight at her sides, as you’d square a pile of cards. There was a pin that had worked loose. He took it out, and laid it on the fender.

He looked at his work. It was all quite tidy.

She was looking at him.

Of course she was. It didn’t stop your eyes. They were wide open and very pale, about a foot from his, and they moved. They went over his face, from one side to the other. There was water standing in them that couldn’t fall, because she couldn’t blink. She could hear him; she could hear everything. She was trying to say the rest of the word.

He didn’t want her to be frightened. She was to understand that there was nothing to be frightened of. It would wear off. She would be quite safe here; nobody ever came. He knew what he was doing. He knew exactly what he was doing, and he wasn’t angry; there wasn’t any hurry, he had all the time in the world, and he would see to it. He would see to all of it. He bent down, and he smiled at her, so that she’d know.

He kissed her on the forehead, as you’d sign something. Her skin was cold.

“I’ll be back,” said Harry. “Stay there.”

He stood up. He picked up the pot and straightened it on the shelf. On the wall a kitten in a bonnet mewed at him.

Hermione hadn’t moved. He’d forgotten about her. She was standing by the fender, with the powder running slowly out between her fingers on to the hearth. He looked at her, and she looked back. She had seen him in a good many ways. She’d sat at a desk and watched him cry, and she had seen him in a corridor, in first year, with a girl’s wand in his hand. She looked down at the carpet. She looked for some time. She looked up at his face, at whatever was on it. He did not know what was on it. He couldn’t feel anything there at all.

She didn’t say anything. She had no notebook in her hand, and she didn’t reach for it.

She threw what was left of the powder, and said two or three words, and went.

The fire dropped, and hissed, and burnt up green. Harry took a good handful from the pot. He didn’t look behind him. He stepped over the fender and into the flames, and they were not hot; and he turned, as you have to, and shut his eyes, and said where he was going, very clearly, in a voice that carried.
