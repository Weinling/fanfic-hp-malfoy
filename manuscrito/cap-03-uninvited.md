# Chapter 3: Uninvited

“You were rude to my friends,” said Lyra Malfoy.

The station guard looked down. The tip of a wand was resting very precisely against the second brass button of his uniform, and at the other end of it was a thirteen-year-old girl in a camel coat, looking up at him with an expression she had learned from the portrait of her great-grandmother on the first landing at home. It had stopped house-elves at thirty paces.

It had been a trying quarter of an hour.

Her mother had gone through the barrier ten minutes earlier to find Mrs Parkinson and hear, at length, about Mrs Parkinson’s new drawing room, and Lyra had elected to stay on the Muggle side and wait for Crabbe and Goyle. Anyone could simply turn up on Platform Nine and Three-Quarters. It took a particular kind of girl to make an entrance, and an entrance required a retinue, even when the retinue in question had apparently never learned to read a clock.

So she had waited, with her trunk and her eagle owl on a trolley beside her and her shoulders resting lightly against a brick pillar between platforms nine and ten, and one foot tapping. People had looked at her as they went past. People generally did. It was one of the few things about King’s Cross that felt normal.

The guard had not been normal. He had hovered at the edge of her vision for some minutes, a stout man with a peaked cap and a clipboard he did not appear to need, and then come over to ask whether she had lost her mum and dad, and to tell her that the Leeds train was going in four minutes, and that there was nothing else off this platform for half an hour, and that a station was no place for a girl on her own, there were all sorts about these days.

Lyra had clicked her tongue and gone back to tapping her foot. She had found that with Muggles it was best not to encourage them.

Then Crabbe and Goyle had come shoving their trolley through the crowd at a dangerous speed, Goyle out of breath and short of a hair ribbon (“Mum made us come through the Leaky Cauldron and then she couldn’t work out which way was north”). Lyra had just finished assuring them that they were not late, having been there a quarter of an hour, when the corner of their trolley clipped the guard’s knee, and he hopped back and rounded on them.

“Oi! Watch where you’re going with that, you clumsy great—”

And that had been quite enough.

“I don’t mind it for myself,” Lyra went on, pleasantly. “I’m used to being envied. But they’re *mine*.”

The guard’s mouth opened and nothing came out of it.

Lyra considered him, at her leisure, as one considers a menu. “Warts, I think. On the soles of your feet. Starting tonight.”

She gave him the thin smile her father kept for shopkeepers, tucked her wand back into her sleeve, and wheeled her trolley round the pillar with Crabbe and Goyle close behind, straight at the solid brick of the barrier and through it.

She had not, of course, done anything at all. She was thirteen, the Ministry knew when underage witches waved their wands about in Muggle stations, and she had not needed to. The old charms on the barrier would see to it that by lunchtime he had forgotten her face. But something would stay with him, down where the charms never quite reached, and Lyra was entirely confident that for the next week or so he would take off his socks every night and check.

Crabbe was shaking with suppressed laughter. “Warts!”

“On his feet,” said Goyle, with deep reverence.

“Don’t gawp,” said Lyra, extremely pleased. “It’s common.”

Her mother was waiting by the third carriage, having escaped Mrs Parkinson, and kissed her goodbye on both cheeks. Then she held Lyra by the shoulders and looked at her, the long, considering look she had been giving her across the breakfast table all summer, and said only, “Write on Sundays.” Lyra promised she would.

She had not told her mother about the diary. She had not asked about the east cottage either, where the light had come on four more times before the end of August without anybody at breakfast ever mentioning a guest. Between the two of them there was a great deal of not asking going on, and Lyra did not want to find out on a crowded platform which of them was better at it.

The compartment she wanted was the last one in the last carriage, and when they reached it there were two sixth-year Ravenclaws already inside with their feet up.

“We could just turf them out,” said Crabbe, who was still pink from the run across the station.

“Turf them out? Crabbe. We are not *Gryffindors*.”

“Oh,” said Lyra, a little louder, to nobody in particular, pausing in the doorway. “Someone’s here already. I’d rather hoped to sit here today.”

It was enough. The Ravenclaws looked at each other, then at her, and scrambled up with the slightly dazed smiles of people who were not sure why they were doing something but felt quite good about it, and within a minute they had gathered their things and gone off up the corridor in search of somewhere else. Lyra paid them one smile each on the way out. They seemed to consider it a bargain.

“You never even asked them!” said Crabbe, delighted.

“Certainly not. Asking is very nearly as bad as turfing. They overheard a wish, and they were good enough to grant it.”

She settled into the seat by the window and flicked her wand at the wicker hamper Pobble had packed. Out floated a silver teapot, three cups and a small avalanche of paper bags from her mother’s confectioner in Diagon Alley, and Crabbe and Goyle caught them with the reflexes of long practice. For a few minutes the compartment was very peaceful indeed.

Lyra poured herself a cup of tea. “Today,” she said, “nothing is going to happen. Nothing at all, from here to Hogsmeade.”

“Nothing,” agreed Goyle, through a mouthful of Fizzing Whizzbee. “Oh, but Malfoy, did you hear about Potter?”

Lyra inhaled a mouthful of tea, coughed into her handkerchief until her eyes watered, and then fixed Goyle with a look that should by rights have removed her eyebrows.

“I said *nothing*, Goyle. Potter is not nothing. Potter is a weather event.”

“But this one’s *good*. He got arrested! By the Muggle Aurors!”

“By the what?”

“You know. The ones with the tall blue hats. Police.” Goyle leaned in, thrilled. “My cousin’s friend’s dad works in Magical Law Enforcement, and he said the Ministry had to send actual Aurors to fetch him out. And they’re all over the station this morning, didn’t you see? All those green cars out the front?”

Lyra had seen them, and had assumed they were there for some tiresome official. She set her cup down.

The arrest itself did not surprise her in the least. Potter had spent two years committing every offence the school rules had a name for and several they did not, and he always turned up to Transfiguration the next morning looking faintly pleased with himself. What interested her was the rest of it. If the Ministry simply wanted a wizarding boy out of a Muggle police station, anybody with a decent Memory Charm could have gone round and done it in a quarter of an hour. Nobody sent Aurors for that. Aurors were sent when somebody at the Ministry was worried.

“Maybe he nicked something,” offered Crabbe.

“I have no doubt he’s committed a crime, Crabbe. That isn’t the interesting part.”

“Don’t stop on my account.”

Potter had opened the compartment door, walked in and sat down next to her in a single unbroken movement, as though he had been invited and was running slightly late. He had grown over the summer, which Lyra noted with disapproval, and his hair had not been cut, which she noted with no surprise whatsoever. He was already sitting beside her, one trainer up on the opposite seat, by the time she managed to say anything.

“P-Potter?”

“Chocolate Frogs. Lovely. Don’t mind me.”

He helped himself from the pile on the seat opposite. Lyra stared at him, and then at the gap in her sweets, and then at him again.

“Put that back!”

“It’s one frog, Malfoy. Has your dad stopped your pocket money?”

“*Stopped my*— Do you have any idea who you’re talking to? I could buy the trolley, Potter. I could buy the *train*!”

“Then you won’t miss one.” He tore the packet open and fished out the card. “Come on, come on… oh, not *again*.” It was Dumbledore. Potter looked at the card with the tragic air of a boy who had been given socks for Christmas. “That’s eleven of him. I could start a choir.”

He put the card in his pocket and, with no interest whatsoever in the chocolate, let the frog go. It made one heroic leap across the compartment and landed on Crabbe’s knee, and Crabbe screamed and flung it off, and it vanished under the seat with both Crabbe and Goyle scrabbling after it.

Potter watched them for a moment, grinning. Then, still watching them, he said in a quite different voice, low and ordinary, “Good summer? Talk to your dad?”

Lyra’s hands were folded in her lap. She kept them there.

“Extensively,” she said.

It came out perfectly smooth, the way the first one had in her father’s study, and she was aware of a small cold satisfaction at how easy it was getting. Potter looked round at her. She held his eyes without blinking, and after a moment he nodded, once, like a man ticking something off a list.

“Good,” he said.

Goyle surfaced from under the seat with the frog clamped between both hands and a smear of dust on her nose, and Potter was himself again at once, lounging back with his arms folded behind his head.

“Go on, then. You were saying something about me. I’d much rather hear it to my face; I’m better looking from the front.”

It was galling, but there was no better witness to Potter’s crimes than the criminal. Lyra swallowed her irritation, which was large and sharp-edged and went down badly.

“What on earth did you do,” she said, “to get arrested by Muggles?”

“Oh, that.” He seemed pleased she had asked. “There’s this woman, Aunt Marge. My uncle’s sister. She’s horrible. She’s horrible to me, and she’s horrible about my mum and dad, and she came to stay for a week, and by the last night I’d had enough of her. I wanted to hex her into next Tuesday, but you can’t do magic outside school, and I didn’t fancy getting expelled over Aunt Marge. So I didn’t. After dinner she fell asleep in the armchair with her mouth open, snoring, the way she does, and I got every packet of popping candy I’d been saving up and tipped the lot in, and then most of a bottle of Coke after it.”

Lyra waited for the part where something happened.

“What is popping candy?”

“Muggle sweets. It’s like sherbet, except it goes off in your mouth like tiny fireworks. And there’s this story every Muggle kid knows, that if you have it with Coke your stomach explodes.” His grin widened. “It doesn’t. But Aunt Marge knows the story as well. She woke up fizzing and choking and absolutely certain she was about to go off like a bomb, and she was screaming that I’d poisoned her, and she rang the police. They took me down the station in the back of a panda car.” He seemed to consider this the best part. “Better than a hex. Nobody can expel you for sweets.” He looked at her with interest. “You’d like it. It fights back. I’ll get you some one day.”

Lyra felt a small, disgraceful flicker of curiosity about popping candy, and put her foot on it.

“And then the Ministry turned up,” Potter went on. “Half of Magical Law Enforcement. Got me out of the police station before anyone could take my fingerprints.”

“Why the Ministry?”

“No idea. Some maniac’s got out of prison, they said. Not safe, they said.” He shrugged.

Maniac. Prison. Not safe. Lyra laid the pieces side by side and they fitted together with an almost audible click. The Ministry was very frightened about Potter’s safety indeed, frightened enough to send Aurors rather than a clerk, and there was only one maniac out of prison this summer.

Monday’s *Daily Prophet* had carried yet another piece on Sirius Black, the fifth or sixth since he had escaped from Azkaban at the end of July. Lyra had read it over breakfast while her father read the financial pages and pretended not to be reading over her shoulder. Black had killed thirteen people with a single curse, twelve Muggles and one wizard, in the middle of a street in broad daylight, and then he had stood among the bodies and laughed. He was the first prisoner in history to get out of Azkaban. Nobody knew how, and nobody knew where he was going.

And the Ministry had sent Aurors for Potter.

Knowledge was power. Lyra felt the truth of it now, settling in her chest like a good cup of tea. She leaned back, a good deal more comfortable than she had been a minute earlier.

“Sirius Black, you mean.”

“That’s the one.” Potter was unwrapping another Chocolate Frog, which he had not been offered.

Lyra watched him do it and felt something she had not expected to feel for Harry Potter, which was pity.

She knew what her father knew, or at least the parts he had let slip over port that summer. Sirius Black had been the Potters’ best friend, and he had sold them to the Dark Lord anyway. Her father had seemed to find that the only admirable thing about him.

Black was also, though nobody at the Manor ever said so, her mother’s cousin. His name had been burnt off the family tapestry long before Lyra was born, and her mother had a way of leaving the room whenever the *Prophet* printed his face. Lyra thought of that long look on the platform and wondered, for the first time, whether it had been about her at all.

And now Black was out, and the only Potter left was sitting in her compartment eating her sweets, with no idea that the man who had killed his parents was very probably coming to finish the job. If Black managed it, the boy keeping her family’s secret would be gone, and the sum Lyra had worked out at her desk in July would stop adding up.

She had no patience with stupidity. But even she could see there was something unbearable about a boy who nearly died every year and still walked about treating the world as a large and entertaining playground.

“Honestly, Potter,” she said, “ignorance must be very restful. You look positively serene.” She said it kindly, by her own standards. “If I were you, I’d make it my business to know Sirius Black better than he knows himself. Find out what he wants, and you’ll know where he’s going.”

It was good advice, freely given, and she did not give much of either. Potter snorted.

Lyra had just begun to frown when he reached over and mussed her hair.

He did it the way one might pat a pony, briskly and with approval, and he did it to hair that had taken twenty minutes and the combined efforts of her mother and a house-elf that morning. Lyra sat there with her hands in her lap and her mouth slightly open, and every part of her stopped to take offence at once.

“Don’t fret about me, miss,” said Potter, sounding rather proud of her. “I’m better informed than I look. Oh, and if a big black dog turns up, be nice to it. Feed it something. It’ll be starving.”

“What?”

“Bye.”

He was on his feet and through the door and gone, as quickly as he had arrived, and the door slid shut behind him with a small click of finality.

Lyra turned her head. Her neck needed oiling.

“POTTER!”

It was always like this. Potter went through a room like a draught through a house: every door banged, every paper went up in the air, and by the time anybody got up to shut the window he was three corridors away. And she had fallen for it *again*. Her hair was in ruins. Her tea was cold. She had given him advice, real advice, and he had patted her on the head for it.

“Hooligan,” she said. “Lout. Yob. Guttersnipe.”

“Malfoy?” Goyle held out a paper bag, as one might offer a bandage. “Fizzing Whizzbee?”

“I want,” said Lyra, “to know why the two of you sat there like a pair of hatstands while he *ate my frogs*.”

“What were we meant to do?” said Goyle, very reasonably, and Lyra had nothing to say to that. She was in no position to make threats herself, not with the diary sitting in his pocket or wherever he kept it; and what exactly were Crabbe and Goyle supposed to do?

*Who does he think he is?* she thought furiously, which was a stupid question, because everybody knew exactly who he was, and that was the whole trouble. She began, with shaking hands, to repair her hair.

She had just got the last pin back in when the train began to slow.

It was far too early for Hogsmeade. The rain that had been following them since Peterborough was lashing the windows, and outside there was nothing but dark moorland and darker sky. The carriage gave a shudder, the brakes shrieked, and all along the corridor doors slid open and voices rose, asking each other what was going on. Then the lamps went out.

The cold came a moment later. It did not come through the window or under the door. It came from inside the compartment itself. Something had gone out of the air, something Lyra had never noticed it contained until it was gone. The rain on the glass began to freeze into crooked white lines. Her breath smoked. And underneath the cold came something worse, a slow grey feeling that seeped into her like water into a boot, the certainty that nothing had ever been pleasant and nothing ever would be again.

Crabbe gave a small, strangled whimper. Goyle had both hands pressed to her mouth.

Lyra felt it too, every bit as much as they did. But she was a Malfoy, and she would sooner have frozen solid on that seat than let two girls from her own House see her shaking. She sat up very straight, laid her hands flat on her knees where nobody could see them tremble, and spoke in the tone her mother used for burst pipes.

“Dementors.”

“*What*?”

“Azkaban’s jailers. Father said the Ministry was lending them to the school to watch for Black. Nobody said they’d be checking tickets.” A tall shape glided past the door, a darkness in the shape of a cloak, and did not stop. “It isn’t us they want. Drink your tea.”

They did. The cold thinned, bit by bit, like a tide going out. Crabbe and Goyle were looking at her with enormous, grateful eyes, and Lyra had just picked up her teacup again, very casually, to show them how little the whole business had troubled her, when a shout came from further up the carriage.

It was Potter’s voice. It was not a word so much as a noise, raw and high, the kind of sound nobody made on purpose.

Then a flare of silver-white light in the corridor, and a girl’s voice, shrill with fright: “Harry! *Harry!*”

Granger. Potter’s Mudblood. Lyra was on her feet.

The compartment was four doors along. The lamps were flickering back on, and in their unsteady light she saw Weasley flattened against the window, white to the lips, and Granger on her knees, and Potter lying on the floor between the seats.

He was not moving. His face had gone the colour of candle wax, and his glasses were askew, and for one horrible, stupid moment Lyra thought of what Dementors did with their mouths, and then, *not him, he’s Potter*, as if being Potter had ever stopped anything from happening to him.

“He’s all right,” said a quiet voice. “He’s only fainted. They take some people like that.”

A man in extremely shabby robes was putting away his wand. They had been patched at both elbows and darned at the hem, and he looked as though he had not slept properly in a year, but he knelt beside Potter with the calm of someone who had done this sort of thing before.

Lyra found, to her annoyance, that her knees had gone slightly unreliable with relief. It was purely practical. If Potter died on a train, her father’s diary would end up in some Auror’s evidence box with nobody to explain it, and that would be extremely inconvenient.

The shabby man was already giving orders, in a mild voice that nevertheless expected to be obeyed. “You, young lady, would you go up to the front and tell the driver what’s happened? Thank you. And you’re Ron Weasley, aren’t you? Would you find the trolley and get him something hot to drink? Tea, plenty of sugar. He’ll want it when he wakes.”

“Uh,” said Weasley. “Right. It’s just…”

He trailed off. His ears had gone red. Lyra, standing in the doorway, knew exactly why, and it seemed a shame to waste it.

“He can’t,” she said, stepping inside. “Weasley couldn’t afford a cup of tea if you turned him upside down and shook him.”

“Oh, brilliant,” muttered Weasley. “Malfoy.”

Lyra raised her chin at him. “There’s a whole pot of it four doors down, Weasley, still hot. Say please, and I’ll consider it.”

Weasley looked at her, and then down at Potter, and visibly swallowed a great many things.

“Just watch him,” he said, and did not say please, and went.

Granger had already set off towards the front of the train with a last frightened look at Potter. Lyra sniffed at the empty doorway.

“I’ve had better manners from house-elves,” she informed it.

Which left her alone in the compartment with an unconscious Potter and the shabby man. He looked like a tramp who had wandered onto the wrong platform, but grown wizards did not ride the Hogwarts Express for fun, and there was only one post at the school that needed filling every single September.

“You’ll be Miss Malfoy, I think,” he said, glancing up at her. “The hair’s rather a giveaway.”

“Yes. And you’re the new Defence Against the Dark Arts teacher.”

He looked mildly surprised, and then mildly amused. “I am. Though not officially until tomorrow, so until then you may call me anything you like. Remus Lupin.” He straightened Potter out on the floor and drew his wand again. “I’m going to bring him round with *Enervate*. Would you hold his shoulders for me? People sometimes come up rather suddenly, and I’d rather he didn’t crack his head on the seat.”

Lyra looked at Potter, and at her new robes, and at the floor. “With my *hands*? He’s been on the *floor*.”

“The floor,” said Professor Lupin gently, “is where he is.”

She looked for an answer to that, and could not find one.

Lyra accepted the blow to her dignity, knelt in the dust of a stranger’s compartment, and pressed both hands down on Potter’s shoulders. His jumper smelled aggressively of some Muggle washing powder, the flowery kind that seemed to be shouting about how clean it was. Professor Lupin moved round to Potter’s head.

It was strange, seeing Potter with his mouth shut. With his eyes closed and his face still, he looked like one of the marble boys on old tombs, folded hands and all. It seemed monstrously unfair. The moment he opened either his eyes or his mouth, there was not a bigger villain in the whole of Hogwarts.

She had seen him with his mouth shut once before, before Hogwarts, in Madam Malkin’s: a skinny, silent boy on the next stool in clothes much too big for him, who hadn’t known what Quidditch was and had answered all her questions as if they might bite him. She had forgotten all about him before she reached the corner. Looking down at him now, she thought she might have preferred him that way. He had been almost sweet, in those days, when he still said nothing.

“*Enervate.*”

Potter came awake all at once, like a dropped spring, and nearly took her with him. She held on out of sheer outrage, and it was only because she held on that his head missed the edge of the seat.

Then he saw her. He stared at her from about a foot away as though she were the strangest thing that had happened to him all day, which, given his day, was saying something, and for a second something went across his face that she could have sworn was colour.

“What— what’s *Malfoy* doing here?”

“I came to see what was being murdered,” said Lyra. “It turned out to be you, making a noise like a peacock in a trap.”

Potter’s face could not decide what to be. It tried mortified, and furious, and baffled, and settled at last on a sort of thunderous offence that Lyra would have paid good money to have painted. She watched every bit of it. After two years, it was like being handed back something he had stolen from her.

She let go of his shoulders and sat down on the seat, arranging her robes. Potter stayed where he was on the floor, his back against the opposite seat, and pushed his glasses straight.

“Who was screaming?” he said.

Lyra stared at him. “Nobody was screaming, Potter. Except you.”

He looked at her for a moment as though she had answered in a foreign language. Then he looked away, at the black window with the frost still melting down it, and something went out of his face that Lyra did not have a name for. She had spent two years trying to find a way to make Potter look like that. Now that he did, it was not nearly as satisfying as she had imagined.

“Followed me onto a *train*,” he muttered, not to either of them. “Bloody…”

“Dear me, Potter.” Lyra leaned back. “All that swagger, and one Dementor puts you on the floor?” She let that sit for a moment, because it deserved to. “They’re obviously smitten. You aren’t even a convict, and they came all this way to kiss you. I’d watch that, if I were you. Keep this up and your first kiss will be with something that hasn’t got a face.”

Potter’s face screwed itself up. Lyra looked down at him from the seat and enjoyed every second of it.

She was opening her mouth to say something even more cutting when Potter moved.

One moment he was on the floor, and the next he had come up onto his knees and his face was very close to hers. For a second he did not move. He looked at her the way he had looked at the black window, as though he were working something out and did not much like the answer, and Lyra, who had never once in her life lost her place in the middle of an insult, lost it. Then something brushed across her mouth, so quickly and so lightly that for a second she genuinely did not understand what it had been.

Then she did.

Lyra made a noise she had never made before in her life and clapped her hand over her lips.

Potter got to his feet and dusted off the knees of his jeans. “First kiss with a Dementor?” he said. “Not any more.”

It had to be a dream. She would have given anything for it to be a dream. But her mouth was still tingling, very faintly, where it had happened, and dreams did not do that, and Lyra could not put three words in the right order.

“What— you— that— what did—”

“Wasn’t going to let a Dementor have my first one, either,” said Potter. He said it very lightly, and not quite to her; he was looking out at the dark again. His ears had gone scarlet. The Dementor, Lyra supposed, with the small part of her mind that was still working. “Call it charity. For somebody with a murderer after him.”

For several seconds Lyra could not speak. Then it all arrived at once.

“You— I have *never*— nobody has *ever*— you *thief*—”

“Louder,” said Potter. “The prefects’ carriage can’t hear you.”

“Shut up! Forget it! Forget all of it! *Obli*— *Obliviate!*”

“That’s not how you do it,” said Potter helpfully. “You need a wand. And you have to mean it.”

“*OBLIVIATE!*”

Professor Lupin, who had been kneeling on the floor with his wand still raised and his mouth slightly open, cleared his throat. Then he reached into his robes, took out an enormous slab of dark chocolate, and broke it in two with a loud crack.

“Eat that,” he said, holding out a piece to each of them. “Both of you. It helps.” He looked from one scarlet face to the other. “With the Dementors, I mean.”
