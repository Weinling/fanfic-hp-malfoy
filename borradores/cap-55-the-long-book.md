# Chapter 55: The Long Book

“You will have noticed,” said Abraxas Malfoy, “that I am not dead.”

“Yes, Grandfather.”

“Good. That saves a quarter of an hour.”

He poured the tea himself. He did it slowly and with a certain enjoyment, holding the pot rather higher than was necessary, and he did not spill a drop.

It was eleven o’clock on Boxing Day, and the east cottage was smaller by daylight. Draco had held out as far as the toast. He had come down to breakfast grey under the eyes, and looked at his egg, and said “I know who the gentleman is” in a small defiant voice; and their mother had gone on pouring the coffee, and their father had lowered the *Prophet* an inch and looked past him, at Lyra. He had not seemed angry. He had seemed, if anything, relieved, like a man who hears the bailiffs on the drive at last.

“He has asked for you,” her father had said. “Eleven o’clock. Don’t sit until you’re told, and don’t lie to him. He finds it tiring.”

“Sit,” said her grandfather.

She sat. The chair was very hard, and it had been put where the light from the window fell on her face. She knew that trick. She had seen it done across a study desk to a man from the Ministry when she was nine.

The chessmen were asleep on their board. The box had gone from the table, and the long book lay shut beside the sugar with its lock closed.

“How long?” said Lyra.

“Since the February of 1982. You were not quite two, and I am told that you bit the Healer.” He pushed a cup towards her with one finger. “The pox was real. They gave me a fortnight, and for once they were being optimistic. The Dark Lord had been gone three months. Every man who had ever dined with him was being asked about it on oath, by Barty Crouch, in a very small room. It was your father’s idea, I should say, and the best he ever had: nobody can serve a summons on a family vault. We had a nice quiet funeral, with the doors shut. I sent lilies.” He drank some of his tea. “Then I failed to die. I have been failing for fourteen years, in this chair, with a face like a suet pudding. It leaves one a good deal of time for correspondence.”

“You wrote the letter I took to Karkaroff.”

“I did. You carried it very well.”

“You’ve been running this family from a cottage.”

“Somebody had to. Your father is charming, and he reads a balance sheet like a man looking for his own name. Who did you think was paying for your Firebolt?”

She had a great many questions, in a great many sizes. She had lain awake sorting them. There was the one she wanted to ask, and beside it there was the one she was going to.

“You were a prefect in 1943,” said Lyra.

The cup stopped, a very little, on its way down to the saucer.

“I was in the trophy room in September,” she said. “On a detention. There’s a brass plate. *A. Malfoy*, between a Lestrange and a Mulciber. And on the shield above it, somebody called T. M. Riddle, for Special Services to the School. In the same year.” She kept her eyes on his. “You went pale last night. I have never seen anybody in this house go pale. And when you said *him*, you didn’t say it as my father says it.”

The old man looked at her for some time. The corner of his mouth went in.

“Your father,” he said, “has been in that room twenty times, and I don’t suppose he has ever read a plate that hadn’t got his name on it.” He put the cup down. “Tom Riddle sat beside me in Arithmancy for five years. He was the cleverest boy I ever met. He had one set of robes, and a charity grant, and a very pleasant manner, and he borrowed from me, in our fourth year, the price of a second-hand copy of Bathilda Bagshot. He paid it back. He always paid things back. I thought it the most promising thing about him.”

It was a moment before she understood what she had been told. It went on arriving after that, in pieces, like the news of an accident.

“Riddle,” said Lyra.

“You have the name now. There are perhaps thirty people living who do, and I shouldn’t care to be the thirty-first who said it at dinner. The father was a Muggle. I gather he is dead.” He looked at her over his long fingers with a mild, attentive interest, as one watches a piece of litmus paper. “Go on, child. Do say something. Your father took it very badly. He had to lie down.”

She thought of the sixth-years in the green lamplight, and the fathers on her list. She thought of what she had stood up and told a roomful of frightened children in June, about what they were and how old it was. She picked up her tea, because it was there, and it was perfectly steady. She had been learning that on Thursdays.

“What’s in the book?” said Lyra.

He laughed.

It was a dry small noise, and it seemed to take him by surprise. He laughed for about as long as it would take to turn a page, and then he reached across the sugar and laid one finger on the lock, and it opened. He did not open the book at the beginning. He let it fall where it wanted to.

“*Thos. Greengrass*,” he read, not looking at it. “*Two hundred in gold, for the Michaelmas rents, repayable on demand.* That’s 1688. His own hand, you see, on our paper; that is the only rule of form. One writes it down on one’s own paper. Then it’s true.”

She knew that one. Her father had said it, giving her a small green book on her eleventh birthday. She had thought he was being funny.

Thomas Greengrass’s name had a line through it, ruled very straight and very black.

“He died owing,” said Lyra.

“He did. In his bed, with a priest. And the book collected. Nobody ever did it; nobody pays a call. It simply collects when the debtor dies, and it keeps what it collects, and it gives nothing back. That is the whole of the law. It took the two hundred, and everything the two hundred had bought, and I understand that the Greengrasses have not held a card worth playing since.” He turned over a leaf with his nail. There were a great many leaves. “There’s a Selwyn who owed for a horse. There’s a bishop. You will have heard it said that nobody has ever died owing a Malfoy. People take it for a boast, and I used to take it for a joke. It is what the book is *for*.”

The fire settled. On the board, a small black pawn stirred in its sleep and muttered something about the Sicilian.

“Does he owe?” said Lyra.

Her grandfather closed the book. He did it without any hurry, and the lock clicked.

“There’s the question,” he said. “Well done. You came in by the side door after all.” He held up his right hand, between them, in the light from the window, with the fingers spread. It was an old man’s hand, all knuckle and vein, and it did not shake. “I’ll tell you what I told your father. On the night of the Third Task I sat at a table in the north with eleven men, and I had Tom Riddle on credit for fifty-two years. I had found him the first of his friends. I had kept his place for him for fourteen years, in the dark, with a face like a pudding, and I had built him the door he came back through. I fully expected to be thanked. And he got up, in his new body, and came round the table, and put his hand against my cheek. I felt the fever go out of me like water out of a bath. I picked up a cup. I had not been able to pick up a cup since before you could walk.”

He looked at the hand.

“Nobody has ever done me a greater injury,” said Abraxas Malfoy. “Do you understand why?”

She had stood in an Owlery once, with the door at her back, and been told the terms.

“He paid you,” said Lyra. “All of it, at once, in front of everybody. And now you owe him.”

“And what’s given can be taken.” His hand went down to the head of the cane, and rested there. “Your brother does as he’s asked. I sent him into that room at eleven years old, with a list, and he went. You would have wanted to know what it was for. That is why he’ll have the house, and the book, and you will not. I had an heir who asked what things were for. I was one. Look where it’s got us.”

“What was it for?”

“Drink your tea,” said her grandfather. “You’re too thin. I am going north tomorrow, with a box, to tell a man that somebody has been at his things, and I should like you to go on being alive when I get back, if only for the conversation.”

She walked back between the yews with her hands in her pockets.

It had turned very cold in the night, and the snow creaked. Her brother’s footprints were still there, going one way, with her own inside them. She got as far as the sundial before she let herself think about the box.

*Black*, she thought. *Not tarnished.*

She had seen that before. She had been twelve, in an empty classroom, and a boy had put a book down on a desk between them with a hole punched through it, and the paper round the wound had looked just like that, as if it had bled. *There aren’t many things that will break one of those*, he had said, in the straw, with the owls all round him, and she had been a great deal too angry to count.

There were seven. He had told her that when she was twelve. A diary, and a locket that a boy had died for in a cave. And a woman’s crown in a cathedral of junk on the seventh floor, that somebody had broken *a long time* ago, and left there, and said nothing about. Not on the third floor, with the moon on the lake. Not on the sixth, with her hair coming down. She had lain with her head on that boy’s shoulder in a room full of cups.

*Somebody in that castle knows what this is.*

She stood quite still beside the sundial. There was an odd high singing in her ears. She was so angry that she could have sat down in the snow, and under the anger, like a stair that was not there, she was more frightened than she had ever been. By tomorrow night that old man would be standing in front of the Dark Lord with the thing in his hands. And it was not a very long way, for somebody clever, from a black stain to a boy who had once been down in the Chamber.

The ring was in her pocket. It had been warm since breakfast. He would be calling; he always called at Christmas, whatever she had said to him, at about the time that Mrs Weasley stopped the singing. Her fingers had closed on it last night in the cottage, on the bare metal, without asking her. She did not know what colour that had sent him. She had only to take off her glove. *He knows. It’s you. Get out of that house and tell your Headmaster.*

And who had carried it down from the seventh floor, wrapped in his own scarf?

She went on standing there. A white peacock had come round the end of the hedge, with its tail dragging, and it looked at her with one flat eye. If anybody came asking who had warned Harry Potter, there was one child in the world who could say what was in that box and who had seen it opened. She had told a boy in June exactly which side of this she would be on, and how far.

That evening she took a handkerchief out of the drawer of her bedside table, very soft, folded small, and wrapped the ring in it, three times round, so that it should not touch her skin. She put it at the back of the drawer. She lay for a while looking at the drawer. It did not seem to make any difference to anything; she could feel where it was, as she had always been able to.

“You knew,” said Lyra.

Her mother was at her glass, with her hair half down. She had a thin gold chain about her neck that went in under her collar. She did not turn round.

“I have taken him his soup on Thursdays for fourteen years, darling,” said Narcissa Malfoy. “Who did you imagine it was for?”

“You told me that he was dead.”

“I told you that he had died of dragon pox behind closed doors. It was the best thing that could have been said of him, and it was very nearly true.” She picked up a pin, and looked at it. “There are men who come up the drive after dark. You have asked me nothing about them since you were six. I was so proud of you.”

There did not seem to be a great deal to say to that.

Her mother put in the pin. Behind her, on the dressing table, a list lay under a scent bottle, in her small upright hand, and Lyra read it upside down, as she had been taught. It was mostly linen. Halfway down it said *D.’s room—move next to mine*.

“Why?” said Lyra.

“The nursery chimney smokes.”

“It’s never smoked.”

Her mother looked at her at last, in the mirror, from a long way off.

“Go and play with your brother,” she said. “While it’s fine.”

So she did.

She could not have said why. She was fifteen, and a prefect, and she had two hundred lines of Arithmancy to do; and on the Thursday afternoon, with the old man gone north and the whole white lawn lying empty under a low red sun, she went out in her second-best cloak and found Draco behind the yews with his back to her, building something, and she put a snowball down his neck.

The noise he made brought a peacock out of the hedge.

He had their father’s eye and no patience. He threw too hard, and he threw at her face, and he could not be made to understand that one was supposed to stop and make the next one properly. He got her twice on the ear. She had four years and a Seeker’s arm, and she drove him the length of the lawn and back, and up on to the sundial, shrieking; and somewhere in the course of it his face stopped being grey. He had the bow tie on, under his coat. He had lost another tooth. He fell over backwards on purpose and lay there making a shape in the snow, out of breath, with his hair full of it, and laughed up at the sky.

“I’m going to tell Urquhart’s brother I beat you.”

“You did not beat me.”

“I got you on the *ear*.”

“I allowed that. It’s called a feint.”

“It’s called the *ear*.” He looked at her upside down. The laugh went out of his face, not all at once, and something smaller came and looked out of it. “Lyra. It wasn’t me, was it? He said it wasn’t. It was like that when I got it. It was already broken.”

“I know.”

“He looked so—” He stopped. “Is he cross?”

She sat down in the snow beside him, which was the end of the cloak. She put her hand on his head. It was wet, and very warm, and exactly the shape that it had always been.

“You did precisely what you were asked,” said Lyra. “Nobody could be cross with that. It was the most perfectly done thing I ever heard of. Nobody in the whole of the school could have found it but you.”

He considered this. It made its way across his face, slowly, like the sun coming up a wall.

“I know,” said Draco, and he hit her in the ear.

*Keep him so*, her father had written. She let him get away with it. She chased him all the way back to the garden door with the light going, and he was still telling her about the ear as they went in; and she was to think about that afternoon a great deal, later, and to wonder what she had done with it.

The old man came back on the Friday night, on foot, from the end of the drive.

She watched him from the window seat. It took him a long time. He had the cane, and he leaned on it, and he stopped twice between the yews; and he had no box. There was a lamp lit in the cottage until the morning. When Draco ran down after breakfast with the chessmen under his arm he found the door bolted, for the first time in his life, and he came back up the path very slowly and did not tell anybody.

On the Saturday, Pobble opened the south wing.

It had been shut since before Lyra was born. She had supposed that the roof was bad. She came along the gallery after lunch and found the door at the end standing wide, and a smell of cold and old lavender coming out of it, and Pobble on a stool with her ears flat, polishing the handle of a door that nobody had touched in fifteen years. There were dust sheets going past in the arms of two elves she did not know. There was a very great deal of silver on the floor.

“What’s all this, Pobble?”

The elf looked at the door handle. Her hands had not stopped. She was shaking so hard that the cloth made a small quick noise against the brass.

“Pobble is not to say, miss,” she whispered. “Master says the rooms with the most light.”

Her father was in the study. She found him standing in front of the three portraits and the nail, with his hands behind him, gripping one another so that the knuckles stood up white. He turned round when she came in, and he was smiling, the wide delighted smile from the platform. It had got stuck. It stayed where it was while the rest of his face looked at her.

“My treasure,” said Lucius Malfoy. “There you are. We shall want the blue, I think, tomorrow. Or not the blue; your mother will know. He is coming to dinner.”

For a moment she could hear the clock.

“Here,” said Lyra.

“Tomorrow night. Just the family. He asked for it particularly.” He came across the room, and took her face in both his hands, and kissed her forehead; and his lips were dry, and his hands were cold, and they held on a little longer than they generally did. “He asked for you by name.”

Her mother was on the stairs. She stopped as Lyra went by, and reached out, and took a thread off her shoulder that was not there. She let it fall over the banister. She did not look at it go.

“Whatever he asks you to do,” said Narcissa Malfoy, very quietly, “do it the first time.”

She went on up.

That night Lyra sat on the end of her bed in the dark, with her hands in her lap, and furnished a room.

She did it with great care. She had never needed so much. A breakfast table, with the light coming in sideways. A dress for Christmas. A boy she had quarrelled with. She looked for the best thing that she had, the thing that would look most like what he expected and would bear the most handling, and she found it, and she carried it in, and set it down in the middle of the floor: Draco on a stool, with the Hat over his eyes and his feet not reaching the ground, and the table going up round her in a roar.

It looked very well there. It was the only thing in the room that was alive.

In the drawer beside her knee, three times round in a handkerchief, something was still faintly warm.
