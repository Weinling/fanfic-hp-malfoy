# Chapter 35: Spectacle

“It screams,” said Potter. “That’s all it does. I’ve had it open in the dormitory, I’ve had it open in the common room, I’ve had it open in an empty classroom with the door shut, and it screams. Seamus says if I open it again he’s going to drop it out of the window.”

“Then don’t open it in the dormitory.”

“Thanks. Very helpful.”

They were in the library, on the Saturday afternoon, a week before the second task, at a table at the back where the shelves of Magical Law came down to meet them and nobody ever came. The golden egg sat on the table between them, shut, shining softly, like an ornament off a very expensive mantelpiece. He had brought it with him under his arm, everywhere, for a fortnight. It had begun to look, Lyra thought, rather like a pet.

“You haven’t worked it out,” she said.

“No.”

She had heard it once. Everybody in the castle had heard it once, the night after the dragon, when he opened it in the middle of the Gryffindor celebrations and the noise came all the way down the Great Hall: a dreadful, sawing, shrieking wail, like a dozen kettles being murdered. She had sat at the Slytherin table with her hands over her ears like everybody else and thought, quite clearly, *I have heard something like that before*; and it had taken her a week of going through the back shelves in her free periods to remember where.

She had not heard it. She had read it.

She took a book out of her bag and opened it at the place she had marked, and turned it round, and pushed it across the table to him with one finger, as Granger had pushed her the Horntails.

*The language of the merpeople is beautiful and musical below the surface, but to the ear above water it is almost wholly unpleasant, and has been compared by more than one unfortunate listener to a shriek.*

Potter read it. He read it again. He did not ask her where she had found it, or how, or whether she was sure; he shut the book carefully, with his hand flat on the cover, and sat looking at the hand.

“There are merpeople in the lake,” said Lyra, in case he had not got there.

“I know,” said Potter. He looked up, and he was grinning. “Lyra. That’s brilliant.”

“I know,” said Lyra. “Get your cloak.”

The lake was grey and still, and frozen at the edges in thin plates that cracked if you looked at them. Nobody was down there but the Durmstrang ship, shut up and silent on the water, with smoke going up from one funnel. They went round past the end of the shingle to the rocks below the beech trees, where the Durmstrang boys had dived in November, shouting about the cold; and Potter lay down on his front on the flattest rock, with the egg in both hands, and looked over the edge into the water.

“If I fall in,” he said, “I want it known that this was your idea.”

“You won’t fall in.”

She knelt down beside him on the rock, which was so cold it went straight through her robes, and took hold of the back of his collar with both hands. She took hold of it properly, his robes and the shirt underneath and a good deal of the jumper, and wound it round her fist, and braced her knees.

“Go on.”

He took a breath, and put his head and both arms under the water, and opened the egg.

The shriek did not come. There was nothing but the lake round his head, and the cold, and his shoulders gone rigid under her hands with it; and then, very faintly, from somewhere under the surface, so faint she might have imagined it, she thought she heard something that was not a shriek at all. It went on for some time. She did not let go of his collar. She knelt on the frozen rock with the wind off the lake in her face and both fists wound into his robes, and held on, and counted, and when she got to thirty and he had not moved she pulled.

He came up gasping, with the egg shut in his hands and water pouring off his hair and his glasses gone sideways.

“Well?”

“Hang on—” He coughed, and shook his head like a dog, which she did not appreciate, since most of it went on her. “There’s a song. Singing. Under the water, it’s singing.” He shut his eyes, to get it right. “*Come seek us where our voices sound.* Something about the surface— we can’t sing above it. *An hour long you’ll have to look, to recover what we took.*”

“What you’ll sorely miss,” said Lyra.

He opened his eyes.

“It was the next line,” said Lyra. “I heard it. Just. Through the water.” She sat back on her heels, and let go of his collar at last, and found that her fingers had gone white. “The lake, then. An hour. And they’re going to take something of yours.”

*One of you dies*, said Moody, somewhere at the back of her head, *you or Potter*. She did not say it. She had not said any of it, in three weeks; and she sat on the frozen rock with the words in her mouth and let them stay there, and it was like holding a coal.

Potter rolled over on to his back on the rock and lay there with the egg on his chest and the water running out of his hair into his ears, looking up at the sky.

“I can’t swim, you know,” he said.

He said it to the sky, not to her, in an ordinary voice.

“Don’t be absurd. Everybody can swim.”

“I can’t. Never learned.” He was quiet a moment. “My aunt and uncle used to go to the seaside. Every summer, for a week. My cousin had lessons, at the pool, Saturdays. I didn’t go.”

“Where did you go?”

“Nowhere. I stayed in.” He turned his head on the rock, and looked at the frozen edge of the lake. “There’s a cupboard under the stairs. At theirs. That’s where I slept, till I was eleven. They’d lock it when they went to the seaside, so I wouldn’t touch anything.”

She had known that he had nobody at Christmas. She had known that his letters in the summer came by birds she had never seen before, and that he signed them with an ugly name, and that he never went home at Easter. She had not known this. She sat on her heels on the frozen rock and looked at him lying there, the most famous boy in Britain, with lake-water in his ears, telling the sky about a cupboard; and her hands had shut into fists on her knees before she knew it.

“I shall curse them,” said Lyra. “Both of them. And the cousin.”

“Don’t.”

“I shall. I know some very good ones. My father taught me one for boils that he says the Ministry has never been able to prove—”

“Don’t,” said Potter again, and sat up. He was not smiling. “He’s still my uncle.”

She stared at him.

He looked back at her steadily, with his hair in his eyes and the egg in his lap. Whatever it was, he had decided it a long time ago, and he was not going to explain it to her, because it was his.

Lyra unwound her scarf from her neck.

It was the cashmere one, grey, the one his owl had once tried to eat in the Owlery, which had cost a great deal more than anything he was wearing. She pulled it off, and put it over his head, and dried his hair with it, hard, rather harder than was necessary, as if his hair had personally offended her. He let her. He sat on the rock with his eyes shut and his head bowed and let her do it, and when she had finished, and his hair was standing up in every direction like a hedgehog’s, she wound the scarf twice round his neck, and tied it, and left it there.

“You’ll catch your death,” said Lyra, “and then where will I be.”
