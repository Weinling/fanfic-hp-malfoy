# Chapter 69: The Date

“You keep leaving,” said Diggory.

Harry lowered his wand, and the Shield went out with a small flat pop. He had held it for about four seconds.

It was the third Thursday in April, in the long room behind the kitchens, and the smell of somebody’s bread was coming through the wall. Umbridge had been gone three weeks. Nobody had to come in by twos any more, and the room was full. Down at the far end Neville had the third-years and the little ones in a row along the white line, with their wands up and their tongues out. He had been doing that since February. Harry had started spending his Tuesdays on a stone floor, and he had come back one Thursday to find a corner of the room that did what Neville told it, in a whisper, without either side having apparently noticed.

“I’m here,” said Harry.

“You’re not. You put it up faster than anybody I’ve ever seen, and then you’re out from behind it, looking for something to hit.” Diggory came round with his wand behind his ear and looked at Harry’s feet. He took Harry’s left elbow in two fingers and moved it an inch, as you might move a vase. “It isn’t a door, Potter. You don’t open it and go through. You stay behind it. That’s all it is. It’s the most boring thing you’ll ever do, and you go on doing it while somebody else does the interesting part. Again.”

Harry put it up. He had learnt it twice, both times at fourteen, both times for a maze, and nobody had mentioned that part. He stood behind it with his feet where they had been put, and every inch of him wanted to be somewhere else. Five seconds. Six.

“Hopkins does twelve,” said Diggory. “Left-handed.”

“Sorry,” said Hopkins, going past.

The door at the kitchen end opened, and Hermione came in backwards. She had flour to the elbow, and she was saying to somebody behind her that it was not a question of whether they *liked* it.

“They like it,” said a slow voice from the passage.

“That’s what’s *wrong!*” said Hermione. A large hand came round the door with a bun in it. She looked at the bun. She took it, with dignity, and shut the door, and saw them all looking. “It’s a very complicated position,” said Hermione.

Diggory took the wand from behind his ear.

“Right,” he said to the room, without raising his voice. “Mr Potter’s going to show you something. He’s been meaning to since November.”

It went quiet from the far end first. Harry turned round. He could see Ron with his arms folded, and Ginny, who had stopped not trying at about Easter. He could see a very small boy in a bow tie, by himself against the wall. He had not said a word in this room since Christmas, and he had gone on coming twice a week, and he looked up now with his pale tired face.

Harry had known for some weeks that it couldn’t be the hand. He put up his wand and went along the passage in his head and took hold of a short-legged table with a book under one leg. A ceiling that said *THREE*. Somebody bawling “Mate!” in his ear, with no idea why.

“*Expecto Patronum*,” said Harry.

It came out at a trot. It went up the white line with its brush straight out behind it, silver and in no hurry, and the whole room backed away against the walls with its mouth open. It stopped in the middle of the boards. It looked round at all of them, one after another. Then it sat down with its chin up.

Nobody said anything. Neville’s third-years had stopped breathing.

“It looks as if you’ve spilt something,” said a very small voice.

Harry’s heart gave one hard knock. Draco Malfoy had come away from the wall. He was looking at the fox with his head on one side, and no expression on his face at all. He put out one finger towards its ear; it looked at him down its nose, and he took the finger back. Ron, for some reason, had started to cough.

The fox went out. Diggory stood looking at the place where it had been, for rather longer than it deserved.

“I get fog,” he said. He looked at Harry. “Don’t look pleased with yourself. Feet.”

Harry looked down. His left foot had wandered off. He put it back, and he put up his wand, and he stood there behind nothing very much, doing the most boring thing he would ever do, with thirty people going back to work around him.

“Nine,” said Diggory at last, as if it had been got out of him with pliers, and he went away to see what Longbottom was doing to the third-years.

There was no chip shop in Hogsmeade, so Hermione had looked into it.

It turned out that an old wizard from Grimsby had taken the shed behind Zonko’s over the Easter holidays, and was frying things in it on Saturdays with a cauldron and a bad-tempered Heating Charm, and that you could get a pennyworth of chips in a cone of the *Daily Prophet* for a Knut and a half if you did not mind what was on the page. She told them this at breakfast on the third Saturday in April, in the tone of somebody reading out the minutes. Harry put down his fork. He looked at her across the toast, and she looked back at him, with a small straight face and her chin on her hand, and turned over a piece of parchment; and that was how it came about that at half past twelve that day, in the first warm sun of the year, the three of them were sitting in a row on the low wall behind the post office, with their legs hanging down, eating chips.

That was all it was. He wanted that understood. Nobody was chasing anybody. There was no Horcrux in it, or a plan, or anyone’s life at the end of it, and they had not come out here for any reason in the world. Ron had got salt on his prefect’s badge. He was explaining something about the Wimbourne Wasps, with a chip in each hand, to somebody who was not listening; he had let in three against Hufflepuff in February, and he had been growing ever since, about a foot in the shoulders; it had stopped mattering to him what the Slytherins sang. Hermione had brought a small bottle of vinegar from the Three Broomsticks and was holding it out of his reach. There was a smell of wet stone drying and hot fat. A very old witch went by on the lane with a very old cat, and neither of them looked at anybody. Up above the roofs the hills had gone a thin bright green, the snow had shrunk to one long dirty rag along the top of the ridge, and he sat in the middle of it with the paper getting warm and transparent in his hand and thought, with a kind of slow astonishment, that he had been looking forward to this for eleven years, in two lives, without ever once having known what it was.

“You’ve gone funny,” said Ron.

“What?”

“You’re smiling at a chip.”

“It’s a good chip.”

“It’s all right,” said Ron, considering it. He ate it. He looked sideways at Harry, in a way that he had, with his ears going a little pink; and then he looked straight ahead of him, at the wall of the post office, and he said to it, gruffly, with his mouth full, “You’ve been all right lately. You know. Better. I don’t know what it is. I thought you were going to go off your head, in the autumn, with that Umbridge, and your hand, and you never used to say anything. I said to Hermione. Anyway.” He went redder. “Good. That’s all. Budge up, you’re on my foot.”

Harry budged up.

He looked down at the paper in his hand. It had gone rather blurred, and he thought it was probably the vinegar. Somebody had reached past him; Hermione had leaned across without looking, with her eyes on her book, and tipped the bottle over Ron’s cone. Ron held it out for her at the right angle, without looking either, and went on talking about the Wasps. They had the whole of that movement by heart. They had no idea that they were doing it. There had been a time, and it was not so very long ago, when he would have gone over that as you go over a sore tooth, and gone up to his dormitory and done a sum. He watched them. It was still there. He thought it would probably always be there. It was just that today it seemed to be a thing that was going on, on a wall, in the sun, between two people he was extremely fond of, and it did not seem to have much to do with him.

A big black dog had come up the lane and sat down on his foot. It looked at the chip with terrible sincerity. He gave it the chip.

“—so there we are, the four of us, at two in the morning, on the Astronomy Tower,” said Sirius, “in our pyjamas, except for Peter, who’d got a hat—”

“I don’t think I want to know this.”

“You do, you know. It’s educational. Your father had a Nimbus handle in one hand and a live goose in the other, and he turned to me, perfectly calm, and he said—”

They had the room over the bar at the Hog’s Head, with the dirty window and the smell of goat. Sirius had his boots on the table. He had got thinner in the face since the summer and a good deal louder. There was grey at the sides of his hair, and he wore a Muggle waistcoat that he had found somewhere, in a violent shade of plum, with the buttons done up wrong. He had been a person in a kitchen since the break-out, on Moody’s orders, and it had done things to him. They had put something on his window. But he had got out, on the strength of a sick aunt of Moody’s, for the whole of a Saturday. He had walked up the lane on four legs and eaten eleven chips. He was as happy now, leaning back with a glass of Aberforth’s terrible firewhisky, with his godson sitting across from him, as Harry had seen him since a night by a lake.

He told the story about the goose. He did all the voices. It was extremely funny, and it had a really dreadful ending, and Harry put his head on his arms on the table and laughed until there was a pain in his side.

He had always known that it would come to this. He had thought about it, on and off, since he was thirteen. There would be a day, somewhere in a room with a dirty window, when he stopped being on the wrong end of it; when he could sit and listen to Sirius Black talking about a dead man, and it would just be a good story about somebody he would have liked. He had thought it might feel like winning.

“You’ve got a girl,” said Sirius.

Harry lifted his head.

“Don’t look at me like that. I’ve been waiting since New Year. Nobody flies to Slough in a blizzard for a Horcrux.” He was grinning, with his long yellow teeth and his eyes shining. “I said to Remus. I said, he’s got the look. James went round for an entire term with that look, it was like living with a very happy cow. Is she nice?”

Harry thought about it. He thought about a girl on a terrace, with a spoon in her hand.

“No,” he said.

“*Good*,” said Sirius, and he banged his glass down on the table, in pure delight. “Good. Nice is for people with something to fall back on. Does she hit you?”

“With a ruler. Once.”

“Oh, I like her. Bring her to Grimmauld. We’ll have a party. I’ll get out the good elf-heads. I’m going to be a disgraceful old man, Harry, I’ve decided. I’m going to live to be a hundred and thirty, in a house with too many stairs, and embarrass your children at weddings. Lily would have—” He stopped, and laughed, and shook his head. He had taken a pickled onion out of the dish. He looked about him on the table, with his eyebrows up, and he held out one long thin hand, with the fingers waggling, not looking. “Pass the salt, Prongs.”

The window rattled a little. Somebody was shouting at a goat, down in the yard.

It was the eighteenth of June. He had known that since he was eleven. It was a Tuesday this year. He had worked it out once on the back of a Charms essay, and he had worked it out again that morning, while he was cleaning his teeth, as he had every morning for some time. He knew how long it was to the day. There had been a stone arch, on a raised platform, in a big dim room like a pit, and a tattered black curtain that stirred when there was no wind. Sirius had been laughing. That was what he could never get past. She had got him in the chest, and he’d had a look on his face of surprise, and he had still been laughing at something when he went backwards through the veil, in no hurry, like somebody going home.

Fifty-nine days.

Harry picked up the salt. He held it for a moment; it was a dented pewter thing, and it was rather sticky. He put it into the long hand, and he felt the fingers close on it, warm and dry and alive.

“There you go,” said Harry.

He told her that night.

He had not exactly planned to. It was not on the timetable; he had found the door by himself and got the fire going, and she had come in at about ten, with her book of rounds, looking thin and tired and extremely clean. She had got very neat since Easter. He did not know how to say it. Everything about her had been put away. She kissed him, on the way past, on the corner of the mouth. She sat down in the chair with her feet under her, and asked what he had done with his day, and he opened his mouth to tell her about the chips. He told her about the chips. He told her what Ron had said, and about the bottle of vinegar, and she laughed at that, in the right place, and said Weasley had the emotional range of a Quaffle; and he told her about the goose. Then he stopped. He was sitting on the rug, with his back against her shins. He found he was looking at the fire, and that he could not go on.

“Lyra,” he said.

He felt her go still. It was a very small thing. He did not call her that, as a rule; not in here; it had got to be a thing he kept for when something had gone wrong.

“There’s something I’ve got to tell you. You said I had to. On the hill. You said tell you things first.” He took his glasses off, and held them. It seemed to be easier without being able to see. “I’ve known this since I was eleven. I’ve never told anybody. Not Dumbledore, not the way it was. I’ve got it worked out. Sirius dies on the eighteenth of June.”

The fire ticked.

He told it to the hearth. He told it badly. He had never said it, not to a living soul, not in that order, and it came out flat and small and took no time at all. There would be a dream, a false one, sent on purpose, and he had fallen for it, at fifteen, in the middle of the History of Magic exam. He had dragged five people with him to the Ministry of Magic, to the room with the arch, after a thing that wasn’t there. It was a trap. It had been for him. Sirius had come after him, with the rest of them. He’d been so pleased; he had been shut up for a year. He had been fighting his cousin on the steps, and laughing. Harry had seen it coming, he had been looking straight at it, and it had taken about as long as it takes to breathe in. He had gone after her. Somebody had held him round the chest. He’d kept shouting for quite a long time that Sirius was only behind the curtain, that you could get him back. He had told Remus Lupin to let go of him. He had been so sure.

“That was my fault,” said Harry. “That one was all mine. He’d told me not to go, and I went. I thought I knew what I was doing. Everyone’s been telling me for years that it wasn’t, and it was.”

There was a silence. He waited. He was aware of her knees against his back, and that they were not moving.

“How long have you been counting?” said Lyra.

It was very quiet. It did not sound like her.

“Every day. Since I got here. I was going to—I thought I’d have thought of something by now. I had it all worked out. I’d know exactly what he was going to do, I’d know the day, I’d stop the dream. Only it’s not going to be the same. It’s all different now. He’s not in the Ministry, he’s in your dining room. I don’t know what it’ll be, I don’t know how it’ll come. I went to see Sirius today, and he asked me for the salt.” His voice had started to do something, and he stopped it, and it went on. “He’s got a waistcoat. He’s going to live to be a hundred and thirty. He wants to meet you. I sat there, and he told me about a goose. He’s the only one I’ve got, Lyra. I’ve got fifty-nine days. I passed him the *salt*—”

It had got into his throat. He tried to swallow it. He had put his hand over his eyes, with his glasses in it. His shoulders had started, and he could not do anything about them. It was not like the time with Hermione. That had just run out of him, like water, with nothing to do with anybody. This hurt. It came up from the bottom in lumps, and there was noise in it, and he hated that; he had been doing this by himself for four years, in dormitories, with a pillow.

She did not say it was all right.

He heard the chair creak. She got down. She came round in front of him and knelt on the bald rug, in her neat robes, and took his wrist in her cold fingers and pulled his hand down, with the glasses in it. He could not see her face. It was a white blur with a good deal of pale hair. She took the glasses away from him and put them on the fender. She did not turn her back. She did not look at the wall, or go and rub anything out. She stayed where she was, about a foot away, and looked at him, while it went on. He had not known that you could be looked at like that and not die of it. It seemed to go on for a long time.

Then she took out a handkerchief. It was very old, and soft as silk, and he knew it. He had not seen it since he put it down on a stair, at thirteen. She wiped his face with it, hard, as you’d wipe a child’s, and she held it to his nose.

“Blow,” said Lyra.

He blew.

“He is my mother’s cousin,” she said. She folded the handkerchief over, and looked at it, and put it into her sleeve. Her voice was perfectly level, and something had come into it that he had not heard since a hill. He could see her better now. She had some colour at last. It was the first time since Easter that she had looked like somebody who was at home. “He’s a disgraceful person in a plum waistcoat, and I’ve given him a chicken leg and a bar of chocolate, and a sugar mouse, for which I have never been paid. He owes me. Do you imagine that I’m going to let him be dropped through a curtain by my *aunt?*” Her chin had come up. She looked past him at the fire, with her eyes narrowed, as she looked at the Snitch; and he knew that look, he had seen it above a pitch and across a chessboard, and he had never in his whole life been so glad of anything. “Fifty-nine days. In a room in the Ministry, with one arch. No. We shall not have that.”

“Lyra, you can’t—”

“We shall *not* have that,” said Lyra Malfoy. “Do be quiet. I’m thinking.”

He put his forehead down on her shoulder. He had not meant to. He was so tired that he could hardly hold it up. She let him. After a moment he felt her hand come up, and rest on the back of his neck, rather as you might rest your hand on a dog that you had decided to keep. He breathed in. She smelt of soap. He had carried that thing for four years and three months, up every staircase in the castle, and he had never once in all that time set it down, and now there was somebody else’s hand on it. He didn’t know what he had thought that would be like. He had not thought it would feel like that. It felt like being able to stop walking.

He woke at some time after two, with a stiff neck, on the rug. She had gone. She had put the blanket from the chair over him, and left a note on the fender, in her beautiful hand. *You snore. Bring me a list of everybody who was in that room. Not on paper.* And underneath, smaller, as if she had come back to it: *Eat something.*

He lay and looked at the embers.

He felt better than he had felt since he was eleven. He felt as if somebody had taken a great stone off his chest. That was just what he wanted to think about, and he lay there thinking about it. She would do something. He knew that. He had seen it in her face. She was the cleverest person he had ever met, and she was going to go away and think, and write to somebody, in that hand, and it would be something that he had not thought of.

It was only that she wrote to her father every Sunday.

He looked at that for a while. He had not put it there. It seemed to have come in by itself. They sat down together and they wrote it, of course; he had been through every word; there had been a very good thing about a hot-water bottle. They wanted to know what he was afraid of. She’d told him so. It was the whole of the arrangement: he told her what to say. He trusted her. He trusted her more than he had ever trusted anybody, in either of his lives, and that was not what this was. She had been so thin at Easter. There was a man who lived in her house, and he looked into people as you look into a cupboard. *He has one room with something living in it*, somebody said, in a dry voice. *He reads signs.* It didn’t have to be anybody’s fault. Whatever she knew, he could get at. She had been sitting at that table, and he had gone in and turned over everything that she had, and put it back. If she didn’t know a thing, then nobody could get it out of her. He had told her the date. He had told her an hour ago, on this rug, with his glasses off. He looked at that, and then he stopped looking at it. It was just common sense. It was what you’d do. It was what you would do for anybody that you wanted to keep safe.

*Nothing moves without asking*, she said, on a hill, with her teeth chattering, and he said *All right*.

He would tell her afterwards. When it was done. It would only be for a few weeks.

He got up, stiffly, and put the blanket back on the chair. His bag was by the door. He dug about in the bottom of it, among the broken quills and the Chocolate Frog cards; and he got out Dudley’s road atlas, with the coffee ring on Hampshire, and he knelt down by the last of the fire and began to look for somewhere with no roads on it.
