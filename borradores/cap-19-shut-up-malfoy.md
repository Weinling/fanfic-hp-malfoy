# Chapter 19: Shut Up, Malfoy

“Two hundred and seventy,” somebody sang, as Lyra turned into the corridor below the Charms classroom. “Two hundred and *seventy*—”

There were three of them, Hufflepuff third-years, crouched in a row against the wall like children round a rabbit hole, and they had plainly been waiting four days for exactly this. One of them was a boy she had once made cry in first year over a Chocolate Frog card, and he looked as if he had been saving it up ever since.

It was the best thing that had happened to their House in living memory, and Lyra could hardly blame them for it. That did not mean she had to listen. She stopped in front of them and waited, and one by one they got up off their heels and found the wall behind them.

“Do go on,” she said. “It’s a very good song. It has one number in it, and you’ve learned it.” She looked past them at the thing on the flagstones: a little wire trap with a square of treacle tart in it, just big enough for one rat. “Diggory won that match. You three were in the stand, eating toffee. And this is how you celebrate? Crouching in a corridor, chasing vermin? Have you thought about ferrets? You’d make natural ones. You’ve got the crouch already.”

“It’s not ours!” said the boy. “Somebody just left it—”

Behind them, something grey shot out from the skirting board where they had cornered it, past the trap and past Lyra’s shoe and away down the corridor. All three Hufflepuffs said “*Aw*.”

“Oi! That’s *mine*—”

Weasley came pounding round the corner, red in the face, and skidded to a halt when he saw who it was.

“Don’t touch it, it’s set. Was there a— Oh, *great*. Malfoy.”

“Weasley. Did you make this yourself? It looks it.”

“My dad showed me, actually. He’s good with Muggle stuff.” He was already on his knees by the trap. “Was there a rat? Did you lot see it? Which way?”

The Hufflepuffs looked at one another, and at Lyra, and said nothing useful. Lyra sighed.

“Grey. Or brown. Rat-coloured. It went that way, extremely fast. I didn’t stop it to ask its name.”

“Did it have all its toes? Scabbers is all skinny, and he’s got a toe missing on one of his front feet—”

“I did not count its *toes*, Weasley.”

“Well, it matters! If it’s Scabbers, then Harry and Hermione—” He stopped, and scratched his head. “Never mind.” He turned to the Hufflepuffs. “Cheers. Look, if you see a brown rat, come and find me, will you? Gryffindor table. And leave the trap alone. And if there’s ever one in it, don’t *kill* it.”

The Hufflepuffs said they would, and fled. Weasley set the trap again, very carefully.

“I thought your rat was dead,” said Lyra. “The whole school heard you say so. At length.”

“Yeah, well. I thought so too.” He sat back on his heels. “He’s old, he’s never been any good on his own. Only Harry and Hermione won’t have it. They’ve been out looking for him every day for weeks, and they *mean* it. I can’t just sit in the common room while they do that, can I?”

So that was what Potter and Granger did together every afternoon, round corners and up staircases and into lavatories nobody used. They were looking for Weasley’s *rat*.

“Every day. For a *rat*.”

“For *my* rat.” He dug in his pocket and brought out a square of newsprint gone soft with folding: the photograph from the *Daily Prophet* in the summer, the whole Weasley family waving in front of a pyramid, and on the shoulder of the youngest boy an old rat, asleep. “Look at him. He’s not much. Sleeps all day, bites if you wake him. He was Percy’s first. But he’s ours, and they know that, and that’s why they’re looking.”

“A new owl would be cheaper than all this. Even your family could run to an owl.”

“Wouldn’t be *him*, though, would it?”

“It would be an owl. With all its toes.”

She waited for him to go red. He only shrugged.

“Suppose. I hated him when Percy handed him on. Thought it was the worst present anyone ever got. And now I go round setting traps for him.” He looked at the photograph. “Funny, that.”

It was very deflating. Weasley had spent two and a half years with Potter, and it seemed to have worn something smooth in him; insults went off him like rain off a slate. She could have called him a blood traitor, and he would only have made a face.

“How dull you’ve become,” said Lyra, and turned to go.

“You could try being less foul and making it up with Harry,” Weasley called after her. “He’s looked like death all week.”

Like death. Lyra walked on. Potter had pushed away the hand she held out to him, and used her own brother to do it, and gone off with Granger after a rat; and *he* looked like death.

She and Potter had been at war since the first week of first year, and the whole school had watched it: hexes in corridors, points lost in Potions, a Snitch caught with a broken arm. Then a diary on a classroom desk, and everything after, and somewhere in all of it the war had turned into something she could not have drawn a map of. She did not know where the border had moved. She only knew she was on the wrong side of it.

There was a word for it. Weasley would have used it about his rat.

“Attached,” said Lyra, under her breath. “Honestly.”

A witch in a ruff, in a portrait by the stairs, said “*Ha*,” and went back to her embroidery.

Snape had asked her on Monday, after Potions, in the voice he kept for things that were not quite orders. He had business at the Ministry on Wednesday and would not be back till late. Professor Lupin was unwell, as he was unwell every month at about this time, and there was a draught he must have before moonrise. It would be ready on the desk. Would she see that he got it?

It was the first thing Snape had asked of her since November. She owed him, and she knew it, and she had said yes before he finished.

Snape’s office was cold, and smelled of the things in the jars. Lyra went between the shelves without touching anything, to the desk, where a small cauldron sat over a low flame and a pewter goblet stood ready beside it. The potion was done. It gave off a thin, bluish smoke and a smell like boiled stockings.

She was filling the goblet when she saw the marks.

There was a dried grey splash on the desk beside the cauldron, where some had boiled over, and across it went a line of tiny prints, off towards the edge of the desk. They went up the side of the cauldron, and then they came down it again, damp and in a hurry.

A rat. It would be, in this castle, this week.

Lyra looked at the goblet, and at the window, where the light was already going. She could not have brewed another if she had had all night, and she did not have all night; she had until moonrise. A rat could not have drunk much. Whoever drank it need never know. She put the lid on, wiped the bottom of the goblet on Snape’s blotter, and went.

Professor Lupin opened his door looking worse than she had ever seen him, grey to the lips, as though somebody had drawn him in pencil and then rubbed most of it out.

“Miss Malfoy. Is that from Severus? Thank you. Come in a moment, out of the draught.”

Lyra came in, and left the door open behind her.

There was an old sheet of parchment spread on his desk, covered in fine lines like the plan of a building, and his eyes kept going back to it while he took the goblet from her. He lifted the lid and drank, and grimaced.

“Vile. One of these days somebody will invent a version that tastes of anything else, and I shall marry them.” He set the goblet down half empty. “Tell Professor Snape I’m grateful. He’ll hate that.”

Lyra watched him drink it, all of it, and thought of the line of little prints on Snape’s desk, and said nothing.

“You’re a werewolf, sir.”

Lupin lowered the goblet.

“Severus told you.”

“Not in so many words. He set the essay.”

“Yes. He would have.” He put the goblet down. “When?”

“November. The thirteenth.”

He looked at her for a long moment.

“The thirteenth,” he said. “And you’ve said nothing to anybody since.”

“No, sir.”

“May I ask why not?”

“You told me to stand aside, once,” said Lyra. “In the staffroom. I don’t like owing people.”

For a second he looked almost young. “Then I’m in your debt, Miss Malfoy,” he said, “which I suspect you’ll enjoy.”

She was polite to him because he was a teacher, and because she had decided some time ago that she rather approved of him. The open door was for the rest of it.

“Does the Board know? About the staff?”

He smiled, a little wryly, as though he knew exactly whose daughter was asking, and what her father had been until the summer. “The Headmaster knows. He took the view that a man with a potion and a locked door once a month is no great danger to anybody. It helps to have a Potions master on the premises, of course.”

“The Potions master thinks you let Sirius Black into the castle,” said Lyra. “He set that essay to have you found out.”

“Sirius tells me you were in the Shack. That Harry trusted you with that much.” He looked at her over the goblet. “Then you know a dog needs nobody to hold the door for it.” He said it quite calmly. So he and Black had talked, and Black had told him who had fed him chocolate. “And Severus has his reasons, all the same. Sirius and I were at school together. There were four of us: James, Sirius, Peter Pettigrew and me. We were in and out of each other’s pockets for seven years.” His eyes went to the parchment on the desk, and came back. “James is dead and Sirius can’t say a word, which leaves me. I’m the last man alive who can stand up and say what Peter is. So if you’ve a mind to tell the world what I am, Miss Malfoy, I’d ask you to hold off until we’ve caught him.”

“Then tell somebody. Tell the Headmaster. Tell the *Prophet*.”

“A werewolf, writing to the *Prophet* to say that Sirius Black is innocent and the hero who died trying to stop him is alive?” He rubbed his eyes. “They would print it, I imagine. On the page with the jokes. And the two men who could swear to it can’t so much as point.” He hesitated. “Nor can they tell anybody that Peter goes about on four legs.”

“On *four*—”

“It was for my sake. They found out what I was in our second year, and instead of running a mile they spent the next three teaching themselves to be animals, so that I shouldn’t be alone at the full moon.” His mouth moved. “Also because it was the most dangerous thing they could think of, and they were fifteen. James was a stag. Sirius, as you know. And Peter—” He held up a finger and thumb, a little way apart. “Brown. Easy to overlook. He always was.”

The trap in the corridor that afternoon, and the square of treacle tart. *He’s got a toe missing on one of his front feet.* And the other thing, the thing everybody knew, that had been in all the papers: that of Peter Pettigrew, when Black had finished with him, they had found one finger.

Twelve years in a cage on a Weasley’s bedside table, being fed crusts. Lyra would have taken Azkaban. But then Lyra had never been a man who was afraid of what was waiting for him outside the cage—

“Pettigrew,” she said. “It’s Weasley’s rat. That’s what Potter’s been hunting. Not a pet at all. Pettigrew.”

Something else about rats tugged at her, from earlier that evening, small and grey and on its way out of reach—

“Exactly so—” Lupin stopped. He had gone, if anything, greyer. “You— Harry hadn’t told you?”

“No,” said Lyra. “He hadn’t.”

She sat quite still. A rat. The diary on the table in May, *our hostage*; the Shack, and the rain; her brother’s name in Potter’s mouth, to make sure she went. And at the bottom of all of it, a rat in a wire trap with a square of treacle tart.

“A rat,” she said. “He sent me home for a rat.”

“I’ve said too much. That was clumsy of me.” Lupin leaned forward. “Miss Malfoy, Peter would sell his own mother for another hour of breathing. A Death Eater has a master to answer to; Peter answers to nothing but his own skin. If he once thought you mattered to Harry, you would be the first thing he took. I’m quite sure that was all Harry—”

“And Granger?”

Lupin blinked. “Granger?”

“She goes everywhere with him. Is Granger *rat-proof*?”

Lupin opened his mouth and shut it again. He had the look of a man who had faced Dementors and Severus Snape and, once a month, himself, and was finding out that there was still something left in the world that he had no idea what to do with.

“I shall have a word with Harry,” he said hastily. “A firm one. If you would just sit down for a moment—”

But she was already at the door. She heard him say “Miss Malfoy—” behind her, and then, much more quietly, as though to the room in general, “Oh, *Harry*.”

She walked. She did not know where; anywhere that was away. All term she had been bringing him things, like a cat bringing mice to a door: Snape’s office, her father’s letter, the Fidelius, carried up three flights of stairs. Poor things, most of them. But hers. And all term he had stood in the doorway and let her, and when at last she held out her hand he had looked at it as if she had offered him a dead mouse—

At the far end of the corridor, Potter went past.

He was there and gone in a second, round the corner. Lyra went after him at once. When she reached the corner he was a dark head going down the staircase below her. Shouting would have fetched every prefect in the castle, so she saved it. She went down after him, very nearly running, one flight and then the next.

Her ribbon had come off somewhere on the second landing. She did not go back for it.

Potter was at the far end of the ground-floor corridor, under the tall windows, with Granger. The tall windows had gone black, and the torches were lit, and the two of them stood in the light of one with their heads together, exactly as they had stood in the window bay before the holidays, and Lyra was halfway down the flagstones before it occurred to her that he did not look like somebody who had just run down four flights of stairs. He looked like somebody who had been standing at a window for ten minutes, talking.

She did not stop to wonder about it.

Granger saw her first. Then Potter turned round.

“Malfoy?”

“You.” She had no breath left, and did not wait for any. “A *rat*. All of it—the rain, and the Shack, and *hands off, Malfoy*—that was about a rat? You sent me away so you could go round the castle looking for *Pettigrew*?”

His eyes went wide behind his glasses. “Who told you that?”

“Does it matter?”

She did not give him time to answer. “Your godfather can’t talk and your werewolf can’t leave his office once a month. That’s your army, is it? Against a rat, in a castle with seven floors?” She looked from him to Granger and back. “How many of you *are* there, Potter? You’re in the library and on the pitch and halfway up the stairs, all at once. Do you take it in turns?”

Potter glanced at Granger. It was a very small glance, the kind two people give each other over a thing they have agreed not to say, and Lyra saw it and hated it.

“He’d use you,” he said, not quite looking at her. “If he got one sniff of you, you’d be the first thing he went for.”

“I see,” said Lyra. “So you take *Granger*.”

Potter shut his mouth.

He shut it so fast she heard his teeth meet. Something happened at one corner of it, there and gone, as though he had caught himself about to do a thing and put it away again; and Lyra, who had catalogued every expression that face owned, most of them out of spite, could not tell what it had been. She decided it was guilt. It had better be.

She took another step.

“And what is she, a mascot? Is Granger’s neck less breakable than mine? Or is it that she’s *clean*—no father, no diary, nothing in your pocket with her name on it—so you can *trust* her? Is that it? She’s here because she wants to be, and I’m only here because you’ve got my father’s diary in your pocket?”

Her voice cracked on *pocket*. She heard it crack, and went on regardless, because if she stopped now she would never start again.

“Did you honestly think it was the *diary*?”

It had been the diary, to begin with. He had put it on a desk in an empty classroom in May with a hole punched through it and told her she was his hostage, and there had not been a great deal of room for argument. But she had been the one to put out her hand. She had stood there in her dressing gown and done the sums, properly, the way she had been brought up to, and the sums said that the Dark Lord had better stay dead, for her father’s sake, for all their sakes. And under the sums, somewhere she had never let anybody look and did not look now, there had been another line, and it said that she would rather have Potter alive than—

She did not let herself finish. She took hold of his collar instead, with both hands.

“Don’t flatter yourself,” she said into his face. “Nobody makes a Malfoy do anything. I chose. I sat in that classroom and did the sums and came down on your side, and I have never once gone back on it, and you put it on a shelf. As if I were something you could leave somewhere safe and come back for when you had time. As if I weren’t even worth *telling*. You owe me two. You said so yourself. And you have never once asked me for anything. Not *once*.”

Malfoys did not do favours. They extended credit, and only to people they meant to deal with again.

He did not answer. For once in his life he looked as if he had nothing ready, and after a moment he looked at Granger instead.

Granger had been watching all of this with an expression Lyra could only call rapt, like somebody at the theatre who has just been told she must go up on stage. She put it off as long as a decent person could. Then she cleared her throat.

“Malfoy. Please. You’re—this isn’t—” She did not quite meet Lyra’s eyes. “It wasn’t his to tell. Not all of it. I’ve got something that helps Harry, and it’s a secret, so—”

“Keep your *secret*. I’m not talking to you.”

Granger looked at her then, very briefly, the way one person who has seen a timetable looks at another, and went gamely on. “He hasn’t slept properly in weeks. If you knew what he’s been—”

“And *you* know, of course.” It came out of her like something with teeth. “Two little Muggle-raised children. You must have so much to talk about.”

“*Malfoy!*”

Granger had raised her voice; Lyra had not known she could. It only made things worse. Lyra was past being made better by anything. She knew exactly what she was going to say next, and she knew she would be sorry, and she reached for it anyway.

She had not said it since October. In the empty Arithmancy classroom, with no Potter anywhere near, it had not come out. It came now. It was the worst thing she had, and she wanted the worst thing; she wanted the pair of them to see precisely what it felt like to stand in this corridor with her hair coming down, and there was no other word in her that would show them.

“Don’t you *dare* raise your voice to me, you filthy little Mud—”

“Shut up, Malfoy.”

And then she could not have finished if she had tried, because Potter had bent his head and stopped her mouth with his.

This time there was no second in which she did not understand.

The word went wherever words go when there is nowhere left to say them. She still had his collar in both fists. She did not let go of it, because she had forgotten she was holding it, and because letting go would have meant deciding something. His glasses knocked against her cheekbone and he did not seem to notice. This went on. It went on until she noticed that she had stopped breathing, and then that he had too; long enough for her to hear, from very far away, a small strangled sound that was Granger with both hands clapped over her mouth; and somewhere in it Lyra forgot what she had been trying to say.

When he drew back, it was only a little way. His ears had gone scarlet, exactly as they had on the train, and this time there was no Dementor to blame. He did not speak at once. He looked, for a moment, as though he needed the moment.

“I told you in the library,” he said. His voice was not quite steady, and he did not try to make it steady. “Say anything you like to me. Not that.”

Lyra opened her mouth. Nothing in it was a word.

“Wh—that—you—”

“Call me whatever you want,” said Potter. “Tell me not to smile. You’ve every right; I earned that one. But don’t go making yourself smaller to do it.” A strand of her hair had come down across her face when the ribbon went. He hooked it with one finger and put it back behind her ear, carefully, as if it might go off. “Every time you try, I’ll stop you.”

Somewhere in there her hands had let go of his collar. She did not know what to do with them, so she did nothing with them.

“Was that—” Her voice came out small and wrong, and she made it try again. “Is that *charity* again?”

“No,” said Potter.

He did not dress it up, and he did not turn it into a joke. She had been waiting for the joke. It was the only thing left she could have held on to, and he did not give it to her.

He stepped back and showed her both his hands, open, as you might show somebody you were not carrying a wand.

“I got you wrong,” he said. “I thought it was only the little things you lied about.” He was quiet for a second. “You never say what you mean, do you. Not about anything that counts.”

He did not smile. She had told him not to, in the rain, and he was still doing as he was told. She could see what it was costing him, at the corners of his mouth, and that was worse—far worse—than if he had grinned at her outright.

“Go to bed, Malfoy,” he said, quite gently. “Tomorrow I’ll tell you everything.”

She stared at him.

“Everything?”

“Everything.”

Behind him, Granger had taken her hands away from her mouth. She looked at Lyra for exactly one second, without a word. From Granger, it amounted to a speech.

She did not remember the stairs down to the dungeons. She supposed she must have taken them, and given the password to the wall, and crossed the common room past whoever was in it, because at some point she was lying face down on her bed with the hangings drawn and her shoes still on.

She lay with two fingers pressed against her mouth, as though there were something there she might otherwise mislay.

Twice, now. The first one had been charity.

Above the lake, where she could not see it, the moon was coming up full.
