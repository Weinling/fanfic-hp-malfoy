# Chapter 65: Three

Harry came out on to the pitch on the Saturday with the taste of disinfectant still in his mouth.

It had been four days. He had cleaned a stone floor by hand, and given Snape a Herbology essay, and been told that it had the charm of a municipal lavatory. He had slept, on and off, about as much as you sleep on a night bus. Whenever he stopped doing something, he could see a thin man against a wall, with both hands up at his neck. *Did it serve?* It had got to be the last thing he heard at night and the first thing in the morning, in that dry voice. There was a good deal of low white sun and a hard wind off the lake. Hufflepuff were in yellow. All round the stands a great many people were eating things out of bags, and he looked at them as you might look at the inhabitants of a foreign country.

“You look like death,” said Angelina. “Mount up.”

He got on his broom, because that was what happened next.

He didn’t see much of the first half-hour. He went up to where it was cold and hung there with his eyes going over the pitch. Far below him the game went back and forth like something being done on a tablecloth. He knew there was a Snitch. It was a thing he knew, the way he knew the second of May. Smith had got the Quaffle, and scored, and scored again. He had a very clean flying style and he talked the whole time. You could see his mouth going. He came in on the left-hand hoop for the third time, and he said something to the Keeper, on the way past, with a smile, that Harry didn’t have to hear.

There had been a song, in November. It had been three months. Harry had seen Ron stop nineteen and twenty-six and thirty-one, by the light of Hagrid’s lanterns, with frost on his eyebrows and then with rain down his neck. He had fetched for most of them. He had thrown a good many, and he had got no better. It had not made very much difference on a Saturday afternoon. There were things you could do in the dark in front of your sister that you could not do with a Slytherin stand looking on, whether they sang or not. They weren’t singing today. They had just come down to watch. He could see the green from here, and he didn’t look for anybody in it.

“*THIRTY–NIL!*” said Lee Jordan. “And that’s Smith again, Hufflepuff’s elegant Chaser, who I’m told has a very nice mother—”

Ron had his elbows in.

Harry noticed that. He didn’t know why he noticed. He had been looking at nothing, and he found that he was looking at a long figure in scarlet in front of the hoops, a very long way down, with its big hands open. It wasn’t looking at Smith. It wasn’t looking at the stand. Smith came in again, flat and quick, and dropped his shoulder, and drew back his arm; and the head in front of the hoops did not turn. It stayed exactly where it was. It waited. It was like watching someone at a chessboard who has stopped reading the back of a magazine.

The Quaffle left Smith’s hand. Ron went right, all arm, and hit it so hard with the heel of his glove that you could hear it from the top of the sky.

There was a pause of about the length of a breath.

Harry heard somebody in the Gryffindor stand. It was a thin high voice, quite alone. It had no idea what it was doing. It was shouting “*Good—good tackle! Well tackled!*” and it was Hermione, standing on a bench in a scarf, with her fists up. Then the rest of them had got there. It came across the pitch like a door being flung open.

Smith tried the right-hand hoop. Ron took that one against his chest and sat back on his broom with a grunt. Katie went up the other end and scored. Smith tried a long one, from the edge of the area, with his hair blowing, and stopped smiling, and Ron went up for it with one hand and got his fingertips to it and turned it over the top. Nobody could have told you how. He looked as surprised as anybody. He looked round at the thing he had just done. He had gone a colour you could see from four hundred feet, and his mouth was wide open, and he was laughing.

*Oh*, thought Harry.

He couldn’t have said what it was. He hung up there on the end of the longest week of his life. He hadn’t anything to do with it at all; nobody had told him this one. He had seen the end of it once, in another life, coming up from the trees, in a crowd that was singing about a king. It was not the same. It was a cold Saturday in February and somebody who had put his badge on with cold hands in the dark had just done something that he’d been told he couldn’t. He felt it begin somewhere under his ribs. It came up through his chest and it hurt, like the blood coming back into a foot. There was a small gold flicker just off the end of the Hufflepuff stand, about a yard behind Cho Chang’s friend’s left ear. He had been looking at it for some time.

He put his head down and went.

Diggory had seen it a second after he did. He finished four lengths behind, flat along his broom, and came down at the Hufflepuff end and got off rather slowly, as a captain does with thirty points. Then he walked the length of the pitch, because there were people looking and he was Head Boy, and he shook Harry’s hand, once. He looked at the Snitch, which was trying to get out between Harry’s fingers.

“Fourteen weeks,” said Diggory. “*Later*, you said.”

He had made a fox on a hearthrug three Sundays ago, and it had sat down with its chin up, and there was a very small boy in that room behind the kitchens who had grown up looking at that chin across the breakfast table.

“I know,” said Harry.

“Good game,” said Diggory, in a clear pleasant carrying voice, and he went away to see to his team.

The final score was two hundred to thirty. Ron had let in three.

He said so to everyone. He said it on the pitch, with Ginny on his back and Angelina shaking him by the front of his robes. He said it in the changing room, and all the way up the marble staircase, walking in the middle of a crowd of people who were all walking backwards, and he said it to the Fat Lady, who said that she was delighted. “Three,” said Ron. “Out of *nineteen*. I’ve been working it out. That’s eighty-four per cent.” He had a split lip and a bruise coming up on his collarbone in the exact shape of a Quaffle, and he had an expression on his face that Harry had last seen on Neville, on the morning that his plant put out a knob.

“Eighty-four point two,” said Hermione, who had gone hoarse, and had not let go of his sleeve. “It was very good tackling.”

“It’s not *called*— It doesn’t matter. It’s not called that. Say it again.”

The party started in the corner and spread. Nobody decided that it should. It was the short-legged table, with a bucket on it, and then the sofa, and then the whole of the room. Fred and George had got something in a crate that they would only say was educational. It went off at about seven, and it wrote *THREE* on the ceiling in green and gold sparks, and then it wrote something else. Dean had got out a long roll of parchment, with a drawing on it of Smith’s face at the moment of the third save, and he went round pinning it up. It was extremely like. Lavender Brown gave them Lee Jordan. Lee gave them Lavender, with the hair. Somebody found the wireless.

Harry did not do very much for the first hour. He sat on the floor by the fender with a plate. He had got a lot of noise on all sides of him, and it was warm; he was aware of being rather slow, like somebody who has been a long time under water. Neville sat down next to him, with the pot.

The grey thing had flowered. He hadn’t seen that. It had put up a thick stalk from the top of its head at some time in the last week, with one small tight bud on the end, the colour of an old bruise. It didn’t smell of anything much. Neville had given it a rosette.

“It came out at the second save,” said Neville.

“It didn’t.”

“I had it under my coat.” He had got a large smear of gold paint across his forehead, and his round face was shining. He was looking at the room, with his mouth a little open. “Isn’t it *nice?*” said Neville.

Harry looked. There was Ginny, on the arm of the sofa, arm-wrestling Fred for a Sickle, and winning. There was Seamus, telling a first-year about the Hufflepuffs, with gestures. Parvati and Lavender had got up on a chair with the wireless. There was a long red-haired boy on the hearthrug with his shirt pulled down over one shoulder, showing people his bruise. It was very stupid. It would be gone in a week. Nobody in that room was thinking about the second of May; there was nobody there who had ever heard of it; and he wouldn’t have been able to tell you, sitting on a floor with a plate of cold sausage, why his eyes had started to sting.

“Yeah,” said Harry.

“You’ve got something on your—” Neville pointed.

It was a streamer. He took it off, and looked at it, and put it round the plant.

Then somebody had got him by the arm. It was Ginny, flushed and out of breath, with a Sickle in her teeth. She took it out. “You’re not sitting there,” she said. “You caught the Snitch. There’s a rule. Get up, you’ve got to do the dive.” He said that he hadn’t got to do anything. He said that he couldn’t. She looked at him, for about a second, with her very direct eyes. She didn’t ask. She just took hold of his other arm as well, and leaned back with all her weight.

They did the dive. It was done off the back of the sofa, with Lee Jordan holding him by the ankles and Dean being the Snitch. It went wrong. There was a certain amount of breakage. He came down on Seamus with somebody’s elbow in his ear, in a heap, and the wireless had started to play something with a great many trumpets in it, and from underneath him a muffled voice was saying “Three! *Three!*” He heard a noise going on, and it seemed to be coming from him. It was a great cracked whooping bray. He had not known that he still had that.

So he stopped being careful. It was as simple as that. He hadn’t been told to. He got up and took off his glasses, and wiped them on someone. There was a dance going on, of a sort. Fred had got Hermione by the hands; she was explaining to him how badly she danced, and showing him. Harry danced with Parvati. He danced with Neville and the plant. At some point he found that he was standing on the short-legged table, with *Unfogging the Future* sliding out from under it, and that he had his arm round Ron’s neck, and that Ron had got his round his, and they were bawling something at the ceiling that had once been a song about a hippogriff. He didn’t know the words. Nobody did. Ron’s lip had started bleeding again and he didn’t care. He had stopped being able to say *three*. He was just holding up three fingers.

“Mate!” Ron bellowed in his ear. “*Mate!*”

“What?”

“I don’t know!”

It was the funniest thing that anybody had ever said. They both had to get down.

It was Seamus who heard it. He had gone back to the wireless, to find some more trumpets, and he stopped with his hand on the dial, and his mouth came open. He turned it up. He held up one hand. It took a while for that to go round the room. A small flat excited voice was saying that it was most extraordinary; that nobody present at Wimbourne this afternoon would be likely to forget it; that for the first time since the spring of 1993, and against all reasonable expectation—

Ron had gone quite white.

“No,” said Ron.

“—*the Chudley Cannons*—”

“No. Shut up. *Shut up*, everybody!”

“—by a hundred and seventy to a hundred and sixty—”

He didn’t make any noise. He sat down on the floor. He sat with his long legs folded under him, and his split lip, and the bruise, and he put both his hands on top of his head, and looked in front of him. There were tears running down his face. Nobody laughed. It was a thing that had to be respected. Fred took off an imaginary hat.

“I’ve had a thought,” said Hermione. It was hardly a voice at all by now. She had come round the end of the sofa with her hair all over the place. She felt in the pocket of her robes, and looked at what she found, and pushed it into Ron’s hand. “I was told to give you this. If anything of the kind ever happened. I was told that it wouldn’t.”

It was a very small envelope, of thick and faintly ribbed paper. There were two Sickles in it. There was a slip with one line on it, in a clear, upright, beautiful hand that he had seen on a good many other things. It was the same hand that had once told him he was worth the ink.

*Luck. It will not recur. Same terms for the League?*

Ron looked at it for a long time. His lips moved. He had gone on crying, in an absent-minded way. Harry, looking over his shoulder with a chicken leg in his hand, saw him turn it over, to see if there was anything on the back, and start very slowly to grin. It came up one side of his face. He put his hand out, without looking, and somebody put a quill in it.

“That’s from nobody,” said Ron. He looked up, and there was something at the back of his wet blue eyes, not very much; he had once beaten a stone queen. “Isn’t it?”

“Haven’t the faintest,” said Harry.

“No,” said Ron. “Me neither.”

It was very late. They had let the fire go down. Somebody had got a blanket over Neville, who was asleep on the sofa with his mouth open and a plant on his chest, wearing a rosette and a streamer. There were people lying about here and there, in the corners, not saying much. The ceiling still said *THREE*, a little faintly. It seemed to have got into the plaster.

Harry had the big chair by the fender. He was lying in it sideways, with his legs over the arm. He hurt in about nine places. On the short-legged table somebody had left a mug. Ron had gone to sleep sitting up, with his back against the end of the sofa and his head tipped against Hermione’s knee. She was still awake. She had a book open in her lap that she wasn’t reading, and she had one hand resting on his hair. She didn’t seem to have noticed that. She looked across at Harry, and for a moment he thought that she was going to ask him something; there had been a look, on the Wednesday, over the toast. She looked at the mess on the floor, and the ceiling. Then she smiled at him. It was very tired and quite undefended. She held one finger up to her lips.

He had thought it would come back. He’d been waiting for that, in a dim way, for an hour or so, as you wait for a tooth. A dusty floor, and a man looking at him. He went and looked. It was still there. He didn’t suppose it would ever not be. It was only that it was a room in a house, now, and a long way off, and it had a great many other rooms in between.

*Give me something dull*, somebody had said.

He thought about that. He was very nearly asleep. He went along the passage, in a manner of speaking, as he had been going every night, to the cold clean place he had built, with its wall and its Herbology essay and the bare floor in the middle where there was nothing to put. He had looked for days. He only had the one good thing, and you could not leave that out.

He went back to the corner. He got hold of the table.

It was awkward to carry. It had one short leg. He took it along the passage and in at the door and he set it down in the middle of the floor, with a rude dragon on the top of it and a book under the leg, and a ring where somebody had put down a mug. He looked at it. It wobbled slightly, and stopped. You could turn that over. You could look underneath it as long as you liked; it had been looked at by Fred and George. It wouldn’t tell anybody a thing, except that there had been some people sitting at it.

It would bear a good deal of handling. It had.

He stood there for a bit, with his hands in his pockets. Then he went to sleep.
