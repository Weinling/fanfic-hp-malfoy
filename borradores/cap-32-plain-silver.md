# Chapter 32: Plain Silver

“I choose my partner, Headmaster.”

Viktor Krum said it from the door of the cabin, with snow melting on the shoulders of his furs, in the flat, heavy English he used for everything from Quidditch to the weather. He had been sent for. He had come in, and stood, and listened to his headmaster explain to him in Lyra’s hearing what an honour was being done him, and what a pair they would make, and how pleased Mr Malfoy would be; and when Karkaroff stopped for breath, he said that.

“Viktor—”

“I have asked already.” Krum did not look at Karkaroff. He looked at Lyra, quite steadily, without any embarrassment. “She says yes.”

Then he nodded to her, as one nods across a pitch to a Seeker one has nothing against, and went back out into the snow.

She had come down to the ship because the Ball was in three days and Viktor Krum had not said one word to her in eleven, and Slytherin had begun to notice. Montague had asked at breakfast, with great innocence, whether they did it by owl in Bulgaria. A thing said in front of the whole of Slytherin had to come true, or else she was a liar; and Lyra Malfoy was a great many things in that common room, but she had never once been caught out in that.

“Miss Malfoy.” Karkaroff had gone a curious colour over his beard. “A misunderstanding. The boy is young, he has his head full of— But we are not without resources! There is Poliakoff. A very good family. His father and your father have done business together for—”

“I was not aware,” said Lyra, “that I was being auctioned, sir.”

She did not wait for the answer. She went out on to the deck, past three Durmstrang boys who had very obviously been listening at the porthole and who straightened up as she passed as though they might be in with a chance, and down the gangplank, one wet step at a time.

At the bottom, on the shingle, Krum caught her up.

He did not hurry about it. He came down the gangplank behind her with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders hunched, and stopped a yard off, frowning at the lake.

“Headmaster says many things,” he said. “He says them in your name. I did not know. I am sorry for this.”

“You needn’t be,” said Lyra. “I know whom you asked.”

He looked at her then, sharply, and she saw him remember the library, and the girl with the hair, and a question asked at the edge of a Quidditch pitch.

“You tell me she is not Potter’s,” said Krum slowly. “You tell me true?”

“I generally do,” said Lyra.

Krum turned that over, slowly, like a broom that pulled to one side when he meant to find out which. Then he nodded once, and went back up the gangplank to his ship.

Lyra walked up the slope.

She did not mind that Viktor Krum was taking somebody else to the Ball. She told herself so twice, and the second time it was true. She had never wanted him. She knew exactly whom he was taking, and she wished them a very pleasant evening, and a great deal of hair.

What she minded was having been refused.

Nobody refused her. It was not a thing that happened. Boys asked, and she said no, kindly or otherwise, and they went away and asked Daphne; and today she had swallowed every scrap of pride she possessed and walked up a gangplank in the snow to offer herself, as near as made no difference, to a boy who had not asked; and she had been turned down, politely, in front of a man with yellow teeth who would very likely write to her father about it.

And all for a boy on a desk with his feet on a chair. *Only go with Krum. Or look as if you are.*

He would sort something out. He had said so. She did not know what it was, and he had not told her, and she had walked up that gangplank anyway; and now she wanted him to come round the corner of the greenhouses with that infuriating face and explain it to her, very pleased with himself, so that she could be extremely cold to him for about a minute and a half and then forgive him.

He did not come round the corner of the greenhouses.

Lyra stopped at the top of the slope, out of sight of the ship, and put her hand in her pocket and took hold of the ring, and for once she did the looking. She held it in her fist and thought, quite hard, of a boy on a desk with his feet on a chair, and waited for it to warm towards him.

It did not warm. It did not pulse. When she opened her hand it was lying in her palm exactly as it had come out of the box in Zonko’s: plain silver, and cold, and pointing nowhere at all.

He was not at dinner.

The Great Hall was fuller than it usually was at Christmas, since nobody from the fourth year up had gone home who could possibly avoid it, and the Gryffindor table was a long, noisy stretch of red and gold with Weasley at the middle of it, eating, and Granger beside him, not eating, with a book propped against the water jug. Lyra looked once, as one looks at a clock. There was a space on Granger’s other side where nobody sat.

He was not at breakfast on Friday either.

She found them in the library afterwards, at the table by the window, Granger behind a wall of books and Weasley in front of a sheet of parchment with nothing on it. The library was nearly empty. Madam Pince was dusting something at the far end with the expression of a woman who would rather be dusting it in an empty building.

“Where is he?”

Weasley looked up. “Who?”

“Don’t,” said Lyra.

Weasley opened his mouth, and Granger kicked him under the table, and he shut it again and looked aggrieved.

“He’s gone off for a few days,” said Granger. “With his godfather. It was something for Dumbledore, he said.”

“It’s the holidays, Malfoy,” said Weasley. “People go places.”

“He told you that.”

“Wednesday night.” Weasley shrugged. “After dinner. Said not to make a fuss.”

Granger said, “He said he’d be back for the Ball,” and looked down at her book while she said it, and did not look up again, and Lyra, who knew precisely what it looked like when a person was keeping something back, saw her do it, and could not for the life of her think what it was.

“Did he,” she said, and left them.

In the corridor outside, where there was nobody, she took the ring out of her pocket and put it on.

She had not worn it on her hand since August. It went on to the fourth finger of her left hand, where he had put it under a cloister in May, and shrank to fit, as it had then, and sat there cool and quiet: the plainest thing she owned.

It had worked all summer. That was the thing she kept coming back to, all that Friday morning, through a lunch she did not eat and an afternoon she spent walking. Every night at nine, behind the hangings of her bed at the Manor, it had told her how he was, from Surrey, and once from a cave on a coast she had never seen: purple at four in the morning, gold at a quarter past two, green by breakfast. Distance had never troubled it in the least. She knew the slip from Zonko’s by heart, and she went through it now, colour by colour. Red, for mortal danger. Purple. Gold. Green. The slip said what each one meant, and which way to look.

It did not say anything about a ring that stopped. It did not say what colour a ring went when the person wearing the other one was dead.

She looked down at her hand perhaps a hundred times that day. It was silver every time.

She was on the fourth floor at about four o’clock, in a corridor that nobody used even in term, when she heard the leg.

It came round the corner before the rest of him: a long clunk on the flagstones, and then the good foot, and then the staff. Mad-Eye Moody stopped a few yards off, in the grey light from the window, and looked at her with both eyes. The dark one stayed on her face. The blue one went down to her left hand, and stayed there.

“Your ring, Miss Malfoy,” he said. “What’s it showing?”

Lyra did not put her hand behind her back. It would have been no use whatever, and he would have enjoyed it.

“Nothing.”

“Nothing at all?”

“It’s silver, sir.” She heard her own voice and did not like it. “It’s just silver.”

Moody grunted. The blue eye left her hand and rolled away, up and backwards into his head, as though it had remembered something several floors off that it wanted to look at; it stayed there a moment, fixed on whatever it was, and then came swivelling back.

“Then wherever he is,” said Moody, “that ring can’t reach him.”

He shifted his weight on to the wooden leg, and turned.

“Do you know where he is?”

Her voice went up on the last word and she could not stop it. Moody stopped, with his back to her, and for a moment she thought he would not bother to answer.

“I know where he isn’t,” he said.

He did not move off. He stood there with his back to her, waiting, and she understood that he meant to stand there until she went; so Lyra turned round and walked away first, down the empty corridor, with her hands at her sides. The leg did not follow her. She did not hear it once, the whole length of the corridor, and that was worse than if it had.

He had not said Potter was safe. He had not said he was in danger either. He had asked about the ring as a man asks the time of somebody whose watch he does not trust, and gone away with the answer, and he had not once in the whole of it looked as if he cared which. She tried to be angry about that, and could not get it to take.

In August she had lain awake a whole night watching the ring go purple. That night she lay awake and watched it do nothing, and found it was much worse.

Saturday was the same, all day, and then it was Christmas.

At half past seven on Christmas night Lyra was sitting on the end of her bed in the fourth-year dormitory, dressed, with her hands in her lap.

She had done everything. She had begun at four o’clock, out of nothing but habit, because a thing that is going to take three hours does not care whether one wants to do it. She had washed her hair and dried it and put it up, in the style her mother’s maid did for the opera; she had practised it in August until she could do it blind, and it had taken an hour and a half and every pin was where it should be. She had put on the violet silk. It fitted as if it had been poured. There was a looking-glass by the door, and she had looked in it once on her way past and seen exactly what she had meant to see since the night of the dragons, and it had not helped in the least.

Her presents were in a heap at the foot of the bed, unopened, except for Draco’s, which had turned out to be a Chocolate Frog card of Viktor Krum with a note to say that he had three.

The others had gone down long ago, in a cloud of scent and complaint. Daphne Greengrass had asked twice whether she was coming, and been told *presently*, and gone. Lyra sat on the end of the bed with the ring on her finger, and looked at it, and did not get up.

The door opened.

“Malfoy,” said Crabbe. “It’s half past seven.”

She was in robes of a deep, warm yellow that her mother had chosen, and she looked, Lyra thought distantly, like somebody you would be glad to see coming up the path. She stood in the doorway for a moment. Then she came in and shut the door and sat down on the bed beside Lyra, carefully, so as not to crush anything.

“Krum’ll be waiting.”

“Krum is not waiting,” said Lyra. “Krum is taking somebody else.”

Crabbe drew a breath to say something about that, and then, visibly, did not. She looked at Lyra instead. She looked at her hair, and at the silk, and at her hands lying still in her lap; and Lyra saw her take it all in, in her slow, thorough way: that Lyra had not sent anybody for anything all day, nor complained about the bathroom, nor said one cutting word about Daphne’s shoes, which had deserved several.

“Malfoy,” said Crabbe, in a low, frightened voice. “Is it the Imperius?”

Lyra very nearly laughed. She had thrown the thing off in the middle of a classroom, rather than climb on a desk, in front of the whole of Slytherin.

“No.”

Crabbe’s eyes went down to the ring. She knew that ring. She had once been told to bin it.

“Then what’s Potter done?”

“Nothing.”

“*Nothing?*” said Crabbe.

“He’s not here. He hasn’t been here since Wednesday. He hasn’t done anything.”

Crabbe stood up.

Lyra had known her since they were eleven, and had never once seen her angry; she had not been sure that Crabbe knew how. She found out now. Crabbe stood in the middle of the dormitory with her fists shut, and was angry, thoroughly and without a scrap of dignity, as though somebody had to be and Lyra had plainly forgotten how.

“It’s *Christmas*,” said Crabbe. “It’s the *Ball*. He can’t do *nothing*, not tonight, not after you’ve— after all that— he can’t just *not come*! I’ll— if he’s in that castle somewhere I will find him and I will—”

She ran out of things she would do, and stood there breathing.

Lyra looked up at her.

“How long have you known?”

It was not the question she had meant to ask. Crabbe had given him the ring. There was no use asking whether she knew; but Lyra found that she wanted, very badly, to know since when.

“Since the lawn,” said Crabbe.

“What lawn?”

“First year. The first flying lesson.” Crabbe unclenched one fist, and looked at it, and seemed to wonder what it had been for. “When he caught you. Afterwards you went and stood over him and shouted at him to go to the hospital wing, and you had hold of his sleeve. You had hold of it till Professor McGonagall came. I was there. I was the one shouting your name.”

Lyra did not say anything for a moment.

She remembered the lawn. She remembered the grass in his hair and the blood on his sleeves, and being furious, and the wind. She had not known that anybody saw her hand.

“You never said.”

“You’d have stopped,” said Crabbe.

Then she wiped her face with the back of her wrist, and was Crabbe again, and held out her hand.

“Get up. Come on. If he doesn’t come, I’ll go with you.”

“You’re going with Nott.”

“I’ll stand him up.” Crabbe considered this, and found she had no objection to it. “He’ll only talk about his horse.”

Lyra laughed.

It surprised them both. It came out of her quite loud, in the empty dormitory, the first time in three days, and it went on for longer than the joke deserved; and Crabbe stood over her with her hand held out, beaming, as though she had done something clever.

Lyra took the hand, and got up.

At the door she stopped, and looked down at the ring. A sensible girl would take it off. Half of Slytherin would be at the next table, and Potter wore the other one where anybody could see it.

She left it where it was.

Theodore Nott was waiting at the top of the dungeon steps in a dark green dress robe, looking as if he had been told to wait and would go on doing so until the castle fell down. Lyra sent Crabbe to him with a small push between the shoulder blades.

“Go on. He’s come all this way to tell you about his horse.”

Then she went into the Great Hall by herself.

They had done it well; she could give them that. The walls were covered in a silvery frost that glittered, and there were garlands of mistletoe and ivy all across the starry black ceiling, and the House tables had gone, and in their place stood a hundred little round ones, each with a lantern on it that smelt faintly of cloves. A band was setting up on a stage at the far end. It was the most beautiful room she had ever been in that she had not been born to, and she walked into the middle of it in violet silk with every pin in place, and heard it begin.

“—*Malfoy*, isn’t that—”

“—thought she was going with Krum—”

“—saw Krum earlier, he had somebody, I didn’t see who—”

A Ravenclaw girl in pink smiled at her across two tables, the small, satisfied smile of somebody who has been waiting for years to see a particular thing happen to a particular person. Lyra looked back at her pleasantly until it went away.

She took a table near the wall, and sat at it alone. Goyle arrived a minute later on the arm of Blaise Zabini, which explained a good deal, Zabini having lost to Goyle at chess three times in November without once appearing to mind. Goyle looked at the empty chairs round Lyra and opened her mouth, and Zabini, who was cleverer than he let on, steered her away to the next table before she could say anything into it.

Up at the staff table, the Headmaster’s chair was not empty.

Dumbledore was in it, in robes the colour of plums, smiling at the room over the tops of his half-moon spectacles as though he had never been anywhere in his life. Lyra looked at him for a long moment. Moody was at the far end of the same table, with his flask, and his blue eye going round and round the Hall; it rested on her for a moment, and on her left hand, and moved on.

“The champions,” said Professor McGonagall, from the doors.

Everybody stood up.

They came in two by two. Cedric Diggory first, with Cho Chang of Ravenclaw, the pair of them so used to each other already that they forgot to look at anybody else. Then Fleur Delacour in silver-grey, like something off the top of a cake, on the arm of Roger Davies, who was gazing at her with his mouth slightly open and appeared to have no idea where he was. And then Viktor Krum, scowling at the floor; and on his arm, a girl that nobody knew.

The Hall murmured. Who was that? Was she from Beauxbatons? Had anybody ever seen her before?

Lyra had.

She had sat across a library table from her a month ago, behind a wall of dragon books, and passed her nothing, and been passed page three hundred and twelve. It was Granger. It was Granger with her hair smooth and shining and put up at the back of her head, in robes of a soft periwinkle blue, walking carefully because she was not used to the shoes, and smiling at the floor when she nearly turned an ankle as though she was not used to being looked at either. Lyra had always suspected there was a face somewhere under all that hair. She had not expected Granger to be the one who found it.

So that was whom Krum had asked; she had known that since the shingle. What she had not seen until this moment was the rest of it, and it was so simple that she was annoyed with herself.

*Only go with Krum. Or look as if you are.*

The whole thing stood on Viktor Krum asking Granger. It stood on a sentence of hers at the edge of a Quidditch pitch, *She is not Potter’s anything*; and Potter had been sure of it a month ago, in a classroom on the third floor, and had not told her that either.

“*Malfoy*.” Goyle had come back, Zabini or no Zabini, and was standing over her table, scarlet. “Granger stole your partner!”

“So it appears.”

“But she— he— I’ll—”

“You’ll do nothing whatever, Greer,” said Lyra. “Go and dance with Zabini.”

Out on the floor, the champions had taken their places for the first dance: Diggory and Chang, Delacour and Davies, Krum and Granger. There was a space beside them, and the band was waiting, and nobody stood in it. Professor McGonagall had turned round in the doorway and was looking out into the Entrance Hall with her mouth gone very thin.

The ring went warm.

It happened on her finger, under the table, between one breath and the next: three days of nothing, and then heat, as if somebody had breathed on it, and with the heat a small definite pull, like a hand on her sleeve, towards the doors. Lyra turned her head before anybody else in the Hall had thought to.

Potter was standing behind Professor McGonagall in the doorway, out of breath.

His hair was wet, as though he had put his head under a tap and called that dressing, and his tie was not straight. He had dress robes on, bottle green and very new; and under the hem of them, for anybody who cared to look down, were the grey trousers of his school uniform. He was holding his right hand a little away from his side, and the fingers of it did not quite open. McGonagall said something to him, short and furious, and he said, “Sorry, Professor. I know. I’ll sort it,” and came into the Hall.

He did not look round for her. He did not need to. He came straight across the floor between the little tables, past the champions and the empty space beside them, past Goyle, whose mouth had fallen open, to the table by the wall where Lyra sat alone in violet silk; and half the Slytherins in the Hall turned in their chairs to watch him do it.

“Malfoy,” said Potter. “Evening.”

He said it a little too loudly, for the next table, and she understood at once that he had practised it.

“I don’t suppose you’ve heard. I was going to bring Hermione, as it happens, and it turns out Krum got in first.” He had the rueful smile ready, and put it on. “And you’ve been left as well, by the look of it. Champions have to have a partner, apparently. It’s in the rules. So I thought, since we’re both stuck—”

It was kind. She could see every piece of it, and every piece was there for her: the borrowed bad luck, the rules, the two of them thrown together with no choice in the matter, so that a man reading about it in Wiltshire would find nothing anywhere that his daughter had chosen. He had built her a door to walk out of, and stood there holding it open, with his hair wet and his hand that would not open, three days late.

Lyra stood up.

“Nobody left me anywhere, Potter,” she said.

She did not say it loudly. The next table had gone quiet to listen to him, and it stayed quiet to listen to her, and so did the one beyond it.

“I turned Krum down.”

Potter stopped.

The rueful smile went on for half a second without him, and then it went, and there was nothing on his face but plain astonishment, like a man who has put his foot down on a stair that is not there.

Out on the floor, ten yards away, Viktor Krum turned his head.

He looked at Lyra, and then at Potter, and then, gravely, at the Hall in general.

“Is true,” said Krum. “She refuse me.” He looked down at Granger. “Is lucky for me.”

Granger, on his arm, looked up at him with her mouth a little open, and went pink to the roots of her smooth hair. She did not look at Lyra.

She had told him the truth at the edge of a Quidditch pitch, for the worst of reasons, and on the shingle she had not quite answered him; and here he was, in front of three schools, telling a lie for her without a flicker, in that flat heavy voice that nobody in the world would ever think to doubt. She thought he had just given her his answer to the shingle. She inclined her head to him, very slightly, across the floor, and Krum turned back to Granger.

The Hall had heard it. She could feel it hearing it: the tables turning, the whisper going outward from the wall in rings. *Lyra Malfoy turned down Viktor Krum.* And then, a moment after, the rest of it. *For Potter.* In front of Slytherin, and Durmstrang, and Beauxbatons, and the judges’ table, and anybody who cared to write it down. Her mother, at least, would not have to ask whom the violet had been for.

She held out her hand.

Potter looked at it. Then he took it, with his right, carefully; and as his cuff slid back she felt it under her fingers, on the inside of his wrist: a raised ridge of skin, hot to the touch, running round the bone like the mark of a whip.

She turned his wrist a quarter of an inch towards the light, and looked, and turned it back.

“Later,” said Potter, under his breath.

Lyra smiled at him, beautifully, for the benefit of the room. Behind the smile she said, so low that only he could hear it:

“You make me miserable.”

He did not have anything ready for that. She watched him look for something, and not find it, and give up looking.

“I know,” he said.

On her finger, against his hand, the ring was not silver any more. It had gone pink, a clear foolish Zonko’s pink, and she did not take her hand away to hide it. On his hand, the other one had gone the same.

“Lyra—”

“They’re waiting,” said Lyra. “Dance, Potter.”

He led her out into the empty space beside the champions, with her hand in his and the whole Hall watching, and the band, at long last, began.

She put her other hand on his shoulder. Over it, at the judges’ table, between Madame Maxime and Ludo Bagman, Professor Karkaroff had stopped clapping.
