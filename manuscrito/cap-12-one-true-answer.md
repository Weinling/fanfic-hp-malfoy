# Chapter 12: One True Answer

Professor Lupin came back on Thursday, and the first thing Lyra noticed was that he had a new patch.

It was on the left elbow of his robes, a square of brown tweed that matched nothing else he had on, and whoever had sewn it had been in a hurry or could not see very well, because the stitches went round it in two different sizes. That was the second thing.

The third was the rest of him. He had looked ill on the train, and better after a few weeks of school dinners, and now he looked worse than either: thinner in the face and greyer all over, as though somebody had gone over him with a damp cloth and taken some of the colour off.

He put his battered briefcase down on the desk and smiled round at them, and the Slytherin third-years, who had spent Monday afternoon copying wolf skulls off a blackboard in silence, were glad to see him and did not trouble to hide it.

“Good afternoon,” he said. “I’m sorry to have been away. I gather Professor Snape kept you busy.”

He opened the register. He had got as far as Bulstrode when one of the Warrington cousins put up his hand, in the manner of a boy who very much hoped to be rescued.

“Sir, he set us an essay. A whole roll, sir, on page three hundred and ninety-four. For Monday.”

Lupin’s quill stopped above the register. It was only for a moment. A bead of ink gathered on the nib and hung there, and he looked at it as though it interested him, and then he made a small neat tick beside Millicent’s name and went on to the next.

“I’ll have a word with Professor Snape,” he said mildly. “You needn’t hand it in.”

They cheered. Parkinson banged the desk with the flat of his hand, and somebody at the back said “Best teacher we’ve ever had” not quite under his breath, and Lupin smiled down at the register and went on reading out names as though he had not heard.

Lyra joined in. But she had seen one more thing, and she kept it.

Nobody knew what was on page three hundred and ninety-four of a book without looking; Lockhart would not have known what was on page ten of one of his own. Lupin had not reached for the textbook. He had not asked what the chapter was, or why Professor Snape should have leapt to the end of the book with two-thirds of it still to go.

He had known already.

The lesson was on Hinkypunks. He had one in a tank on the desk, a frail, one-legged little wisp of a creature that looked as if it had been made out of the smoke off a candle, hopping from side to side behind the glass with a lantern held up on a stick.

It lived on moorland, Lupin told them, and waited for travellers after dark, and let its light bob along just ahead of them until they had followed it off the path, and then it watched them sink. Goyle said that was the saddest thing she had ever heard. Parkinson said he would never fall for it, and Lupin said that was what most of the people at the bottom of bogs had said as well.

They spent the rest of the hour learning to tell a Hinkypunk’s lantern from a real one. As far as Lyra could see, it came down to not following lights you did not know. She took careful notes and thought about page three hundred and ninety-four.

When the bell went, Lupin said, without looking up from the tank, “Miss Malfoy, could you spare me a moment?”

Crabbe looked at her. Lyra gave her the smallest shake of the head, and Crabbe went out with Goyle and shut the door behind them a little more slowly than she needed to.

This time he did not bother with chairs. He came and sat on the edge of the desk in front of hers, with his hands folded, and seemed to be looking for the right place to begin. Close to, he looked more tired still. There was a fresh scratch across the back of one hand, red and only just closed, of the sort Lyra had once had from a peacock.

“I wanted to ask how you are,” he said. “After Saturday.”

“Very well, thank you, Professor.”

“Good. I’m glad.” He paused. “I’m told you stayed with him.”

She had told nobody. She had been very careful to tell nobody. Half the school had been in the stands on Saturday and the whole of it had been talking ever since, and every version she had heard ended with Potter and a flash of white and a hundred Dementors in flight; she came into none of them, except as the girl who had caught the Snitch while Potter was falling, which some people considered very poor form.

Nobody below the clouds had seen what happened at the top of them. That left two people who might have. One was the Headmaster, who had come out across the pitch with his wand raised and that face, and had not looked at her once. The other had been on the end of her hand, and had turned his head, and looked at it.

She could not decide which of them she would rather it had been.

“I was trying to catch the Snitch, sir,” she said. “He was in the way.”

“Yes,” said Lupin gravely. “I expect he was.” He studied his folded hands. “I’m not going to ask you about it. I only wanted to say one thing, and then you can go. You’ve met a Dementor twice this year, and a boggart once, and those two have a good deal in common. They both go looking for the thing we would rather not look at, and they are very good at finding it. Afterwards people tend to discover that it doesn’t go back where it came from quite as tidily as they would like.” He looked up. “If you should ever want to talk about either of them, my door is open. There’s no obligation, and nobody else need know.”

It was kindly meant. Lyra could see that, and that he was waiting rather than pressing, and that it would be the easiest thing in the world to say something. She had seen the Slytherin stand twice since Saturday, coming at her very fast out of the dark, both times at about three in the morning, and had lain afterwards with her eyes open until Millicent began to snore. She had told nobody about that either.

“Thank you, Professor,” she said, with perfect courtesy. “But I won.”

Lupin looked at her over his folded hands. Then he smiled, and it was a real smile, if a worn one.

“So you did. Congratulations.” He got up off the desk, more stiffly than anybody his age had any business to, and went back to the tank. “The offer stands, all the same. It doesn’t expire.”

She thanked him again and went.

At the door, with her hand on the handle, she found she had stopped.

Lupin had his back to her and was bent over the tank, tapping very gently on the glass with one finger while the Hinkypunk hopped about inside with its lantern up, and Lyra thought, for no reason she could have given, of the staff room in September: the wardrobe, and the hood, and the face inside it, and her wand arm that would not move.

*Stand aside, Miss Malfoy.* Nobody had asked him to. He had simply stepped in front of her, between her and the thing, while she was still standing there with her wand in the air.

She went out and shut the door quietly behind her.

By Friday the whole of the Slytherin third year had forgotten the essay had ever been set. Lyra wrote it anyway.

She wrote it on Saturday afternoon at a table at the back of the library, behind Ancient Runes, where the windows looked out over the lake and nobody came but the occasional Ravenclaw who had lost her way. It was the first dry Saturday in a fortnight and anybody with sense was outside in it.

Lyra had sense; she also had two reasons, which was one more than she generally needed. Professor Snape would want that essay whatever Professor Lupin said, and Professor Snape was at present in a position to write a letter to her father, and she had no wish to hand him a grievance to write it with.

The other reason was simpler. She wanted to know.

She began with what was in the book, because that was where one began. A werewolf could be told from a true wolf by its snout, which was a shade narrower; by its eyes; by the set of its skull, which was more like a man’s than any wolf’s ought to be. She copied out the two skulls Snape had drawn on the board and labelled them in her neatest hand.

Then she turned the page to the section on the human form and found that it was hardly a section at all. In human shape, the author admitted, the afflicted wizard could not be told from any other, except that he was apt to fall ill in the days about the full moon, a peculiarity which would be noticed, the author added, by anybody who troubled to keep a calendar.

Lyra kept several. She took her Astronomy charts out of her bag and unrolled the one for November, and put her finger on it, and there it was in her own careful ink: full moon, Saturday the sixth.

The day of the match. Nobody had seen the moon that night; there had been nothing in the sky but storm, and Lyra had spent the evening in the common room being congratulated, with the whole weight of the lake overhead.

But Lupin had been missing from the staff table since the Friday before it, and indisposed on the Monday after, and on Thursday he had come back greyer and thinner, with a new patch on his elbow and a fresh scratch across the back of one hand.

Her father had once remarked, about somebody else entirely, that you could always tell a werewolf by his tailor, since nobody would give one a job. She had laughed at the time.

There was the quill, too, stopping above the register at the words *page three hundred and ninety-four*, and Snape on Hallowe’en, looking along the empty staff table at one chair in particular.

She thought for one absurd moment of the black dog by the lake on Monday, two nights after, and dismissed it. It had been a dog, snout and all. A starving one.

It was all very suggestive, and none of it was proof, and she was about to tell herself so when she remembered the boggart.

She had been looking straight at it. Lupin had stepped in front of her and it had swung round to him, and for an instant, before he got rid of it, it had been a silvery-white orb hanging in the air like the moon seen through cloud. She had thought at the time that it was the dullest fear she had ever heard of.

A boggart became the thing you were most afraid of in the world. A grown man, who had faced a Dementor on the train without turning a hair, was afraid of the moon.

Lyra sat back in her chair.

Her first thought, and she was not proud of the order they came in, was that she had eaten his chocolate. She had sat in a railway carriage and eaten chocolate out of a werewolf’s pocket, and the dark sort, at that. Her second was the staff room, and the armchair he had pulled round to face his own, and how close he had been sitting.

She had been brought up to know exactly what a werewolf was. It was among the first things one learned about the world outside the Manor gates, along with Mudbloods and the Ministry: which things were wizards, and which had stopped being wizards and become something else. Werewolves were the second sort. They were what her father’s friends meant when they said over the port that the Ministry had gone soft.

And this, then, was what Professor Snape had sent her to find. A werewolf at the staff table: a man with something to hide and a reason to hate the Ministry, and the Dementors, and anybody who kept a register. Snape thought he had let Black in.

She went back to the charts and found October. There had been a half moon at Hallowe’en, which meant that on the night Black came, Lupin had been a man like any other, with two hands and a wand.

She tried to remember whether he had been sitting at the staff table during the feast, and found she had no idea at all. She had spent the feast looking at the Gryffindor table, for reasons that were nobody’s business, least of all hers.

So she could not clear him.

But she could see the hole in what Snape had given her, now that she was looking for it. A werewolf was a wolf one night a month. On the other nights he was a man in patched robes, and there was no door in the castle he could open that any other man in patched robes could not.

Being a werewolf made Lupin a great many things, in Lyra’s opinion, and none of them was Sirius Black’s accomplice.

Snape had a reason to hate him; she had watched him look at Lupin down the staff table on the first night of term as though he were Potter. And Snape had told her himself, in so many words, that he could prove nothing.

She had something Snape had not, besides. On the train Lupin had had Potter flat on the floor of a compartment, out cold, with nobody else there but a girl holding his shoulders down, and what he had said was *Enervate*. Then he had broken a slab of chocolate in two.

Professor Snape had told her on Monday that what a man meant mattered a great deal less than what came of it. What had come of Lupin, so far, was Potter sitting up.

It was not proof either, and she did not pretend that it was.

The essay was the obvious place for what she had. One paragraph at the end, in her best hand, and Snape would have exactly what he wanted out of somebody else’s quill; whatever she had come to owe him in his office on Monday would be paid, and he would have nothing left to write to her father about.

Leave it out, and he would keep that letter, unwritten, for as long as it suited him. That was the price, and she looked at it squarely.

Or she could write to her father, who would have Lupin out of the castle within the week and enjoy every day of it; she had watched him go after Hagrid in September, when all Hagrid had against him was a hippogriff.

But first he would want to know how she knew. The answer ran straight through Snape’s office to Sirius Black and a matter she had been told in plain words to put out of her mind, and afterwards the name Malfoy would be on the front of the *Prophet* again, when she had sworn in July that it would stay off.

In any case she was in no hurry to send her father’s house anything else to be quiet about. It was three weeks now since she had written to Draco not to play cards with the gentleman in the east cottage, and Draco, who answered letters before he had finished reading them, had not answered.

In the end none of that decided it. It was the staff room in September, and the voice that had said *Stand aside*, and the fact that he had never once mentioned it since.

Malfoys paid what they owed. Her father had taught her that along with the remark about werewolves and tailors, and she saw no reason to keep one lesson and not the other. She did not have to like the creditor. She would keep his secret, and she would keep an eye on him.

She had Defence again on Monday, in the front row, where she always sat. She rather suspected she would take the second row.

The essay, when it was done, ran to a roll and a half: the skulls, the snout, powdered silver for the bites, the Werewolf Code of Conduct of 1637, and a paragraph on the human form so strictly general that a Ministry clerk could have signed it.

It was the best piece of work she had done all term, and it accused nobody of anything. She would hand it to Professor Snape the same day and let him look for what was not in it.

She was blotting the last line when Madam Pince came round lighting the lamps. Lyra did not pack up.

She had not been avoiding Potter. She had simply been busy since Tuesday, in a succession of places he happened not to be: the far end of the Potions dungeon, the long way round to Charms, the back of the library on the first dry Saturday of the month.

It had worked very well. It went on working until about a quarter of an hour after the lamps were lit, when somebody pulled out the chair on the other side of her table, turned it round, and sat on it backwards with his arms folded along the top.

“You’ve been avoiding me,” said Potter.

“Winning takes up a surprising amount of time.”

He looked entirely recovered, for somebody she had last seen going off the side of a broom with his eyes shut. The broom itself, from what she had heard in the corridors, had still not turned up. His eyes went down to the parchment between her elbows. He read upside down, she had learned in October, faster than most people read the right way up, and she saw him reach the end of the title.

*The Werewolf, and How It May Be Recognised in Either of Its Forms.*

He stopped smiling. When he looked up there was nothing in his face at all except attention.

“What are you putting in it?”

“Everything in the book,” said Lyra. “Nothing that isn’t.”

They looked at each other across the table, and neither of them said the name. The lamp between them hissed softly. Somewhere at the far end of the room Madam Pince stamped a book as though it had wronged her.

“Good,” said Potter.

So he had known that too, before she did. She had made up her mind about Lupin by herself, for her own reasons, an hour before he sat down, and she did not tell him so. She did not tell him about the dog either. He would want to know what she had been doing by the lake with a slab of chocolate, and she had no intention of going into that.

He seemed to consider the matter closed. He tipped the chair forward onto its front legs and said, “Go on, then. How did you catch it?”

“I beg your pardon?”

“The Snitch. I was out cold. Everyone tells it different, and none of them were up there.” He waited. “You were.”

She had wanted to tell him on Saturday. She had held the Snitch up over her head at the top of the stands and turned round to tell him, and he had been unconscious at the time, which she still considered thoughtless. Nobody since had asked her the right way. Flint wanted to talk about the points and Parkinson about whether it had been frightening, and Crabbe and Goyle had been too tactful to ask about it at all.

So she told him. She told him about the glint of gold under his boot and the forty yards between, and the dive she had made at nothing in the middle of the pitch, and the pull-out thirty feet off the grass with the handle juddering under her, and he laughed out loud and had to put a hand over his mouth because of Madam Pince.

“That was *you*? Half our lot went after you. Wood’s been drawing it on the common-room blackboard all week. He thinks you saw something down there.”

“I did. I saw Wood looking.”

He laughed again into his hand. She went on, through the rain and the climb over the stands and the chase, and left out the cold, and the part in the middle, and came to the end. “And then it was just there, a yard off. So I let go of my broom and took it.”

He stopped laughing. “You let go of the broom?”

It was a Seeker’s question, quick and exact, and nobody else had thought to ask it.

“On purpose,” said Lyra.

He looked at her across the lamp. He said nothing more about it, and neither did she.

“Your third one worked,” she said, after a moment, because somebody had to say something. “The— whatever it was. The light.”

“Yeah,” said Potter.

He was still looking at her. He did not say what it had been, or how he had managed it, or what he had been thinking of when he did; and for once she did not ask.

“You held on,” he said.

It was not a question. So he remembered that much, at least, and perhaps it had been him, then, who told Lupin.

“You were about to make a mess of my pitch.”

“That’s two I owe you, then.”

“You haven’t paid the first.”

“I know.” He settled his arms more comfortably along the back of the chair and rested his chin on them, and looked at her from under his fringe. “Go on, then. You’ve been saving it.”

She had. She had been saving it for a month, and the question for a good deal longer, since a cold classroom in May with the moon in the window and a diary on the desk.

She looked along the aisle first. The Ravenclaws had gone at teatime. There was nobody between the shelves on either side, and Madam Pince was a lamp and a bun at the far end of the room, bent over the returns, well out of earshot. To his face, he had said in the Owlery, and not with anyone else there, and just the one.

She had it ready. She had worked on it on the window seat at the Manor in August and behind her bed-curtains ten days ago, until there was not a word left in it he could slip out through: how he knew. The diary and the number seven and the house up north, the hippogriff in the paddock and, since Monday, a black dog by the lake as hungry as he had said it would be; all of it, from the beginning, in his own words, and true. It would cost him. That had been the point.

He was waiting for it. She could see that he knew more or less what was coming, and had sat down at her table to pay it. She knew to the ounce what it was worth; she had known in the Owlery, when she named her price. She set it aside all the same, as deliberately as she would have declined to play a winning card, and asked the other one.

“On the train,” she said. “When you came round. Who did you hear?”

The other question went with it, for good. He had said just the one, and she had shaken his hand on it.

It was plainly not the one he had come to pay. He put a hand up and pushed his hair back off his forehead, the way he did forty times a day, except that this time his fingers stopped at the scar and stayed there. Lyra had been watching him on a broom for two years, and she knew a tell when she saw one.

“My mum,” he said.

He let his hand drop back to the chair.

“It’s the only way I get to hear her.”

The lamp hissed. Every remark Lyra had ready, and she had a great many, turned out to be meant for a different boy. She thought, without wanting to, of a charm she had never heard of until Monday, and of a week.

“Mine’s a stand,” she said at last. “The Slytherin one, coming at me. About two seconds of it.”

He had been there. She watched him work out which stand, and which two seconds.

“It ends, though,” he said.

“Not in theirs.”

Neither of them said anything after that. From the far end of the room Madam Pince announced that the library was closing, in the voice of a woman who would deal personally with anybody who argued.

Potter got up. He swung the chair round the right way and, for the first time in Lyra’s experience, pushed it in under the table, and went off down the aisle with his hands in his pockets. At the end of it, by Ancient Runes, he stopped and turned round.

“Malfoy.”

She looked up.

“Thanks for asking.”

Then he was gone between the shelves, and Madam Pince came down the aisle with her snuffer and put out the lamp.
