# Chapter 57: Against the Wind

At four minutes to midnight on the last day of 1995, Sirius Black was standing on the kitchen table at Number Twelve, Grimmauld Place, conducting “Auld Lang Syne” with a ham bone, and Harry’s ring tried to take his finger off.

He thought for a moment that somebody had hexed him. He was by the stove, with a mug. He had been by the stove for most of Christmas. It was where you stood in that house if you wanted to look as if you were joining in. Fred and George had got Kreacher’s tea cosy to sing the descant, and Ron had his arm along the back of Hermione’s chair without appearing to have told anybody, and Harry looked down, and the ring on his right hand had gone the colour of the inside of a mouth.

It had done something on Christmas night. He had seen that: a few seconds of a sick dirty yellow, at about one in the morning, and then plain silver again, and he had called and called and got nothing back. He had lain awake working out what you could do with a few seconds of yellow.

This was not yellow. *If mine goes red, I’ll know.* He had said that himself, on the third floor, with a good deal of confidence. *And I’ll come.*

He put the mug down on the stove. Nobody looked round. He went out, and up the stairs, past the elves’ heads, three at a time. In the bedroom he got his boots on and the Firebolt out from behind the wardrobe, and his hand went to the back of the trunk and came out with Dudley’s road atlas, which he had carried up from Surrey in August for no reason he could have told you. He had worked it out from here as well. Of course he had. It was ninety-one miles. With no wind it was fifty minutes.

He opened the window. The cold came in like something thrown, and there was snow in it, going past from left to right, very fast.

The wind was in the west.

Behind him, four floors down, they had all started to shout the numbers.

*You can’t come to Wiltshire, Potter.*

“Watch me,” said Harry, to nobody, and went out over the sill.

He had been cold before. He had been cold in a lake and on a mountain, and for most of one winter in a tent. He had never been cold like the sky over the M4.

For the first half-hour he could still see the lights, going by underneath in a long smeared string, and after Reading he lost them. There was nothing then but the noise. The snow came straight at him out of the dark and got in behind his glasses. He flew flat along the handle with his face in his sleeve and the atlas buttoned inside his jumper, and he had no way at all of telling whether he was moving, except that his hands were dying from the fingers in. The ring was the only warm thing he owned. It hurt. It went on hurting, and every so often it hurt more, on one side, and he found that if he leaned that way it eased; and after a while he stopped steering by anything else.

A year ago he would have known what was going to happen next. He tried to remember what that had been like. There had been a good deal more to it than he had appreciated at the time.

He came down twice to look at the ground, and it was white, and it had nothing on it. The second time he hit it. He lay on his back on what might have been Hampshire with his mouth full of snow and the broom across his legs, and he thought this was going to be an extremely stupid way to go, and that she would never let him hear the end of it. He got up. The left leg had not been right since the summer. It was four in the morning and there was no moon, and he had been wrong about the wind.

The page went green at a place a little way south of Marlborough. On the page there was nothing there. When he came over it the ring went so hot that he yelled, and he looked down, and through a hole in the snow he saw a long pale hill with something on it, and on the top of the hill a very small dark mark that was not a tree.

He came in badly. He stepped off the broom and his knees went, and he stood there holding on to it with both hands until they came back.

She was sitting on the highest part of the ridge, in the open, in the snow, with her knees drawn up. She had a dark cloak on over something pale. The Firebolt lay beside her with snow on it. There was snow on her shoulders, and on her hair, and it was not melting very fast. Below her the whole side of the hill fell away, white on white. You could just make out where somebody long ago had cut the shape of a horse into the chalk. You could see its ear.

She did not turn her head.

“You’re late,” said Malfoy.

Her voice was all right. It was perfectly level. He got a few yards nearer, and stopped, and he put his hands in his pockets, as he had been doing since November; it took some time, because they would not bend. Then he could see her better, and he saw what she was doing. She had her wand across her knees, and she was holding her right hand with her left, very hard. It did not make any difference. Both of them were shaking. It was a tiny fast shake, and it went right up her arms. He had seen hands do that. He had seen them do it on a woman in the Janus Thickey Ward who kept giving her son sweet wrappers.

“I got lost,” said Harry.

“I know. I could feel you going round Andover.”

He waited. There was a great deal of wind up there, and it took most of what you said. After a bit she looked at him. He had been looking at that face for four and a half years, and in all that time he had never seen it with nothing on it. It was not that it was crying. He would have known what to do about that, or at any rate what not to do. It had simply been left there, like a house with the doors open.

“I did it,” said Lyra Malfoy. “It worked.”

She told him sitting down. He sat too, a yard off, because he could not have stayed standing, and she went through it in order, with dates. It was the most frightening thing he had ever listened to.

She told him about the old man in the east cottage, who was her grandfather, and who had been dead since before Harry could talk. There had been no Abraxas Malfoy in the other life; or there had been, and he had really died, and nobody had thought about him since. She told him about a long book with a lock on it. She told him who had been sitting in Lucius’s chair five hours ago. She told him that he had come in by the front door, and wiped his boots, and been very nice to the elf. There was a snake under the table. There was a crown on the cloth.

“He knows it was you,” she said. “He got there over the soup. He said it out loud, with a finger on it. He’s got basilisk, and he’s got the Chamber, and he’s got you; and he’s not angry. He’s *interested*. I think that’s the most horrible thing I’ve ever seen.”

He had thought he was as cold as he could get.

For four years Harry had been the person in any room who knew what Voldemort was doing. Voldemort had not known he was there. It had been the one thing he had, under everything, right at the bottom. He sat on a hill in Wiltshire and felt it go, without any fuss.

There was something he was supposed to do now. He had been meaning to since October. He had been meaning to in the third-floor classroom, and on the sixth, and in a room full of cups, and he had kept finding that it was not the moment. She had asked him in June, and in the straw she had stood there and read him a list, and there had been a crown that was not on it.

“I broke it,” said Harry. His lips had gone stiff, and it came out oddly. “The diadem. Me. A year last September, the first night back, with the fang. That’s three, with the locket and the diary. I left it there, in the Room, because Dumbledore said that if it went missing he’d know. I should have told you. I had about a hundred chances.”

She looked at him for some time, with the snow blowing across her face.

“I know,” she said. “I worked it out on Boxing Day, by the sundial. I’ve known for five days.”

“You—”

“I wanted to see if you’d tell me.”

He opened his mouth, and shut it. It was so exactly the sort of thing she would do, on a hill, in the middle of the night, an hour out of the worst thing that had ever happened to her. He felt something give, behind his breastbone, like a stuck drawer. It might have been the start of a laugh. It did not get any further than that.

“Did I pass?”

“You’re late,” said Malfoy. “I’ve told you that.” She looked away down the hill. “His name is Riddle. His father was a Muggle.”

“Yeah.”

“You knew that as well.”

“Yeah.”

“Of course you did. Is there *anything*—” She stopped, and got her breath. Her hands had started again. She looked down at them, and she held them out a little way from her, as you might hold something that belonged to somebody else. “It took two goes. The first time, nothing happened. I want you to know that. I stood there and said it and there were some sparks. And he said I had to mean it. You said that once. You said it on a train. So I went and looked, and I found something, and I meant it.” She had not raised her voice. “It’s very easy, Potter. Nobody tells you that. It was the easiest thing I’ve ever done. It was like going down a stair. He’s my *father*. He’d just got down on his knees to save me. And Draco watched, and he’s locked his door; and do you know what my father said to me? He said I was magnificent.”

There were a number of things you could say. He had heard most of them, at one time or another, from people who had meant it kindly. He knew what each of them was worth.

“Aren’t you going to tell me that it wasn’t my fault?”

“No,” said Harry.

“Good.”

“I know it’s easy.”

She turned her head.

“I’ve done it,” he said. “Twice. Not here. In the other one. The first time it didn’t work either; I was fifteen. It was somebody who’d just killed someone. She told me the same thing, that you’ve got to mean it. So the second time I did. I was seventeen, and he’d spat in the face of a teacher of mine, and I didn’t have to look for anything. It knocked him across the room.” He looked at his boots. “I liked it. Nobody knows that. I’d never said that out loud to anybody.”

The wind came over the top of the hill and took some snow with it. She went on looking at him. He could not tell what she was thinking; he had never been able to, and it was the best thing about her. But some of the awful open emptiness had gone out of her face. It was as if somebody had come along the street and shut one door.

“What did you do?” she said. “After.”

“Went on. There was a lot on.”

“Did it stop?”

He thought about it. He had promised that he wouldn’t lie.

“The hands do,” said Harry.

It must have been getting on for seven. It was as dark as ever, but the wind had dropped, and somewhere away to the east the sky had started to look as if it was thinking about it. She had said she could stay until the elves lit the fires. She had got out by the garden door, past the lamp in the cottage, with that thing asleep in the south wing, and he had not asked her how, because he did not think he could stand to know.

She looked at him sitting there. He had his chin in his collar, and he had his hands in his pockets. She looked at the pockets.

“How long are you proposing to go on with that?”

“With what?”

“You’ve been doing it since November. You did it in the snow in Hogsmeade. I thought you were going to freeze to the lane.” Her teeth had started to chatter. It seemed to annoy her. “You will have to touch me at some point, Potter. I’m extremely cold.”

Everything in him stopped.

He had not gone a day without seeing it. Red marks, coming up at the edges of his fingers. *Take that back.* He had seen her go white. He had stood on the sixth floor with his back against a wall, and he had seen a thing in Lyra Malfoy’s eyes that he had seen in a great many eyes, in a war, and it had been looking at him. You didn’t get to come back from that. He had worked that out. He had thought that if he kept his hands where she could see them, and where they could do nothing, then at least he would know where they were.

“I can’t,” said Harry.

“Don’t be absurd.”

She moved. She shifted along the ridge on her knees, stiffly, until she was in front of him. She took hold of his right wrist in her shaking fingers, and she pulled. He let her. He did not know how you stopped her. She got his hand out of his pocket, and turned it over, and looked at the back of it, at the line and the three words, in what light there was. It had gone a bad colour. She pushed back her left sleeve. She took his hand, as one might take something out of a cupboard, and she put it round her wrist, in the same place, with his fingers where they had been.

“Hold it,” said Lyra.

He could not breathe. He could feel her pulse. It was going very fast, and it was going against the side of his thumb, an inch from the words.

“I don’t—”

“I’m not forgiving you. Don’t think that for a moment. I’ve got nine answers to what you said to me in June, and that’s not even the thing I’m angriest about.” She had shut her eyes. There was snow on her lashes. “It’s the only thing I’ve got left to do tonight that is up to me. Hold it. Until I’ve stopped.”

He held it. He held it as you would hold an egg, or a lit match, and he felt the shaking come up through his fingers and go on into his arm. It took a long time. He had never in either of his lives been so frightened. He sat on the top of a hill with his eyes stinging, in the dark, and he didn’t move. The sky went from black to a very dark grey. Below them the horse came slowly up out of the snow, a leg at a time.

At some point he noticed that he could not feel it any more. Her hand had gone still in his. Her head had come down against the front of his cloak, without anybody having said anything about it.

“There,” said Malfoy, indistinctly. “That wasn’t so difficult.”

“I’ll tell you what’s going to happen,” she said.

She had got up. She was standing with her back to the east and doing something to her hair, with four pins out of her cloak pocket, by feel. It seemed to help her. By the time it was up she looked very nearly like somebody he knew.

“I shall go home, and have breakfast. On the eighth, I shall go back to school. My father told me in July that if you ever came back to the window I was to leave it open. He has been saying so for a year. *A boy who confides is worth sitting beside.* He thinks you’re in love with me, and that I’m clever. And now that thing is sitting in his chair, and wants to know all about you, and I need to be useful, Potter. I need to be the most useful person in that house, by a very long way, or the next time that he looks up from the soup it will be at Draco.”

He had seen where this was going. He had got to his feet, and he kept his mouth shut. It was probably the cleverest thing he had done all year.

She finished with the last pin. She did not look at him. She looked at the horse. There was a muscle going in her cheek, and he knew what this was costing, to the Knut, because he had been there. He had stood in the straw and offered it to her. *I can keep you safe inside it.* She had said something about a bird.

“I shall come to the window,” said Malfoy. “I shall be seen to. I’ll write to my father every week, and tell him what you’ve told me. So you will have to tell me things. You’ll have to think of them. They’ll have to be good enough to keep me alive, and they mustn’t cost you anything, and you will sit down with me and go through it, and you will not *ever* mention what I said to you in June.” She stopped. It seemed to be a stair she had to get up by herself. He watched her do it. “I said I’d rather be nothing at all.”

“You were fourteen.”

“I was *magnificent*,” she said, with loathing. She turned round, and looked him in the eye. “I shall want scraps, Potter. Good ones.”

Nobody in two lives had ever said anything so beautiful to him.

He did not smile. He had the feeling that if he did, he would be found in the spring. “All right,” said Harry.

“There are terms.”

“I thought there might be.”

“Nothing moves without asking. Me, Draco, anybody. You don’t put me anywhere. If I find you’ve been standing behind a curtain deciding what’s good for me, I’ll give you to him myself. And you tell me things first. Before I’ve had to work them out on a hill.” She drew a breath. “I don’t mean the Forest. I know you can’t. I haven’t forgotten that there’s a door you keep shut. I’m not a fool.”

He thought about what was behind that door. He thought of how it would be to say it, here, at this time in the morning, to that face.

“No,” he said. “All right.”

“And you put a bell on my brother.”

“Yeah.”

Her chin came up. He saw what it took. “Leave it on,” said Lyra Malfoy.

It was properly grey now. There was a thin line of something along the bottom of the sky. She bent, and picked up her broom, and knocked the snow off it. She stood there with it, not looking at anything in particular. Then she dropped it. She took the two steps across the ridge, and took hold of the front of his cloak in both hands, and pulled his head down. Her nose was like ice. Her mouth was very cold, and it tasted of snow, and of being furious. He got his arms round her, and they would hardly bend. It was over almost at once, and then it turned out that it wasn’t. He didn’t know how long it went on. When it stopped he found he was holding on to her, with both hands, hard, by the back of her cloak, and that she had let him, and his eyes were wet, which he put down to the wind.

She stepped back. She looked at his collar, and turned it down, and pressed it flat.

“That,” said Malfoy, “was the last one.”

He found he had a voice. He could not think where it had been.

“This year,” said Harry.

There was a silence. It went on for some little time. She looked at him. She looked at the sky over Wiltshire, in which, as far as anybody could tell, the sun was about to come up on the first of January.

“It’s *January*,” said Malfoy.

“Happy New Year.”

It was not a laugh. It would not have counted as a laugh anywhere in the world; it was one short breath, through the nose, with a good deal of pain in it. She swung her leg over the broom. She went straight up off the top of the hill, and tilted, and dropped away to the south like something falling off a table, flat out, with her cloak cracking behind her. He stood and watched until there was nothing to watch.

Then he looked down. The ring was on his right hand, quite cool. It had been red for six hours. It was going, as he looked at it, a very faint and doubtful pink.

He got on the Firebolt, and pointed it at London. He had the wind behind him the whole way home.
