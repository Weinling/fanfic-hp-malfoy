# Chapter: Bothy

He left on the Saturday night, after the castle had gone quiet, from the flat roof behind the owlery stair.

It was the first warm night of the year. There was half a moon, and a wind you could have lit a match in. He had the Firebolt, and the atlas buttoned inside his jumper, and two days. Nobody would miss him until Monday. He had told Hermione he’d got a headache, and she had looked at him over the Ancient Runes and said nothing, as she’d been saying it for a week. He had told a person on the seventh floor that he had some Potions to catch up on. She was so happy. She had laughed, with her feet on his knee, and told him to mind he didn’t fall in. He stood on the leads and looked at the dark line of the mountains to the north.

There were forty-five days of Sirius. He had found a place on page 71, a long way from any of the red lines. It had a small grey square on it and two words in italics, and he wanted to see if there was a roof.

That was what he was doing. He wanted that understood. He wasn’t going anywhere to get away from a bathroom mirror.

He kicked off, and the castle dropped away, and he flew up the long black side of the lake with his hands getting cold. It was very good to have something to steer. He had gone about half a mile. He was over the first of the trees, with the Forest going by underneath like a sea, and he was thinking of nothing at all, when somebody behind him and a little to the left said, in a perfectly ordinary voice:

“Oi.”

His arm came up.

It came round from the shoulder, with no weight in it at all, and the wand was in his hand. He hadn’t got the broom under him any more; he had turned on it, in the air, without knowing; there was a sound in his ears like a great many people breathing in. He couldn’t have told you what had happened. Everything in him had stopped. It was cold, and it smelt of leaves. He knew that voice. He knew it, and he had got to get that word out of his mouth before— There was something the matter with his chest. It had simply closed. Somebody ought not to be here. That was all he knew. Somebody ought not to be *here*, he had left them, he had gone on purpose, he had not said goodbye—

“Blimey,” said Ron. “Put that down.”

He was about ten yards off, on the Cleansweep, with his hair blowing and a rucksack on his back. He was holding one hand up, palm out, as you might to a horse. There was a good deal of moon on his face. It was large and freckled and rather annoyed.

“It’s me,” said Ron. “You’re not going on your own.”

Harry looked at the wand. It seemed to be somebody else’s. He looked along his arm and it took him some time to find the way back. He got his left hand round his right wrist and pushed. He had started to shake, all over, quite hard; he could feel the broom shaking under him. He had no idea why. There wasn’t anything to be frightened of. There was just a bit of night, and a lake, and Ron Weasley.

“What are you *doing?*” It came out in a high cracked voice.

“What are you?” Ron drifted a bit nearer, looking at him. “You’ve gone a funny colour. You’ve had that atlas out for a fortnight, mate. You think I don’t know what you look like when you’ve got a plan? I’ve been watching you have plans since I was eleven. You go all quiet, and you eat less, and you look at the door.” He hitched up the rucksack. “I don’t know what this is. I don’t care. I said to Hermione, he’s off somewhere Saturday, and she said *I know, he’s got a headache*, and she gave me a look. I’ve brought sandwiches. Where are we going?”

Harry opened his mouth.

Nothing was the matter. It had gone. He could feel it going, draining down out of his chest, and leaving him cold and rather sick, as you are when you’ve missed a step in the dark. He couldn’t remember what he had thought. He’d been going to say something. He looked at Ron’s hand, which was still held up, and which was very big and had a split nail, and his eyes had got hot.

“North,” said Harry.

“Right,” said Ron. “Is it far?”

They got there at about six, with the sun coming up behind them and the whole of the moor going from black to brown to a pale burnt gold.

He hadn’t known there was so much of it. It went on to the edge of the world. There was nothing on it but water, thousands of small dark pools with the sky in them, and rock, and a long way off, a single line of telegraph poles going away from nobody to nowhere. They came down in the heather beside a stream. Harry’s legs had gone. Ron stood with his hands on his hips, breathing it in, with the wind pulling at his jumper and a sheep looking at him.

The bothy was about a hundred yards up the slope. It had been a shepherd’s, or something. It had four stone walls and a door with one hinge, and the Ordnance Survey had told no lies about it. Half of the roof had gone in, and there were a great many slates lying about inside on a floor of old sheep droppings, with a rusty bedstead, and a small tree.

“Well,” said Ron, from the doorway. He looked up at the sky through the rafters. “It’s got a nice view.”

Harry sat down on a stone.

He ought to say something. He had been thinking how to do that for the last hundred miles. He’d got it ready, more or less. It was quite a good story.

“It’s for Sirius,” said Harry. “He’s going to need somewhere. In June. Where nobody knows about it, not Moody, not the Order. Just for a bit.”

Ron turned round. He looked at Harry with his head a little on one side.

“Why?”

There were several answers to that. He looked at them. There was a sheep behind Ron’s shoulder, chewing; it had a blue mark on its side.

“I can’t tell you,” said Harry.

He had said that word to a good many people. He’d said it to Hermione, and to somebody in the straw. He waited. He knew what came next, more or less. He had been told he was a liar by experts, and he had got his face ready.

Ron didn’t say anything for a bit. He looked at the bedstead. He looked at Harry again, for about as long as it would take to move a knight. Then he took off the rucksack, and put it on a wall. He rolled his sleeves up.

“Right,” said Ron. “We’ll want the slates off the floor first. You can’t do a roof from the middle. Who’s got a wand that isn’t shaking?”

It turned out that Ron could mend a roof.

It was the most surprising thing that had happened to Harry for some time. He had been in that family’s house for five summers, in two lives, and it had never crossed his mind to wonder who kept it up. “Well, who d’you think?” said Ron, from the top of the wall, with a nail in his mouth. “It’s five floors on a chicken shed. It’s got no right to be standing. Dad does the charms and it’s me and Charlie do the rest; Bill used to. You can’t just use a Sticking Charm, it’ll let go in a frost. You’ve got to do the lap, look, you start at the bottom. Not like that. Give it here. Who taught you to hold a slate?”

“Nobody.”

“I can see that. It’s like watching you throw.”

He held things. It was what he was for. He stood on a stone in his shirtsleeves, in the rising sun, passing up slates, and being told that he was doing it wrong. Ron worked with his tongue between his teeth. He had a way of looking at a gap and then at the pile, and going straight to the one that would fit. Harry had seen him do that with a bishop. They got the tree out. They burnt the bedstead, or at any rate they tried to, and Ron did something quick and low to the door that made it hang straight, and said that it was only a thing that his mum did. At about eleven it came on to rain, very lightly, out of a clear sky, and they got in under what they had done, and sat with their backs against the wall.

Ron undid the rucksack. There were about nine sandwiches in it, in a napkin, as well as a very large flat object wrapped in greaseproof paper. He looked at that with a certain respect.

“Where did you get those?” said Harry.

“That’s the thing,” said Ron. “I was in the passage by the kitchens. Last night, I thought I’d get something. And that Goyle came out, with her sleeves rolled up. She’s always there. I don’t know what she’s doing, I think she lives there. She looked at the rucksack. She looked at me, and I thought, here we go. And she went back in, and came out with this lot, and put it in my hands.” He bit into a sandwich, and his eyes closed. “Oh, *Merlin*. She said you’d want to keep your strength up. What’s she mean, you? I never said anything about you. I don’t understand Slytherins.”

The rain ran off the slates in front of them, in a line of bright drops, and only about half of it came in.

They stayed for the rest of the day. There wasn’t any reason. The roof was as done as it was going to get. He’d said they could start back when it got dark, and it was a long way off being dark, and there was nothing to do; they had got to the end of everything. Ron took off his boots. He lay down flat in the heather on the south side of the wall, with his hands behind his head and a stalk of something in his mouth. After a while Harry lay down as well.

He couldn’t think when he had last done that. You could hear the water going, under everything, and a bird a long way up. There was a smell of warm peat and sheep. He had a blister on his hand and he had got sunburnt on one side of his nose. He looked at the sky for some time without its meaning anything.

“I think I’m going to ask her something,” said Ron.

Harry turned his head.

Ron was looking straight up. His ears had gone a dull, slow pink, and he had stopped chewing the stalk. “Hermione. I don’t know what. There’s got to be something you ask. It’s been ages. I don’t know what we’re doing. I put my arm on a chair at Christmas and she didn’t say anything, and I’ve been sort of leaving it there. It’s been four months. My arm’s gone dead.” He took the stalk out and looked at it. “She did me a timetable for my revision. She’d done a little star on the days that I’d been good. I looked at that, and I thought I was going to be sick. Is that normal? You’ve done all this. You’d know.”

He could have told him. He could feel it, right there behind his teeth, and it nearly made him laugh. *You’ve got your arms full of fangs. You say something about the house-elves.* It was the only thing he’d ever known that he would have paid to go on knowing. He lay on the side of a Scottish hill and looked at his best friend’s ear.

“I think you just say it,” said Harry. “Badly. Then see what happens.”

“That’s rubbish.”

“Yeah.”

“Is that what you did? In Slough?”

He said it quite easily. A sheep coughed, somewhere down the slope. Harry looked at the sky.

“There’s nobody in Slough.”

“No,” said Ron. “I know.” He put the stalk back in his mouth. “She went up to tens in January. Malfoy. The day after, you started humming. I’ve got a graph, mate. You’ve seen it. It’s got two lines on it, I did the other one for a laugh.” He said it to the clouds, not at all loudly. “I’m not asking. I don’t want to know. I can’t stand her. She’s a horrible cow, and I’ve been losing Sickles to her since Christmas, and if you tell me about it, I’ll have to have an opinion. I just thought you’d want to know that you’re not as clever as you think.”

Harry lay perfectly still. He had a feeling as if somebody had reached over and taken a heavy bag out of his hand, without comment, and put it down.

Neither of them said anything for some time. The shadow of a cloud came across the moor, and went over them, and went on. There was something he had been carrying up seven flights of stairs for six days. He’d looked at it in a mirror. He had called down it, and listened; and he thought that if he didn’t say it to someone he would go mad, and there was just the one person who had been there.

“Ron,” said Harry. “What was I like?”

“When?”

“First year. Before. When we first— On the train.”

Ron thought about it. You could see him doing it. He rolled over on one elbow, and looked at Harry, quite seriously, with one eye shut against the sun.

“Skinny,” said Ron.

“No, but—”

“You ate everything. You bought the whole trolley, I’d never seen anything like it. You kept asking what things were. You had those glasses.” He frowned. “Why? You were all right. You were a bit of a prat. You still do that thing, when somebody’s said something and you’re trying to think what the right face is, you push your glasses up. You did it on the train. You did it this morning, when I told you about the slates. You’ve got exactly the same stupid hair. I don’t know. You’re just you, aren’t you? What are you asking me for?”

He said it as he might have told you the way to the Charms corridor. He looked at Harry a moment longer, and yawned, and dropped back into the heather.

There was something the matter with the sky. It had gone rather bright and blurred. Harry took off his glasses and rubbed them on his shirt. He held them in his hand for a bit. He noticed what he was about to do with them; and he put them on, and left them there, and did not push them up.

He didn’t believe it. He wanted to be clear about that. Ron didn’t know anything. He’d got no idea what he was talking about. He had seen a boy eat some Chocolate Frogs. It wasn’t evidence. It was only that nobody had said it before, and he lay and listened to it go round, under the sound of the water, and the sun was on his face.

He thought that this was probably the best day he had ever had with Ron Weasley. He had a good many to choose from. There had been a chess game, and a car, and a tent in a wet wood, and a boy dropping everything he was holding in a burning corridor. He went through them. He had got to the end of them before he noticed that Ron had started to snore.

They flew back in the last of the light. Ron had to go slowly, on the Cleansweep, and Harry went beside him, with the moor going dark underneath, and all the small pools turning off one after another like lamps. He looked back once. He could just make out the square of it on the hillside, with its patched roof. It leaned a bit. There was a corner over the door that was never going to keep the wet out, and Ron had looked at that for a long time, with a slate in his hand, and said that it would do.

They came in over the Forest at about nine. The castle stood up ahead of them, with all its windows lit. Somebody in Gryffindor Tower was practising the recorder, very badly. Ron brought the Cleansweep down on the flat roof behind the owlery stair and got off stiffly, and stood easing his back. He had got a burnt neck and a long scrape up his arm and he looked as pleased with himself as Harry had ever seen him. He started to do up the rucksack. It seemed to need a good deal of attention.

“Whatever this is,” said Ron.

He didn’t look up. He was doing something to a buckle, with his big fingers.

“I’m not asking. I said. You’ve got your reasons, you always have. It’s just that you’ve got a look, lately, like Percy when he was writing to Crouch. I’ve been thinking about it. I don’t mind not knowing things. I’m used to it. I just want to know that if there was something I ought to—” He got the buckle done, and looked at it. “You’d tell me, wouldn’t you? If it was anything to do with us.”

It was a very quiet evening. A long way down, somebody was calling a cat.

Harry looked at him. He looked at the big bent head with the sun on it, and the bits of heather, and the ears. He had been through this door before, a great many times. He could feel the whole of the thing that was on the other side: a cauldron, in a bookshop. A girl behind a shed. There was nothing whatever to stop him. There was nobody up there at all. He only had to open his mouth.

He had had the best day of his life.

“Yeah,” said Harry.

Ron nodded. He looked up then, and grinned, with his burnt nose. He hit Harry on the arm, and shouldered the rucksack, and went away down the stairs, two at a time, towards the noise.
