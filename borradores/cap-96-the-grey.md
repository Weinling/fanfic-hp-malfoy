# Chapter 102: The Grey

She had never in her life been so near herself, or liked it less.

It sat by the window of a compartment two-thirds of the way down the Hogwarts Express, with its hands in its lap and its ankles crossed, and looked out at Scotland going past in the rain; and it had her hair, and her chin, and the line running down from under her left ear into the collar of her school robes, white and raised, exactly where Professor Snape had put it; and it was holding its knees together as though somebody might at any moment try to take them away.

She was on the seat beside it, under a Cloak that had been made for a man, with the hem tucked under her feet. She had been there since a quarter to two. She had not moved more than a hand’s breadth in all that time, and she had not made a sound, and nobody coming down the corridor and looking through the glass would have seen anything but Lyra Malfoy, alone in a compartment for six, going home to her mother for Easter.

The thing on her wrist lay with its eye open, under her own sleeve, under the Cloak, and listened to the rain.

She put two fingers on the inside of his left wrist.

He uncrossed his ankles. They had settled that on paper a fortnight ago: two fingers, and a pressure, and a direction. Up meant up. Down meant down, or *stop that*, or *your chin*. She had not known, when she wrote it, how often she would want to use the third. He was doing it beautifully in most respects. He had her face, and he had gone over her book of rounds for a fortnight until he could do her walk on paper, and in a mirror in a borrowed room at ten o’clock last night he had stood up straight for the first time in it and looked at the floor, and then not; and she had sat on the fender and watched her own face be frightened, from the outside, for an hour, and it had been one of the worst hours that she had spent.

But his chin was wrong.

It kept going down. It went down by a quarter of an inch whenever he thought nobody was looking, and she would see her own face sink, by the window, into the collar of her robes, with an expression on it that she had never once in her life had, of a boy from a cupboard trying not to be noticed; and she would press, and up it would come, too far, so that for a moment Lyra Malfoy looked out of the window of the Hogwarts Express like an archbishop at a hanging.

Somewhere about Doncaster it got to her.

She could not help it. She had been perfectly steady for four hours and a quarter, and she had been frightened for most of them, and then she watched her own chin go up like a drawbridge for the sixth time, at a field of cows, with the most tremendous dignity, and something came up through the middle of her that she could not get her teeth on. She put her face into her knees under the Cloak. She shook. She made no noise whatever, because she was a Malfoy and had been trained to it since she was four; but the seat shook with her, and he felt it, and turned her head, slowly, and looked at a place in the air about a foot to the left of where she was, with her own eyebrows drawn together into a line.

It was so exactly the face she made at Weasleys that she had to bite the side of her thumb.

At ten past four he went down the corridor to the lavatory with his bag, and came back with his hair still hers; and at ten past five, and at ten past six. Each time the corridor was a little emptier, and the lamps had come on in it, and she sat in the dark with her knees drawn up and counted the flasks in her head, and found that there were enough, and counted them again.

Her mother had sent the Portkey on the Friday, in a box, with a note: *The 7.40, by the left-luggage. Do not be late. N.*

A shoehorn: the old horn one from the boot-room, with a crack along the handle that had been mended with silver wire when she was seven; she would have known it in the dark. He took it out of his bag at the left-luggage office at King’s Cross, among the trunks, at a quarter to eight, and held it out from him at the end of her arm, with her face very white; and she took hold of the end of it, under the Cloak, between his hand and the crack, and with the other hand she found his wrist and took hold of that too, because she did not intend to lose him between a railway station and a field.

The hook came behind her navel. King’s Cross went away.

They came down on the grass, in the dark, in a field.

It was a field. She had known it would be, and it stopped her breath all the same. He was on his knees in the wet with the shoehorn in one hand, and she was on her knees beside him with her fingers still round his wrist; and there was nothing in front of them but a long dim slope going up into the night, with thistles on it, and a smell of sheep, and the wind coming over from the downs. There was no gate. There was no gravel. There was no house. She had lived in it for sixteen years and three-quarters and she could not see it, because she had never needed to be told.

She felt him look.

He did it with her face, but it was his: she felt it through his wrist, the whole of him going still and then, slowly, beginning to go forward. She knew what he was doing. He was hearing a voice on the floor of a shop, with no breath behind it, saying nine words. He had them. He had had them for five weeks, since she had dragged them up out of herself with her heart stopping; and she felt the moment that he said them over in his head, because his pulse went under her fingers like a horse taking a fence.

He stood up.

She had never seen it happen. She had been told about it, in July, by a man with a cold finger, standing on a step in the sun; and she had stood on that step afterwards and watched people come up out of an empty field, one by one, and stop, and look about them as if somebody had pulled a cloth off a table. She had not known what it looked like from the inside. She was on her knees in the wet with her hand on a wrist, and she felt him see it: the whole of it, all at once, coming up out of the dark. The gates. The two lines of limes going away up the drive. The long pale front, with its lamps lit along the ground floor, and the lake, and something white on the lawn that lifted its head.

He said nothing. He stood in a field with her face and her shoes on, and looked at her house, and his wrist was shaking so that she had to hold it hard.

She pressed. Up.

He went. She went with him, a step behind and a little to the left, with the Cloak gathered in her free hand off the gravel, and her fingers on his wrist; and the gates knew her, and opened for the face they had been told to open for, and shut behind the two of them without a sound.

Her mother was in the hall.

She had known she would be. Her mother always did this, on the first night of a holiday: she stood at the foot of the great staircase in the light of the big lamp, quite still, with her hands folded in front of her, as she had stood when Lyra came home from her first term with a new girl’s voice and a cold, and every term since; and she did not come to the door, because one did not, and she did not smile until the door had shut, because there were servants.

She had on the dove-grey. She had the pearls. She was thinner than at Christmas, and there were shadows under the bones of her face that powder had been asked to see to; and she looked at her daughter coming up the steps in a damp school cloak, with the line running down into the collar, and her face did nothing at all.

“You’re late,” said her mother.

“The train,” said the face beside Lyra, in her voice.

The voice was good. So nearly hers that she felt the hair on her arms lift under the Cloak. It came out a little low, a little careful, as her voice came out when she was tired and had decided not to show it; and it said *the train* as one says it to a mother who will not wish to hear more, and stopped. She had written that line. She had not known, until she heard it, that she said it like that.

Her mother came across the hall.

She came quite slowly. Pobble had appeared at the back of the hall with her ears flat and been sent away again with one glance. Her mother stopped in front of the face, a foot away, a little below it, for he was taller in her shoes than Lyra had ever been in them, and that was one thing they had not been able to mend; and she put up her hands.

They went to the collar of the school robes. It had turned in at the back, on the train. Her mother turned it out again. She did it as she had done it every morning of Lyra’s childhood, on the stairs, before breakfast, with two fingers and a thumb, a small exact correction, and then she smoothed it flat.

Then her hands went to the shoulders.

They rested there. They should not have. Her mother did that, to finish: a touch at each shoulder, light, as one sets a vase straight on a shelf, and then away. Lyra had felt it every morning for sixteen years. She had never once known it stay.

It stayed: perhaps a second longer than ever before, with both hands flat on the shoulders of the school robes, and Lyra stood a yard away under the Cloak and saw her mother’s face, and saw the moment come into it, and saw what it was.

He was standing as she had taught him. Shoulders back. He had done it in a mirror for a fortnight. But nobody stands only as he has been taught, and there was something under the training, in the set of him, that went back further than a fortnight: a way of standing in a hall as though one had come there to be thrown out of it, and meant to stay. Her mother had seen that once before, in December, by the hourglasses, in a boy with his hands at his sides. *You have my cousin’s way of standing*, she had said, to the air beside the wall.

Her mother’s hands came off the shoulders.

She did not step back. She did not look round. She did not look, as anybody else in the world would have looked, at the air beside her daughter, where her daughter was. She looked at the face in front of her for exactly as long as she always had, and no longer; and then she lifted her chin, by a quarter of an inch, as though she were looking at a hem.

“You’ll want to wear the grey,” said Narcissa Malfoy. “The square neck. He will want to see it.”

“Yes, Mother.”

“I’ve had it put out. Go up and change. Draco is waiting to bring the tea.”

She turned away. She went back across the hall towards the drawing room, with her skirts going on the marble, and her back as straight as a wand; and at the door of it, with her hand on the handle, she stopped, and said without turning round, in the voice she used for the linen:

“And mind your chin at dinner. It has been up since the gate.”

Lyra stood in the hall of her own house, a yard from her own face, and watched her mother choose.

She had seen her mother choose before, she thought, without knowing what she was watching; and she had taken it for weakness, because she had been younger and had known nothing whatever about anything. It was not weakness. She saw that now. She saw a woman with her hand on a door, in her own house, with a man in the drawing room who had once taught her son to pour tea, deciding in the space of a breath, with no time and no help and nobody to ask, which of her children she was going to lie for; and choosing both.

She found that she could not see the staircase very well. She pressed, all the same. Up.

Draco brought the tea at half past eight, in the small drawing room, on the silver tray.

He was in the clean grey suit. He had grown, a little, since January, and the cuffs were short, and the tie was still under his ear; nobody had touched it in three months, and it had gone shiny along the knot. He came in at the door without stopping in it. He set the tray down on the low table, beside the fire, with great care, and took up the pot by the handle, with one hand on the lid, as he had been taught; and he poured one cup, with the milk after, and held it out, saucer and all, to his sister, who was sitting in the corner of the green sofa in the square-necked grey with her ankles crossed.

He did not look at her face. He looked, as he had always looked at things, at the edges.

Lyra saw him do it. She was standing against the wall by the window, a yard from the curtain, in the dark, where she had been told by two fingers on her own wrist to stand. She saw his eyes go to the hands that took the saucer. They went to the knees, and to the shoes, and to the row of small covered buttons down the front of the grey, which Harry had done up himself on the landing with her fingers shaking on his elbow under the Cloak, and which were right, every one, she had counted them. She saw Draco count them too. And then she saw his eyes go to the left arm, which was out of its sling since the first of the month, and lying along the arm of the sofa as easily as anybody’s arm, and which had not lain like that since December, because it could not; and his eyes stopped there.

He went on standing with the empty saucer.

His face did nothing. A Malfoy face, and twelve, and it had been in this house since Christmas, pouring tea for a man who had eyes like a lamp turned down; it had learnt. He looked at the arm. He looked, once, quite deliberately, at the air beside the curtain, about a foot to the right of where she was, so that she very nearly put out her hand.

He hiccupped.

It was only once, and quite small. He put his hand to his mouth, with great dignity, and took it away again.

“I beg your pardon,” said Draco.

“It’s quite all right,” said the face on the sofa, in her voice. It was not the right thing to say. She would have said nothing at all, and looked at him.

Draco considered this. Then he took up the pot again, and filled a second cup, and set it on the tray, on the side nearest the curtain, and turned the handle round towards the window with one finger, by about an inch; and went out, without stopping in the door, and shut it.

She did not drink it. She could not have reached it without walking through the firelight. She stood against the wall, and looked at a cup of tea going cold on a tray with its handle turned towards her, and kept her hand over her mouth for some time.

There were nine at dinner. There was a place laid for a tenth.

The place was at the Dark Lord’s left hand, opposite Draco, with the second-best silver and a clean glass and a white card folded in the middle of the plate; and Lyra could read it from where she was, under the end of the long table, between the legs of her great-grandfather’s chair and the wall, with the Cloak pulled round her knees and the smell of beeswax and soup coming down through the cloth. *Mr H. Potter.* Somebody with a good hand had done it. She thought it was probably his own.

She could see feet. That was all, from there. Her mother’s grey slippers, side by side. Aunt Bella’s boots, which were not clean. Avery’s, and old Nott’s, and somebody she did not know. Professor Snape’s black ones, at the far end, quite still. Draco’s small polished shoes, on the rung of his chair, where he had been told not to put them. And in the middle of the left side, in front of her own chair, her own black shoes with the strap, polished so that one could have shaved in them, with somebody’s feet in them who had never worn a strap in his life, set neatly together on the carpet and pressed down hard, as though the floor might go.

She had the end of the Cloak in her teeth. She had counted: he had drunk the last flask but one on the landing at a quarter to nine, with her fingers on his elbow, and there were fifty minutes on it, and the last was in the pocket of the grey.

They let the soup go round. He never ate. She heard the glass turn by the stem, on the cloth above her, as she had heard it turn every night of every holiday for a year; and she heard Avery tell a story about a train, and Aunt Bella laugh at the wrong part. She heard her mother say something about the snowdrops. She heard nothing from her own chair for a long time, and then a spoon set down.

“And our guest,” said the Dark Lord, “is late. Or not coming. Miss Malfoy?”

The feet in her shoes did not move.

“He wouldn’t come, my lord.”

Her voice again: low, and a little flat, and it had something under it that she would have put there herself, if she had thought of it: a girl who had been made to look a fool, and was not going to say so.

“Wouldn’t,” said the Dark Lord.

“I asked him, my lord. In the Entrance Hall. You heard me.” A breath. “He said that he would. And this morning there was a note on my plate. He had thought better of it. He would be spending Easter with Lupin, and I might tell my mother that he was sorry.” The voice did a thing then that she had not taught it, and would not have dared to: it went thin, for a moment, like a child’s. “I don’t know why.”

There was a silence above the cloth. It went on long enough for Lyra to count her own pulse to eleven, against her teeth.

“How very disappointing,” said the Dark Lord. He said it with the most perfect sympathy, as a man commiserates with a hostess over a soufflé. “And after the spoon. One does so hate to be wrong about a boy. Well.” The glass turned. “It doesn’t signify tonight. We shall come to the account later, you and I; there’s no hurry, when a thing has fallen due. Look at me, my dear.”

Her mother’s slippers moved, very slightly, on the carpet.

“She has a headache, my lord,” said Narcissa Malfoy. Her voice was exactly as usual. “She has had it since the train. I’ve given her something for it, and I’m afraid it has made her rather stupid. She was sick on the stairs. I thought you would wish to know, in case she should seem slow.”

A lie, and such a good one, so ordinary, so precisely the sort of thing that a mother says at dinner about a girl who has come home pale from school, that Lyra, under the table, with the Cloak in her teeth, felt her own eyes sting with something that was like pride. That was how one did it. One did not explain. One handed over a small dull reason, nicely wrapped, and let the person take it home.

“Poor child,” said the Dark Lord. “Then I shan’t keep her. Only a moment.”

Aunt Bella’s boots had come round the end of the table.

She heard them come. They came with a little skip in them, as they always did when there was going to be something to watch; and they stopped beside her own chair, and there was a rustle above the cloth, and the sound of two kisses, very loud and very wet, one on each cheek, as Aunt Bella had kissed her at the door in July, on the day she was given a house to keep.

“Poorly,” said Aunt Bella, close and hot. “Poorly little niece. You’ll feel better when he’s done. Everybody does.”

The boots went back. At the far end of the table, Professor Snape’s black ones had not moved at all. She could see nothing of him above the ankle. She would have given a great deal, then, to see his face, and she did not know why.

“Look at me,” said the Dark Lord.

She could see none of it.

That was the thing that she would remember, afterwards, in the order that she remembered things: that she had been under a table in her own dining room, with her knees against her chin and her brother’s shoes eighteen inches from her face, and that the one thing in the world that she needed to see was happening four feet above her head, inside a boy, and she could not see it. She had built it. She had built every piece of it on a window-seat, over six months of Thursdays, and then she had taken it down again, a strip at a time, and given it to somebody else to put up in the dark, in forty-five-minute pieces, behind her face; and he was in it now. The other one. He was standing in the door with his boots wiped, looking round.

She did not know what he saw. She knew what was there to see. A long table laid for breakfast, with a white cloth ironed on the table so that the creases ran the long way. The sun coming in from the left, low, at nine o’clock on a morning in the second week of October. A coffee pot with a dent by the spout. A toast rack with one bar bent. A dress laid over a chair for Christmas, with the hem turned back. A boy at the window, eleven years old, in a jumper too big for him, looking at the door. And a stool.

The stool.

She had written it on the left of her chair, beside the marmalade, where it went.

She knew it the moment she thought of it, with the whole of her, as one knows a step is missing in the dark. The stool was not where it went. It had not been there since the last night of the year before last, when a long white finger had come down in her own head and lifted it, and turned it to the light, and looked underneath, and set it down again a little to the left. She had watched him do it. She had felt the Hat lift. And she had sat on a rug in February with a pencil, and written the stool down where she had put it, at eleven, and not where he had left it; because she had never once in fourteen months let herself look at the place where it was now, and her pencil had stopped above the word, and gone on.

He would come to the table. He always came to the table. He would come to the stool.

She heard the glass stop turning.

Above her head, there was nothing at all: no sound, no breath, for about as long as it takes to lift a small thing up and look at it. Her brother’s foot had come off the rung of his chair. Her mother’s slippers were quite still. And under the table, in the dark, with her arm against her chest and the thing on it looking at nothing, Lyra Malfoy waited for the man above her to find a stool in the wrong place, in a house that she had built for him to walk through; and to put down his glass; and to say, in that pleasant voice, as one says it to a child who has been caught out in a very small lie, *Now, then.*

The glass began to turn again.

“You keep a very tidy house, Miss Malfoy,” said the Dark Lord.

He said it as he had said it once before, a year and three months ago, at this table, with real appreciation. Then he asked Avery to pass the salt.

She did not move. She could not have moved for anything. She knelt under her great-grandfather’s chair with the Cloak in her teeth and the whole of her gone cold, and went back over it, and over it, as one goes over a sum that has come out right when it ought not to have done. She had written it wrong. She knew she had written it wrong. And he had come to the stool, and looked at it, and found it a little to the left, exactly where he had left it; and smiled.

Somebody had put it there. It had not been her, and it had not been Harry, who had never been told.

Four feet above her head, a boy with her face picked up a spoon, as she had taught him, like a pen, and began to eat his soup; and his other hand had gone, under the cloth, to the place behind his right ear, and was pressing there, hard, as one presses a door that has been shut against a wind.
