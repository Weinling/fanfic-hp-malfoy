# Chapter 48: Him, and Now You

Granger sat down opposite her in the library on a Tuesday in the middle of November, at the table by the window, without asking, which was how Lyra had been sitting down opposite Granger since March.

She had a stack of books, as always, and she put them down, as always, between them, like a wall with a gap in it. But she did not open any of them. She folded her hands on the top one and looked at Lyra across it, and Lyra, who had been reading the same page of *Numerology and Grammatica* for twenty minutes, looked back.

“He told me,” said Granger.

Lyra did not ask who. She did not ask what. She sat with her quill in her hand and felt the whole of the library go very far away, as though somebody had put a glass over the table.

“Not all of it,” said Granger. Her voice was very low and very steady, and her face was pink to the ears. “I don’t think all of it. But enough. That he’s—” She stopped, and chose the word, and Lyra watched her choose it. “That he’s been here before. That there was another time, and it went differently, and he remembers. That he knew about the Chamber.” She looked down at her hands. “That you weren’t in it.”

The quill was making a blot on the page. Lyra lifted it.

“When?”

“Last week. I made him.” Granger’s mouth went thin. “I’ve known something was wrong since the hospital wing in June. I watched him read your note. I watched him lie to me about it. I’ve watched him all summer, at Grimmauld Place, looking at things before they happened, and not being surprised when they did, except that he *was* surprised, this summer, all the time, and I didn’t understand why that was worse. So I sat him down in an empty classroom and I didn’t let him leave until he’d told me.” She paused. “It took four hours. He cried, at one point. I don’t think he meant me to see.”

Lyra said nothing.

“Ron doesn’t know,” said Granger. “Nobody else knows. Him, and Dumbledore, and you. And now me.” She unfolded her hands, and put them flat on the books. “I thought you should know that I know. That’s all. I thought it would be worse if you found out some other way.”

*Him, and now you.* He had said it in an Owlery, in June, with straw round his feet and every owl in the place watching, and he had said it as though he were giving her something; and she had supposed, then, that he was. The one thing in the world that Lyra Malfoy knew and nobody else at Hogwarts did. She had carried it all summer and all autumn the way she carried the ring in her pocket, without ever taking it out, because there was nobody to take it out in front of.

It had been hers. And now it was Granger’s too, and Granger had got it out of him in four hours in an empty classroom, by not letting him leave.

She found, examining it, that what she felt was not jealousy, which would have been simple. It was something worse and more useful. It was respect.

“Four hours,” said Lyra.

“About that.”

“I needed four years,” said Lyra, “and a beetle, and a dragon, and a cup that went nowhere.”

Granger looked at her for a long moment. Then something happened to the corner of her mouth that was very nearly a smile, and was gone.

“I had the advantage,” said Granger. “The walls at Grimmauld Place are very thin, and his room was next to mine all summer. He talks in his sleep.” She picked up the top book from her pile, and opened it, and found her place. “He says your name, mostly. In case you were wondering.”

Lyra turned back to *Numerology and Grammatica*, and read the same page for another twenty minutes, and did not take in one word of it, and did not mind.

She saw how Potter got into Diggory’s group on the Thursday, in the Entrance Hall, at the end of lunch, with half the school going past.

She had known about the group for a fortnight. Everybody in the Squad had; it was the worst-kept secret in the castle. Cedric Diggory, who was Head Boy and still the most popular person at Hogwarts, had decided that if Umbridge would not teach them to defend themselves, he would, and he had been meeting a dozen or so seventh- and sixth-years somewhere on the seventh floor twice a week since half-term. Umbridge did not know yet. Lyra had not told her. She had been keeping it, as she kept most things this year, in case it was worth more later.

Potter was not in it. Everybody knew that, too. Diggory had not forgiven him for a hedge in June, and a curse in the back, and a cup; and he had said so, in front of witnesses, in the first week of term, quite pleasantly, in the voice of a man who would never say so again.

So when Potter came down the marble staircase on the Thursday with a heavy leather bag in his good hand, and crossed the Entrance Hall to where Diggory was standing with Cho Chang by the doors, and held the bag out to him, the whole Entrance Hall turned round to watch.

“It’s the prize money,” said Potter. He did not lower his voice. “From the Tournament. The thousand Galleons. It’s yours.”

Diggory looked at the bag. Then at Potter.

“It’s *yours*,” he said. “You won it.”

“I won it from behind,” said Potter. “Everybody knows that. You had your hand on it first.” He held the bag out a little further. “Take it.”

For a moment Lyra thought Diggory was going to. He looked at the bag for a long time, with an expression on his decent, handsome face that she could not read; and then he took it, out of Potter’s hand, and weighed it, and turned it upside down.

A thousand Galleons came out on to the flagstones of the Entrance Hall. They went everywhere: rolling, spinning, bouncing down the steps to the dungeons, under the feet of a crowd of first-years who shrieked and scattered and then, being first-years, began to pick them up.

“I don’t want your money, Potter,” said Diggory. “I wanted the Cup.”

He dropped the empty bag at Potter’s feet, and walked out through the great doors, and Cho Chang went after him.

Potter stood in the middle of the Entrance Hall in a sea of gold, with the whole school watching, and did not pick up a single coin.

It was the ugliest thing Lyra had ever seen him do, and the bravest, and she could not for the life of her have said which was which. She went down the dungeon steps with three Galleons in the toe of her shoe, where they had rolled, and left them on the bottom step for whoever wanted them.

It came out by the next week, by way of Parkinson, who had it from a Ravenclaw, who had it from somebody who was there, that Potter had gone up to the seventh floor that same night and stood outside a door in a bare wall for two hours until it opened; and that Diggory had come out, and looked at him, and told him that the only reason he was letting him through it was that he was the only person in the castle under twenty who could do a Patronus, and that if he ever turned his back on any of them in there, Diggory would know.

“Look,” said Draco.

It was after dinner on the Saturday. He had come and sat on the arm of her chair by the common-room fire, which he never did in front of the House, and he was holding something in his closed hand against his chest, and his face was alight with a secret so enormous that it was physically difficult for him to keep it inside.

He opened his hand. There was a Galleon in it.

“It’s not a real one,” said Draco, very low, delighted. “Look at the edge. It’s got numbers on, where the goblin numbers should be, and they *change*. And when it gets hot, that means there’s a meeting, and the numbers say when.” He shut his hand on it again. “I’m in a *club*.”

Lyra looked at his fist.

“What club?”

“A Defence club. A secret one. Diggory runs it. He’s *Head Boy*.” His voice had gone up; he brought it down again with a glance at the room. “Only seventh years and sixth years. And me. I’m the only first-year in the whole thing. We learnt Disarming on Thursday, I got Urquhart’s brother’s wand off him twice.”

“How,” said Lyra, “did a first-year get into a club for sixth and seventh years, Draco?”

He went pink with pleasure.

“Potter,” he said. “Harry Potter. He came and found me after Charms, on Wednesday, in the corridor, on my own. He said Diggory would let me in if he said so, because Diggory owes him, sort of. He gave me the coin himself.” He leaned closer. “And he said a thing. He said, if anybody ever asks you to go anywhere—anywhere at all, in the castle, at night, on your own—you’re to tell him first. Before you go. Him, not anybody else. And he’d come with me.” Draco considered this, with his head on one side. “I think he thinks I’ll get lost. Everybody thinks I get lost.”

Lyra sat very still in her chair.

*Anywhere at all, in the castle, at night, on your own.*

He had read it in her face. In a dark classroom on the third floor, with the moon on the floor between them, she had said *No* in the voice she had learnt on Thursday evenings, and given him nothing; and he had looked at her one moment longer than he should have, and said *Right*, and gone away; and three weeks later he had found her brother alone in a corridor after Charms and given him a coin that got warm.

It was a kind thing. It was the kindest thing anybody had done for Draco at Hogwarts. It meant that if an old man in a cottage ever sent her brother up seven flights of stairs at midnight to look for something, there would be somebody waiting at the top who knew what it was.

It also meant that Harry Potter had put a bell on her brother, like a cat, so that he would always know where Draco was going.

“Did you tell anybody?” said Lyra. “About the corridor. On the seventh floor.”

Draco’s face fell off its secret into guilt.

“No,” he said. “Course not. He didn’t ask about that. He just said *anywhere*.” He looked at her anxiously. “You won’t tell Father? About the club? He’d say Diggory’s a blood traitor. He’d make me leave.”

“I shan’t tell Father,” said Lyra.

He kissed her cheek, which he had not done in public since he was eight, and went away across the common room with his fist closed on his coin against his heart, beside the list.

She found Potter on her rounds that night, because he had made sure she would: on the fifth-floor landing, at the top of the marble staircase, sitting on the top step with his back against the banister and his cloak in a bundle beside him, waiting.

He got up when he saw her wand-light. He knew what she was going to say. She saw that he knew, and that he had come to be said it to, and that made it worse.

“You used my brother,” said Lyra.

Her voice came out low, and very clear, and it rang in the stairwell.

“Yes,” said Potter.

“He’s eleven.”

“I know how old he is.”

“He thinks you *like* him. He thinks it’s a *club*. He thinks he’s the cleverest first-year in the castle and that Harry Potter came and found him in a corridor because he’s *special*—”

“He is in danger,” said Potter. “And you lied to me.”

She stopped.

He did not raise his voice. He stood on the landing with his back to the staircase and his scarred hand at his side, and said it as he had once said *I’d like to see him in Azkaban*, without anything over the top of it to make it easier to carry.

“In October. In that room. I asked you if you knew anything about who was collecting his things, and you looked at me and said *no*, and I couldn’t tell. For the first time in four years I couldn’t tell if you were lying to me.” His jaw moved. “And then I went down to breakfast and your brother was telling Urquhart’s brother about a door in a wall on the seventh floor. The seventh floor, Lyra. Where there’s a room I know about that I shouldn’t, with things in it I know about that I shouldn’t, and I thought, *she knows*. She knows, and she’s not going to tell me, because it’s him. So I did the only thing I could think of.”

“You put a bell on him.”

“I put a *friend* on him. Somebody who’d be there if—”

“A bell.” She came up the last two stairs on to the landing, so that they were level. “You did to him exactly what you did to me. Don’t you see it? You did it for *four years*. You knew where everybody would be standing, and you made sure they stayed there, and you never told anybody why. You kept the whole world the shape you knew it, Potter, so you’d always know what came next. And I was standing where Draco stood, in that world, your world, and so everything that happened to him happened to me, and you watched it happen, and you let it, because if you’d moved me you’d have had to stop knowing.” Her voice shook. She let it. “And now you can’t see any more. And the first thing you do, the very first thing, is go and find my brother and put him on your board.”

Potter had gone white.

“That isn’t—”

“It *is*.”

“He’s *eleven*,” said Potter, and his voice broke on it, and she understood, too late, that he had not meant it as an argument for her side. “He’s eleven and an old man has given him a list, and I don’t know what’s on it, and I don’t know who the old man is, and I don’t know what happens to Draco if he finds what he’s looking for. Do you? Do you, Lyra?”

She did not.

They stood on the landing, a yard apart, in the light of her wand, and neither of them said anything for so long that the portrait of a sleeping wizard on the wall beside them woke up, and looked at them, and thought better of it, and went back to sleep.

They were both right. That was what she could not get past. She had stood in an Owlery in June and called him a liar for moving her about like furniture, and she had been right; and he had stood in a dark classroom in October and been lied to, by her, about her brother, and he had done the only thing a person could do who loved anybody and had been shut out; and he was right too; and there was nothing at all on the landing between them but the two of them being right, and it was the coldest thing she had ever stood next to.

“Don’t come near him again,” said Lyra.

“I can’t promise that.”

“I know,” said Lyra. “I didn’t think you could.”

She turned round and went down the marble staircase, and did not look back; and on the fourth flight she heard him say her name, once, quite quietly, from the top, and she did not stop.

She lay awake behind her hangings until the green light on the ceiling had gone from black to grey.

She had kept Draco. In a dark classroom she had given away the Dark Lord, and the house, and the coast, and the stone dogs she had put her hand in at seven, and she had kept her brother back, because he was hers, and because she had thought that if she did not look at what he was carrying, it could not hurt him.

It had not worked. It had only meant that somebody else had looked instead.

There was a sheet of old cream paper folded small against her brother’s heart, and something at the end of it that he had not found yet, and an old man waiting for him to find it; and she had been so busy not knowing what it was that Harry Potter had had to go round her to find out.

At some point before the grey turned white, she decided.

She was going to look.
