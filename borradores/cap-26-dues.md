# Chapter 26: Dues

For a moment nobody in the Great Hall seemed to know what to do with their faces.

There could not be a fourth champion. There were three schools, and three cups of flame, and an old man’s golden line on the floor that nobody under seventeen could cross; and the fourth champion was fourteen, and had been in his fourth year for exactly two months. Every head in the Hall had turned to the Gryffindor table, and every face had something different on it, so that looking along the benches was like looking along a shelf of jars in Snape’s office: astonishment, and envy, and suspicion, and a good deal of plain fright.

The one person who did not seem to have any of it was Potter. He looked annoyed.

“Professor?” He put his hand up, as if he were in a lesson. He did not stand. “I think there’s been a mistake. I never put my name in that.”

“You did not?” said Dumbledore.

“No. Honestly. Hand on heart.” He did not take his elbows off the table. If anything he leaned back. He had very much the air of somebody who had been asked to go up and collect a prize he did not want, and was waiting to see whether he could get out of it by staying put.

Lyra closed her eyes.

She did not need them. She knew that voice, and she knew what it would do next, and she found she would rather not watch it do it. So she heard it instead: the Hall drawing breath, and a bench scraping somewhere along the Hufflepuff table, where ten seconds ago they had had their first champion in living memory and had just been told, as far as they could tell, that they would have to share him with Harry Potter, as Harry Potter had to have everything.

“Liar.” It was not loud, but it was the kind of silence that carried. Zacharias Smith: she knew the voice from Herbology, where it disapproved of things. “He’s cheated his way over the line somehow. He’ll have got *special treatment*. Because he’s the Boy Who Lived—”

“Sorry, it’s—Zacharias, isn’t it?” said Potter. “Zebedee? Something like that.” Somebody near Smith sniggered and stopped. “Why would I want to be in it? Seriously. I don’t need special treatment for something I don’t even want.”

“The prize is a thousand Galleons! And the glory—”

“I’ve got the money my dad left me,” said Potter, “and I’ve already got more fame than I know what to do with. The only thing I’m short of is time. So why would I?”

It was entirely true, every word of it, and that was the trouble. There were moments when the truth, said in a certain voice, was simply another way of hitting somebody, and Potter had never in his life been able to tell those moments from the others. She could hear the Durmstrang students muttering to each other in a language she did not know, and the Beauxbatons girls demanding to know, very audibly and in French, why Hogwarts should have two.

“*Imbeciles!*” Professor Moody’s growl went across all of it like a cart over gravel. “It’s a plot. Can’t you see that? Somebody’s put a Confundus Charm on that Goblet, and a strong one. Give me that, Dumbledore.” A wooden leg on the flagstones, going up to the front; a pause. “Can’t make out the hand. It’s been done with an Auto-Answer Quill, or something like it. Thorough. I’ll give them that.”

“He’s hidden his writing, then!” Smith was on his feet; she heard the bench go. “Potter did, so nobody could—”

“Why would I hide my handwriting,” said Potter, “to write my own name?”

It was perfectly logical. A few of the people round Smith might even have nodded, if Potter had stopped there.

“Next time you want to call somebody a cheat, Zebedee,” said Potter, “pick one who wanted the prize.”

Lyra did not open her eyes for the uproar that followed. She knew exactly what it would look like: Hufflepuff on its feet, Durmstrang and Beauxbatons on theirs, and the Slytherins happily on everybody’s side but his. Two minutes ago he had been the boy who could not possibly be in the Tournament. Now he had made enemies of four-fifths of the Hall without getting out of his chair.

“*Silence!*”

Dumbledore did not shout often. The Hall went quiet, although Lyra could feel the anger still going on underneath, waiting.

“Harry,” said Dumbledore, quite gently. “When the Goblet gives up a name, that champion is bound by a magical contract. Whatever the rights and wrongs of it, you must come with me now. Through here, please.”

Lyra opened her eyes. Potter sighed, and got up at last, and ran a hand through his hair, which made it worse. He waved to the Gryffindor table on his way, and only the Weasley twins waved back. Granger was white and anxious. Weasley was not looking at him. His ears were red, and he was staring at his empty plate as though it had said something.

Potter went through the door behind the staff table, and Professor McGonagall rose before the Hall could start up again.

“Champions’ business will be announced in due course! Everybody else, *bed*!”

Lyra got up with the rest of Slytherin. She went down the dungeon steps with Crabbe on one side and Goyle on the other and Parkinson behind them, explaining to everybody that Potter had obviously bribed somebody, and she said nothing to any of them.

An hour later, in the common room, with Parkinson still explaining to the fire who Potter must have bribed, the ring in her pocket began to flash.

She felt it before she knew what it was: a pulse against her fingers, on, off, on, warm and then cool and then warm again. The other ring was looking for her. It had done this twice in October, and both times she had taken her hand out of her pocket and gone the other way.

Lyra stood up.

“Where are you going?” said Crabbe. “It’s gone ten.”

“I’ve left my scarf in the Great Hall.”

Lyra had not been wearing a scarf. Crabbe looked at her neck, and then at her face, and then turned back to the fire and asked Parkinson a question about Durmstrang that would keep him busy for half an hour, which was exactly like Crabbe.

At the top of the dungeon steps she turned slowly on the spot, with her hand closed round the ring in her pocket, the way she had turned on the castle steps in February, and when she faced the marble staircase it grew warm.

She followed it. Up the marble staircase, and along the first-floor corridor, past the portraits settling down for the night, until it was so warm against her palm that it was almost hot; and there was a classroom door standing an inch ajar that had certainly been shut when she passed it that morning.

She went in. The door closed behind her of its own accord, and a voice from the air beside her said, “*Colloportus*,” and then, “Hello.”

“Take that thing off,” said Lyra. “I want to see your face.”

He pulled the cloak off. He looked tired, and his tie was crooked, and his hair was standing up at the back where he had been pushing his hand through it.

“You walked off,” he said. “Twice. In October.”

“You’d only have not answered.”

He did not argue with that.

“Withdraw,” said Lyra. “Tonight. Go back to Dumbledore and tell him you won’t do it.”

“I did tell him.” Potter leaned back against a desk. “I told all of them. Bagman and Crouch were in there, they’re the judges. They say the Goblet’s a binding magical contract. Your name comes out, you compete. There isn’t a way out of it.”

“Are you stupid, or are you only naïve? *That is why you should withdraw.*” She came across the room at him and put one finger against his chest, hard. “Can’t you feel how much somebody wants you stuck in this, with no way out? Somebody put your name in that cup. Somebody wants you in a tournament that killed so many people they had to stop holding it for two hundred years. They want you hurt. They want you *dead*. You’re a fourth-year. You haven’t learned half the magic you’d need. What do you think you are going to *do*?”

They were very nearly her mother’s words.

Potter was looking down at the finger on his chest. When he looked up, he was smiling.

“You’re worried about me. And saying so. That’s new.”

“Don’t you dare try to talk your way round this, Harry Potter. As if I would ever be worried about somebody like—”

She stopped.

He was not somebody like anything. He was hers, in secret, with a ring to prove it.

“Of course I’m worried,” she said, and glared at him as though it were his fault, which it was. “A little. A very little. It would be odd if I weren’t.”

He had not expected that. She watched him not expect it, and enjoyed it.

“Dumbledore said the same thing,” he said, after a moment, more quietly. “Moody, too. That it’s a trap. Somebody wants me hurt in the Tournament, where it’ll look like an accident.”

So she was not cleverer than Dumbledore and Mad-Eye Moody put together. Lyra found she minded that much less than she would have expected. If the two of them had already seen it, they would already be doing something about it.

“Then that’s settled,” she said. “You go through the motions. You stand in the middle of the arena and do nothing, and let them give you no marks.”

He did not say anything. He looked at her sideways, and she knew that look. She had seen it across classrooms for three years, and it had never once meant *yes*.

“No,” said Lyra slowly. “You’re going to do it properly. Aren’t you. You’re going to stand there in the middle of it on purpose and let them come. Because if somebody wants to hurt you in the Tournament, they’ll have to do it *during* the Tournament, where you can see them.”

“Well,” said Potter. “Yes.”

“Are you *mad*?” Her voice had gone up and she did not care. “Do you think being the Boy Who Lived means you get spare lives? You and Dumbledore, the pair of you, you’re—”

“It’s the other way round.” He took hold of her shoulders. She was shaking, a little, under his hands; she could feel it, and so could he. “I’m doing it to keep me safe. And the people round me. If I pull out, do you think whoever it is gives up? They try something else. Poison in my pumpkin juice. A curse in a corridor. I’d rather have it in the Tournament, where I know when it’s coming.”

First year, a man with the Dark Lord growing out of the back of his head. Second year, a diary. Third year, a hundred Dementors with their hoods turned up at him. Lyra had never once in her life had to wonder whether somebody was going to try to kill her this year, and she found that she could not imagine it, and that he could not remember anything else.

She put her forehead against his chest, hard, as if she were knocking on a door.

“Don’t you get hurt.” She did it again. Then she looked up. “If you get hurt, I’ll kill you.”

“Then I’d better not,” said Potter. “I wouldn’t get to see your face.”

He always had an answer. She took two fistfuls of his robes and pushed her forehead into the place between them and stayed there, furious. After a moment she felt his hands leave her shoulders, slowly, as if he were going to put his arms round her—

There was something on his chest. A small round badge, pinned to his robes, with four letters on it: S.P.E.W.

She knocked his hand away and took hold of the badge between finger and thumb.

“What,” said Lyra, “is this?”

“What? *Now?*”

She knew she had seen it before. She went looking for where, and found it: the Gryffindor table, which she did not look at, and Granger sitting in her usual place at Potter’s elbow, with exactly this badge on her chest.

“Granger,” said Lyra. “*Again*?”

It was not a tender voice.

“No—no, it’s not, it’s the Society for the Promotion of Elfish Welfare,” said Potter, very fast. “House-elves. For better conditions. Hermione started it. I’m member number two.” He brightened, as if a thought had struck him. “You could be number three. It’s two Sickles to join.”

Lyra stared at him. Mercifully the name was so absurd that she was too baffled to be jealous.

“It’s pointless,” she said. “House-elves are born to serve wizards. That’s what they are. Free them? The wizards don’t want it, and the elves would want it even less.”

“Except Dobby.”

“Except Dobby. Who is peculiar. You can’t found a society on one peculiar elf.” She was warming to it. “And you needn’t tell me about Dobby, Potter. He was *ours*. When Pobble came to us, she cried for a week in the scullery, because she was quite sure we would send her back. Her old family couldn’t keep her. That was the worst thing that had ever happened to her: not being wanted any more.” She folded her arms. “You can’t set a thing free that is crying to stay.”

He had no answer to Pobble. She watched him not have one, and enjoyed it.

He looked away. “No. Maybe you’re right. Setting them all free would be crueller than anything. I just think—they ought to be treated a bit better. They’re not furniture. They’ve got feelings.” He was losing, and he knew it, and he put down the only card he had left, a little too quickly. “And it’s different, the loyalty you get because there’s a contract, and the sort you get because somebody means it. I saw that this summer, when we went to find the Horcr—”

He stopped.

Lyra did not say anything. She did not have to. She was looking at him, and he could see what she was looking at him with.

“*Horcrux*,” she said. “*This summer*?”

Potter whistled two notes of something and examined the ceiling. “What did I say? I said Hog’s Head, didn’t I?”

“Explain,” said Lyra. “Now.”

He told her.

Before the World Cup, he and Sirius had gone through the house Sirius had grown up in, top to bottom. Every Black still on the tapestry had been on the Dark Lord’s side, and Sirius was not on it, who had been burnt off the tapestry for it; and the Dark Lord had trusted one of his pieces to the Malfoys, so it had seemed worth asking whether he had trusted another to the Blacks. Sirius’s younger brother had been the most devoted of the lot of them. So they had followed what was left of Regulus Black, and it had led them to the Blacks’ house-elf, an old creature called Kreacher, who had not wanted to tell them anything, and who had to obey Sirius, because Sirius was the last of the family.

“It wasn’t very nice,” said Potter. “Getting it out of him. Sirius isn’t—he doesn’t like that house. Or anything in it.” He rubbed the back of his neck. “Kreacher took us to a cave. On the coast, up a cliff. Dumbledore came too.”

He told her about the cave. He told it plainly, without dressing it up, and that was worse. A wall that would only open if you paid it in blood. A black lake inside, so still it did not look like water, with dead things lying under the surface, waiting, and a little boat that would only carry one wizard at a time. And in the middle of the lake, on an island of rock, a stone basin full of something that you had to drink, all of it, before the thing at the bottom would let itself be taken.

Lyra stood there with her hands wrapped in her own sleeves. He was standing in front of her, whole, with his tie crooked. She knew that. Her palms were wet anyway.

“Sirius wanted Kreacher to drink it,” said Potter. “I didn’t think that was right. So I was looking round, and at the edge of the rock there was—” He stopped for a second. “It was Regulus. What was left of him. He’d been lying there fifteen years.”

The elf had seen it and begun to howl. And Dumbledore had knelt down beside the creature, on the wet rock, and asked it questions very gently, until the whole thing came out. Regulus Black had found out what the Dark Lord had done to his own soul, and had turned against him. He had come to that cave to steal the thing and destroy it, and he had drunk the basin dry to get it, and had known when he had done it that he was not going to be able to get back across the lake. So he had given it to his elf, and ordered him to take it home and destroy it, and to tell nobody, and to go. And Kreacher had gone, because he had been told to, and left his master lying on the rock.

“Kreacher tried,” said Potter. “For fifteen years. On his own. He tried everything he could think of, and he couldn’t do it, and he never told a soul, because he’d been told not to. Dumbledore promised him we’d finish it for Regulus. And then he let him off the order.” He looked down at his hands. “It was a locket. We destroyed it.” He stopped. “Dumbledore drank the stuff in the basin, in the end. He wouldn’t let anybody else. Kreacher had the locket, so it wasn’t for that. There was a copy at the bottom, a fake, with a bit of paper folded up inside it. Regulus’s writing.” He looked at his hands. “Dumbledore’s kept it.”

He looked almost proud, she thought, and almost sick, and was trying to be only one of them.

“When?” said Lyra.

“August. Middle of the night.” He thought about it. “A Tuesday.”

At a quarter past two on a Tuesday morning in August, a ring in Wiltshire had gone gold, and she had lain back on her pillow with the satisfied feeling of somebody who has read the last page of a book first. She had not read anything at all.

Lyra stood with that for a while. *He changed his mind about something, and it killed him.* Her mother had written that in a small upright hand, a year ago, from a hired owl, and told her to burn it. And her father had written, in a letter hidden inside a letter, *the most devoted of us all*. She had sat behind her bed-curtains with the two of them side by side and decided that one of her parents was lying, or wrong, or had not been told.

They had both been right.

“My father wrote to me that he was the most devoted of all of them,” she said.

“He was,” said Potter. “Right up until he wasn’t.”

She looked at the badge, still pinned to his chest, and thought about an old elf alone in a dark house for fifteen years, trying to break a thing he had been ordered to break, and failing, and trying again, and telling nobody.

“That isn’t loyalty from the heart,” she said. “That’s an order. An elf’s bound by what its master tells it. It doesn’t matter if the master’s been dead fifteen years. It would have gone on trying till it died.”

“Is that what an ordinary witch would say?”

“Naturally.”

Regulus Black had been fond of his elf, she supposed. All the same he would have taken its obedience for granted, as everyone did, as he had taken for granted the air. That was what being a wizard meant. It did not mean that an elf who had done a thing like that should go unacknowledged.

“Give me that,” said Lyra.

“What?”

“Two Sickles to join? How very small. I’ll give you two Galleons.” She unpinned the badge from his chest without asking, and it came away still faintly warm. “Don’t imagine I agree with a single one of Granger’s ridiculous ideas. This is—acknowledgement. My mother was a Black before she married. You can tell the elf that Lyra Malfoy, of the blood of the Blacks, acknowledges his service.”

She turned the badge over in her fingers, and then put it in her pocket, next to the ring, and looked away.

“And don’t wear that sort of thing.”

Potter made a noise somewhere between a laugh and a groan. “Why do you always *do* this when I can’t—”

“Can’t what?”

“Nothing. Forget it.”

She reached up and straightened his tie, since somebody had to, and brushed down the front of his robes where her forehead had creased them. Then she kept her hands there, flat against his chest, a moment longer than the robes required.

“You knew,” she said. “In May. *Especially next year. If students come from other schools.* And at the World Cup. You told me to go back to my mother before anything had happened. You knew.” She made herself ask the rest. “And tonight? Did you know about tonight?”

He did not pretend not to understand her.

“I can’t tell you that one,” he said.

“Can’t, or won’t?”

“Can’t.”

She looked at him for a long time. He held it, and did not reach for a joke, and did not touch his scar; and the worst of it was that she believed him. It would have been a great deal easier to be angry with somebody who would not.

“Then I’ll tell you something,” said Lyra, “since we’re telling things.”

There was a thing she had been carrying since February that had got heavier, not lighter, in a castle where there was no longer anybody it could be paid to.

“Lupin’s potion. The night of the full moon. Snape asked me to take it up to him.” She kept her eyes on his tie. “There was a line of prints across his desk. Little ones. Something had been in the cauldron and climbed out again. I saw them, Harry. I looked at them, and I thought, a rat’s been in it, and there wasn’t time to make more, and whoever drinks it need never know. So I wiped the bottom of the goblet on Snape’s blotter, and I took it up, and I watched him drink every drop.”

There was a silence in the empty classroom.

“Pettigrew put the neutraliser in it,” said Potter at last. “You heard him say so. On the shore.”

“I carried it.”

“I know you did,” said Potter. “I watched you, from under the cloak. You came round the corner with it, and your little finger was sticking out.”

She had not expected him to know that. She looked up.

He did not say that it was all right. She had been afraid he would, and she would have had to hate him for it. He looked at her for a while, quite seriously, as he had looked at her once across a library table with the lamp between them, and he did not make it smaller than it was.

“You should write to him,” he said. “Lupin. Tell him. He’d rather know.”

“He’ll hate me.”

“He won’t.” Potter paused. “But you should write, anyway. It isn’t the sort of thing you put chocolate outside a door for.”

She opened her mouth to tell him that she had never put anything outside anybody’s door. Then she shut it again, because he had obviously been told, and because he was right.

Standing there with her hands on his chest, she noticed that nowhere, in the whole of it, had she done the sum.

She did not know what that was. She was not sure she liked not knowing.

“I should go,” said Lyra, and took her hands away. “Crabbe will have the whole common room out looking for a scarf I wasn’t wearing.”

“Me too. Ron’ll—” He stopped. Lyra thought of Weasley in the Great Hall, staring at his empty plate. “Somebody’ll want to know what happened,” said Potter.

She went to the door, and unlocked it with her own wand, and looked out into the corridor. It was empty. She slipped half through the gap, and stopped.

“Potter. Don’t try too hard in this Tournament. If it goes badly, give up.”

“What an original pep talk,” said Potter. “I’ll bear it in mind.”

She went down the marble staircase in the dark with the badge and the ring together in one pocket.

Advice was not going to be enough. She knew that perfectly well. Somebody in this castle had put Potter’s name into a cup of fire, because they wanted him dead, and Dumbledore was looking for whoever it was, and so was Mad-Eye Moody, and so was Potter, from the middle of an arena with a target painted on him. None of them would find it in Slytherin. Nobody in Slytherin would tell a Gryffindor anything, or a teacher, or an Auror who had sent half their fathers to Azkaban.

They might tell a Malfoy. They might tell Lucius Malfoy’s daughter almost anything, if she asked them the right way, and she had been brought up knowing the right way.

Lyra reached the bottom of the dungeon steps and stood there for a moment, in the cold, with her hand round the ring.

Then she gave the password to the wall, and went in to begin.
