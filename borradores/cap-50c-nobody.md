# Chapter 53: Nobody

By the Sunday morning she had looked at it from the other side.

*He did not answer for you. He did not think to.* On the Saturday night she had held on to that as one holds on to a rail. But five names had been given in that room, and there was somebody in it who asked everybody everything, in turn, and was in no hurry. Sooner or later he would get to the schoolgirl. He would ask what she had been doing for him since September. And the answer was that she had told Dolores Umbridge a handful of true things not worth a Knut; and that Professor Umbridge, who wrote to Wiltshire on the first of every month, had begun to call her *reliable* in the tone one uses of a quiet horse.

She needed to have done something. It had to be real, and it had to have cost somebody, and there had to be a letter about it on her father’s desk by the first of December.

There was Diggory’s group, which two sixth-years had been arguing about on the stairs. She looked at that for some time and put it back. It would bring the castle down on half the sixth year, and she might yet need it.

And there was what Crabbe had told her on the Friday, in the dormitory, in her low reasonable voice, looking at the floor. Her cousin again. One of the Hufflepuff boys had started keeping a list: who had been in that office, and for how long, and what was on their hands. He had nine names so far. When it was long enough he meant to send it to the Governors. *I just thought you’d want to know*, Crabbe had said, and had very nearly smiled. *Being a prefect.* She had seen the jar. She thought she knew which side of this Lyra was on.

It was a very small thing. That was what recommended it. It was one boy and a notebook. He would get a week of lines and a letter home. People got lines.

She knew what lines were. She knew it with the ends of her fingers, from the knuckles to the wrist. She noticed that, and went on doing up her buttons.

“Wayne Hopkins,” said Lyra.

It was ten minutes to eight on the Monday morning, and the kittens were at their breakfast. It took no time at all. She had wondered whether it would.

“Hopkins, dear?”

“A fifth-year in Hufflepuff, Professor. He is keeping a list of the pupils who have been in detention with you, and of their injuries. I understand that he means to write to the Governors. It may be nothing.”

Professor Umbridge put down her little pink cup. She looked at Lyra with her head on one side, and her wide mouth spread very slowly, until she looked like somebody who has opened a parcel and found exactly the right size of cardigan.

“Oh, you *are* a good girl,” she said. “I shall write to your father this very evening. One never likes to say so, but I had begun to wonder.” She patted Lyra’s hand. Hers was slightly damp. “You’ll sit with me, won’t you, dear? Tomorrow at five. I do think it’s so important to see a thing through.”

“Of course, Professor.”

She said it at once.

⁂

He said sorry when he came in.

He had caught the door with his bag, and it bumped the leg of the second chair, which was pink, and which Umbridge had had brought in and set beside her own with a small table between them. He said “Sorry” before he had seen who was in it. He had grown a foot since the queue outside Charms. He had a long, mild, rather spotty face, and his tie was done up properly, and in five years she had spoken to him perhaps twice. Then he saw her, and her badge, and the plate on the small table, and he looked at Umbridge, and she watched him work out what kind of room he was in.

The notebook was already on the desk. Umbridge turned its pages with the tips of her fingers. She read some of the names aloud, with little sighs, beginning at the top: *Potter, H.* Something slid out from between the leaves and fell on to the lace cloth, and she picked it up, and held it out to him across the desk.

“Yours, dear. What a funny bookmark.”

It was a Chocolate Frog card. It had been bent in half, a long time ago, and somebody had flattened it out again as well as could be done. Agrippa was frowning. There was a mark across his face about the size of the heel of a small shoe.

Hopkins took it and put it in his pocket. He did not look at her. It would have been easier, she found, if he had.

“*I must not tell tales*,” said Professor Umbridge. “Shall we say until it sinks in? Do have a wafer, dear.”

They were pink. Lyra had not eaten anything to speak of since the Saturday; Goyle had been trying for two days. She took one, since she was being watched, and bit it. Across the room, at the table under the window, Wayne Hopkins picked up the quill and began.

She knew the sound now.

It was thin, and wet, and it went on. At about the fourth line he stopped, and turned his hand over, and looked at the back of it, with his mild face gone slack and puzzled, like somebody who has put his foot on a stair that was not there. Nobody had told him. Nobody ever told them. He looked up at Umbridge, who smiled at him and lifted her cup. He bent his head again.

Lyra finished the wafer. She ate all of it. It seemed that one could.

He came on the Wednesday, and on the Thursday. By the Wednesday he was holding the quill in his fist. By the Thursday he had to lift it with his other hand and fit it between his fingers, and when it dripped on the pink cloth he said “Sorry,” quite quietly, to the cloth. There was a perfectly good third shelf in the Potions storeroom. She walked past the door twice. It would have gone in the book, in her hand, and there was a woman with a black bow sitting eighteen inches from her elbow, and she had told the only other person in the world who knew about that shelf to keep the jar.

At six o’clock Umbridge took the quill away herself, out of fingers that had stopped closing on it, and said she really thought it had sunk in.

On the Friday morning Cedric Diggory was cutting up Wayne Hopkins’s sausage.

He did it without any expression at all, very neatly, in the middle of the Hufflepuff table, with the Head Boy’s badge on his chest. Hopkins’s right hand was under the table. Perhaps a dozen people had noticed. Lyra noticed Potter notice. She saw him put down his toast.

He got up and came across the Great Hall, not quickly, between the tables, and the noise went down a little behind him, as it had done all his life. He stopped behind Hopkins. He felt in his robes and took out a small stoppered jar.

She knew it from where she sat. Third shelf on the left; one ounce. It was about a quarter full. He had been carrying it for five weeks.

He set it down on the table, a little way from Hopkins’s plate. Then he put one finger against it and pushed it along the wood, an inch, and another inch, until it stood exactly at the edge of the plate; and he took his finger away.

There were two people in that room who knew what they had just seen. He lifted his head, and looked along the length of the Hall, past Ravenclaw, and found her.

He did not look angry. She had told him once, in an Owlery, that he did not look at any of them as he looked at her. He had always looked at her as though he had no idea what she would do next. He looked at Snape like this. He looked at Umbridge like this. He looked at her for about as long as it takes to read a line one already knows, and then he went back to his toast.

Lyra buttered hers. It wanted doing. Beside her, Crabbe had put down her fork.

“I told you that,” said Crabbe.

It was very low. It was not a question. Lyra went on with the butter, right out to the corners. After a moment Crabbe picked up her plate and her cup, and moved three places down the bench, and sat down next to a second-year with her back very straight.

Goyle looked from one of them to the other. Her big soft face had gone quite frightened. She took hold of the toast rack and pushed it nearer to Lyra.

“Eat something,” said Goyle.

She did not wait to see whether Lyra would. She looked down the Hall for some time, at a long mild boy and a sausage cut into very small pieces, with the expression Lyra had seen her turn on a difficult pastry. Then she wrapped two rolls in a napkin, and got up, and went out by the side door, towards the kitchens.

He was in the window on the sixth floor that night. She had not moved her rounds. It would have looked like something.

He was not sitting. He stood in the embrasure with the moon behind him and his hands at his sides, and she got out her book, because it was something to hold.

“You’re out of bed, Potter.”

“He can’t hold a spoon.”

“I saw.”

“I know you saw. He told me. He says you were in the other chair, all three nights.” His voice was quite quiet. “He says you had a biscuit.”

She had got ready for shouting. She had been ready for it since breakfast. She opened the book, and looked at the page, and heard herself.

“Three,” said Lyra. “She’s very pressing.”

It took a moment to go in. She watched it. It was one of the worst moments of her life, and she would have given a good deal to say it again.

“Who told her?”

“I should think you know.”

“I want you to say it.”

“I told her.” She looked up. “On Monday, before breakfast. His name, his year and what he had in his notebook. Should you like it in writing? I have a very clear hand.”

“*Why?*”

She had not meant to tell him. She had meant to have a great deal of dignity and no reasons whatever. But he had looked at her that morning down the length of the Great Hall as if she were something he had read before, and she found that she was so angry she could hardly see.

“Because somebody gave a house in Northumberland to Albus Dumbledore,” said Lyra, “and his people went and stood about on the cliffs in the fog, like a party of bird-watchers, and were seen. Did you imagine that that would be the end of it? He has been going round the table, Potter. *Who knew the word? Whom did you tell?* There are five names. My father answered for himself and for my mother. Would you care to guess who he didn’t answer for?”

She saw that reach him. He had not known; of course he had not known. It went across his face, and for an instant he looked as he had looked on the third floor with his hand held out.

“You should’ve told me.”

“So that you could do what? Keep me safe inside it?” Her voice had begun to shake, and she flattened it. “You wanted the north. You said *please*. I gave it to you, and this is what it cost, and it has a name, and it’s in Hufflepuff. What did you think it cost? Did you suppose it was free?”

“Don’t you put that on me.”

“Why not? You’ve plenty of room. You let a girl write to a book for a year so that you’d know what came next. How many was it, all told? Have you got a list? I should get one, they’re all the rage. You’ll forgive me if I don’t take lessons in who can be spent from *you*.”

“He hadn’t done anything to—”

“He’s *nobody!*”

It rang along the corridor and came back.

“He’s nobody, Potter. He’s a boy with a notebook and a hand that will heal, and I have a brother—”

She did not see him move. That was what she remembered afterwards. There had been a yard of moonlit floor between them. Then the book was on the flagstones, and her back had met the stone beside the window with a thump that knocked the breath out of her, and he had her by the wrist.

“Take that back,” said Potter.

He said it very quietly.

She was eleven. There was a corridor, and Crabbe on the floor, and her own wand in his other hand, held low and loose; and she would have told anybody that she had forgotten about it years ago. Her wrist had not. It seemed that it had been waiting. The blood went out of her face all at once, and she could not find her knees, and she could not get any air. She was fifteen. She was a prefect, with a wand up her sleeve. She stood against the wall and looked at him, and she could not have lifted a finger.

It was the same voice. They were the same three words.

His hand was right round her wrist, and the moon was on the back of it. *I must not tell lies*, from the knuckles down; and underneath it, smaller, the other three. They were about an inch from her pulse. She could read them perfectly well.

She saw him see her face.

It took him a moment. He looked at her. Then he looked down at his own hand, slowly, as if somebody had brought it to him; and there were red marks coming up on her skin at the edges of his fingers.

He let go of her as though she had burnt him. He went back a step, and another, until the far wall stopped him, and he stood there holding the hand a little away from his side. She had seen him after the dragon. She had never seen him that colour.

“Lyra,” said Potter.

There did not seem to be any air on the sixth floor. She could hear him looking for it.

“I didn’t— I wouldn’t ever—”

She bent down and picked up her book. It took some time. She found the page, and took out her quill, and stood with it over the line, as she had stood on a good many nights that term with her hair coming down. The ink dried on the nib while she looked at it. There did not seem to be a word for it on page nine.

She shut the book.

“Aren’t you—” It had hardly any voice in it. He had got his back against the wall, and his eyes had gone to the book in her hand, and she understood that he was asking her to. He actually wanted her to. “Malfoy. Aren’t you going to—”

She drew down her cuff and did up the button. She went along the corridor to the stairs, at the ordinary pace of a prefect on her rounds, with her wrist held in her other hand, inside the sleeve, where she had once carried letters.

Nobody lost any points that night.

Nobody lost any on the Saturday, or on the Sunday. On the Monday morning the rubies stood exactly where they had stood on the Friday, for the first time since the start of term; and she heard Weasley explaining to Thomas, all down the length of the Gryffindor table, with his ears pink with quiet pride, that it just went to show what you could do with a graph.
