# Chapter 50: In Fives

“Insolence,” read Cedric Diggory. “Insolence. Insolence. Insolence, after hours. Insolent use of a window seat.” He turned a page of the prefects’ book, and his decent, handsome face showed no expression of any kind. “Breathing.”

“He was doing it at me,” said Lyra.

It was the fourth Monday of October, and they were in the prefects’ room behind the Charms corridor, a small stuffy place with a round table in it. Every prefect in the school was present, and nobody had touched the biscuits. Weasley was on his feet. He had been on his feet for ten minutes, with his ears the colour of a fire engine and his badge pinned on straight, and he had in his hand a sheet of parchment on which somebody had plainly been helping him with the columns.

“Seventy points!” said Weasley. “*Seventy*, Diggory. Since the start of term. Off one bloke. We’re bottom of the whole castle, we’re behind *Hufflepuff*—sorry—and it’s all her. I’ve looked. Every time you turn round, there’s another five gone. It goes off in the night. You come down to breakfast and the thing’s dropped again, and nobody knows why, and she’s sitting over there eating a boiled egg.”

Lyra took a small sip of her tea.

“A prefect may deduct up to five points at a time for any breach of discipline,” she said. “It’s Educational Decree Twenty-Four, page nine. I find that Potter is often in breach. I can’t think why he isn’t more careful.”

“*Insolent use of a window seat?*”

“You weren’t there, Weasley. I assure you it was very insolent.”

Beside her, Zabini appeared to have got something in his throat.

Diggory shut the book. He looked at her across the table for a moment with no interest whatever. He had been doing that since the train, and she had got used to it, much as one gets used to a stone in a shoe. It was a curious thing, she reflected, but it had stopped bothering her at about the same time that everything else had.

“Your entries are in order, Malfoy,” said Diggory. “I’d ask you to think about whether they’re in proportion. Weasley, sit down. Next week’s rota.”

Weasley sat. He glared at her over his parchment. There was nothing of last spring in it, and nothing of the train either. Granger, next to him, had said nothing at all. She had the parchment’s twin in front of her, and she was looking down at it, with her chin on her hand, and every so often she made a small neat tick.

Lyra finished her tea. She was, she noticed, in an excellent temper. She had been in an excellent temper for some weeks.

It was an extraordinary thing, to be on the other side. She had supposed it would be cold. She was Lucius Malfoy’s daughter and Dolores Umbridge’s dear; she had been spat at, or very nearly, by a third-year in the Charms corridor. She went to Snape on Thursdays and had her head walked through. She had given a boy the name of a house by the sea, and she had not been able to look at the *Prophet* since. And about twice a week, for about a minute and a half, she was on the seventh floor, or the sixth, or once behind the suit of armour by the Astronomy stairs; and she came away from it with her hair coming down and her heart going, and took out her book, and wrote *Insolence*.

It meant nothing. She had made that perfectly clear. She made it clear every time.

“That was the last one,” she had told him, one Tuesday.

“This week,” Potter had said.

“It’s *Tuesday*.”

The Thursday after that, by the stairs, it had been “Before lunch.”

They were not all funny. She did not put that in the book. There was a night behind the suit of armour when he asked her what she had given Umbridge that week, and she told him, and he said “You could stop,” and she took hold of the front of his dressing gown and asked whether he would care to tell her father or whether she should. Her hands were shaking. He had no right to be the only person in the castle who looked at her like that, and she could not seem to stop coming up here to be looked at.

“*Them*,” said Potter, quite quietly.

“Yes,” said Lyra. “*Them*.”

Neither of them could have said afterwards who started it. It was the same argument, by other means. She wrote *Insolence*, and for once it was the plain truth.

On Thursdays, after Snape, there were cards.

It was Zabini’s game. It had started in the autumn of the Tournament, when somebody had to entertain the Bulgarians, and it had outlived them: a table behind the pillar in the common room, with the green lamp pulled down, a pack that had been through a great deal, and a running score in the back of Zabini’s Charms notes that nobody was allowed to see. Lyra came to it with her head ringing, and took the chair with its back to the wall. She did not play very well on Thursdays. Nobody mentioned it.

Goyle had a tin. She put it on the table without saying anything, took off the lid, and sat on her hands. It was fruit cake, cut in small squares. Zabini laid down his cards. He took a square between finger and thumb and looked at it on both sides; he ate it slowly, with his eyes shut, as his mother was understood to taste Burgundy.

“Six,” said Zabini.

Goyle’s big soft face fell about an inch.

“It was five last week. It wants more of the orange.” He took another. “Six and a half.”

“You can’t have a half.”

“It’s my scale, Greer.”

Draco, who had no business being up, had got his chin on the arm of Lyra’s chair and was looking at the tin with his whole soul. Goyle pushed it an inch towards him without turning her head.

Nott dealt. He played cards as he played chess, like somebody drafting a lease; and across from him Crabbe, who had been looking at her hand for some time with her slow, careful, thorough look, laid it down one card after another, and took eleven Sickles off him. Nott looked at the cards, and then at Crabbe. It was the first time that Lyra had seen his teeth.

“That’s twice,” he said. “Who taught you that?”

“Nobody,” said Crabbe. She had gone a little pink along the jaw. “I watch.”

Lyra picked up her bag. She was extremely tired, and there had been something she had been meaning to ask somebody about butter. “You’ll carry this up in the morning, Nott,” she said; and he said, “Of course,” and stopped smiling, and picked up his cards. Crabbe looked at hers.

She found out about Lavender Brown on the Saturday.

It was nothing. She wanted that understood. She had come down to the lake path with Crabbe and Goyle on the first cold bright morning of the term, for the air, and there were a number of Gryffindors on the bank with a Quaffle; and Potter was among them, in a jumper, with his sleeves pushed up, being taught something by Lavender Brown. It seemed to involve a charm for covering spots. It appeared to need a great deal of hand-holding. She had his right hand, the marked one, in both of hers. She was looking at the back of it, and saying something, with her head on one side, and her very ordinary brown hair blowing about; and Potter, who had not once in five years listened to a word anybody said about his complexion, was laughing.

“Are you all right?” said Goyle. “You’ve gone a funny colour. Have you eaten?”

“I am perfectly well.”

“Only you’ve bent your spoon,” said Crabbe.

Lyra looked down. She had brought a yoghurt. She laid the spoon on the grass, with a certain care, and asked Greer whether there was anything left in the bag.

It was intolerable. She knew exactly how much it was worth. Nobody on that bank mattered in the slightest. She had kissed the boy on Thursday and she had told him that it was over, and she had no more right to mind than the giant squid. If he chose to go about having his hand held by a girl who read her fortune in tea, that was no concern of hers. She had held that hand. She had spent nine days putting it back together with her own fingers. She knew every letter on it. That was *her* bandage.

She ate a chocolate biscuit without tasting it. Goyle watched it go down with an air of quiet achievement.

He was in the window on the sixth floor that night. She had not moved her rounds. It would have looked like something.

“Brown,” said Lyra.

He looked up. He had a Chocolate Frog card in his hand, and he turned it over. It took him quite a long time, and then she saw it arrive; it came up from somewhere behind his glasses, very slowly, and it was the most disgusting thing she had ever seen on a human face.

“You were by the lake,” said Potter.

“I happened to be passing.”

“With a yoghurt.”

“I don’t know what you imagine—”

“Crabbe told Hermione. About the spoon. She thought somebody ought to know.”

“I shall have Crabbe’s *teeth*.”

He had got down from the sill. He was grinning all over his face. There was nothing she could do about it; she had tried everything; it did not seem to mind being looked at. “She was showing me a charm,” he said. “For spots. She did it through the bandage; I told her it was warts. It lasts till dark. Nobody can read it.”

“I’m sure she was. I’m sure it was most instructive. You may have your hand held by the whole of the fifth year, Potter, for all I care, and I hope you’ll be very happy, only you might have the common decency to do it where a person can’t—”

She stopped. He had put his hand out, the right one, with the back of it towards her, and held it up in front of her in the moonlight. Whatever Brown had done to it had worn off. It had healed quite well. The line was silver now, and very fine. Under it were the three small words, and he looked at them, and at her.

“Mine,” said Lyra.

She heard it go. It had come out of her with no warning at all. It had sounded exactly as it had when she was four, and somebody had picked up her doll.

For a moment neither of them moved.

She had said a great many things to him, in five years, in a great many voices, and she had never once seen him look like that. It was not the grin. The grin had gone out of him like a light. He stood there with his hand held up, and he looked as he had looked once on a stool in a robe shop, she thought, for no reason she could have given: as though somebody had handed him something, and he did not know yet whether he was allowed to keep it.

“Say that again,” said Potter.

“I shall do nothing of the kind. That hand is the property of— It was a *remark*. It’s my bandage, that’s all. I’ve put a great deal of work into that hand, and I won’t have it mauled about by people with no training.” Her face was on fire. “Stop looking at me.”

“Never own a Malfoy,” said Potter. It was hardly a voice at all. “You said.”

“I said that *you* couldn’t. I said nothing whatever about the other way round.”

He took one step, and she had him by the jumper, or he had her. It was difficult to say. It went on for rather longer than the others had, and at some point she found that she had her forehead against his collarbone, and that his arms had gone round her, and that they were both simply standing there, on the sixth floor, breathing. His heart was going like a rabbit’s. His hands were holding on to the back of her robes, in two fists. She could feel that he was not going to be the one who let go.

It was she who let go. It took her some time.

“That was the last one,” said Lyra.

“Eighty-five,” said Potter. He did not sound as though he could feel his feet.

“Eighty-five,” said Granger.

She laid a sheet of parchment on the table by the window, in the library, on Hallowe’en afternoon, with the rain coming down outside and the whole castle smelling of pumpkin, and she squared it to the edge with two fingers.

Lyra looked up from her Arithmancy. Then she looked along the table. Potter had been sitting at the far end of it for an hour and a half, behind a very large book about Quidditch, in order to be near Weasley, who had gone to fetch something. He had not looked at her once. He had his ears under control. He had got rather good at it.

“I’m working, Granger.”

“I know you are. I’ve been working as well. Harry, come here, please.”

He came. He pulled out a chair with his foot, and sat on it, at an angle, as somebody sits in a dentist’s waiting room. Granger looked from one of them to the other. She had two spots of colour on her cheeks, and her hair was doing as it pleased.

“I’ve been helping Ron with his figures,” she said. “For the meeting. He thinks it’s spite. I said I’d add it up, because somebody ought to, and because I’ve got a prefect’s right of access to the book. And I have to say that it’s beautifully kept.” She put her finger on the parchment. “Eighty-five points since the twelfth of September. Not one deduction in class, or at meals, or in front of anybody at all. All but one of them after ten at night, and hardly any before midnight. Four on the sixth. Nine on the seventh. One on the third, which isn’t on your rounds at all. And always, *always*, in fives.”

She looked up.

“You could have taken ten,” said Granger. “You could have taken twenty, if you’d gone to Snape. You like round numbers; I’ve seen your Arithmancy. And it’s never once been more than one a night, except for a Saturday in September when you were both in detention, and then it was three.” She took a breath. “So it isn’t a total. It’s a *count*. That’s seventeen. Seventeen of something.”

Nobody spoke. Somewhere behind the shelves, Madam Pince was stamping a book. The rain ran down the window.

“Seventeen *what*, Harry?”

Lyra did not move a muscle. She had been brought up to it. She kept her eyes on Hermione Granger’s, and gave her the pleasant, faintly puzzled look her mother used on tax inspectors, and under the table she drew back her foot, with great precision, to kick Potter on the ankle before he opened his mouth.

She was a quarter of a second too late.

“Fifteen,” said Potter. “One was a line. One was fraud.”

The foot arrived. He made a small noise and looked at the ceiling.

Granger’s mouth came open.

Lyra watched it happen, with interest, since there was by now nothing else to be done. The colour went up from Granger’s collar, slowly, as water goes up a piece of blotting paper. She looked at the parchment. She looked at the column of dates, and the words *Insolent use of a window seat*, and at Potter, and she shut her mouth. Then she looked at Lyra, for some time, as one looks at a person who has been caught keeping a dragon in a bedroom.

“Oh, for *heaven’s* sake,” said Granger. It came out very faint. She put both hands over her face.

“It doesn’t mean anything,” said Lyra.

“It’s finished,” said Potter. “It’s the last one. She keeps saying.”

“I do keep saying.”

“You’re Umbridge’s *dear*. You sit in the front row. You told Ron in front of the whole carriage that—” Her shoulders had begun to shake. She took her hands away, and her eyes were wet. She was very nearly crying, and she was trying very hard not to laugh; Lyra could not have said which was winning. “He’s made a *graph*! He stayed up! He’s going to the Head of House, Malfoy; he’s got a *speech*. He thinks you’re trying to get Gryffindor out of the Cup!”

“Then I should tell him to keep his chin up,” said Lyra. “It’s a very good graph.”

Granger looked at her. She got out a handkerchief, and blew her nose, and looked at her again; and when she spoke it was quite quietly, and she was not laughing.

“Is it safe?” she said.

Lyra picked up her quill.

“Nothing is safe, Granger,” she said. “You may have noticed. But I keep very careful accounts.”

Weasley’s feet were coming along the aisle. Potter got up, and went back to the far end of the table, and his book about Quidditch. As he passed behind her chair he did not touch her, and he did not look at her; and she felt it all the way down her back. Granger folded her parchment in half, and in half again, and put it in the inside of her robes.

“What’s up with you?” said Weasley, sitting down. “Have you got a cold?”

“Yes,” said Granger.

“Miss Malfoy,” said Professor McGonagall. “A moment.”

It was on the stairs, on her way down to the Hallowe’en feast. The Head of Gryffindor had stopped on the landing beneath the great hourglasses, with her hands clasped in front of her tartan and her lips rather thin. Up behind her the four tall tubes stood in their row, in the light of the first torches: emeralds, sapphires, a respectable depth of yellow diamonds; and at the end, sadly reduced, the rubies.

Lyra stopped, and looked her in the eye, and waited.

She had been expecting this. Weasley had left the library at four o’clock with his graph under his arm. She had her answer ready. It was on page nine.

“I have had a visit,” said Professor McGonagall, “from Mr Weasley. He has gone to a great deal of trouble. I have seldom seen such a very thorough piece of work from that quarter, and I told him so.” She looked up at the rubies. “Eighty-five points. All from Mr Potter, whom I have known for some time, and who has, I must say, been bearing it remarkably well. I do not believe I have seen him so nearly cheerful since the summer.”

“He is improving, Professor,” said Lyra.

Professor McGonagall turned her head. Her eyes behind the square spectacles went over Lyra’s face, without haste, and over her hair, which had gone back to its proper colour at last, and was very neatly pinned; and they stayed for a moment on a small dark-red thread of Gryffindor wool, which was caught on the second button of her robes.

Lyra had been looked at by Albus Dumbledore. She had been looked at by Mad-Eye Moody, with both eyes. She felt the blood begin to come up the back of her neck.

Professor McGonagall reached out with one long finger and thumb, and took the thread off, and let it fall over the banister.

“I was at school under Armando Dippet, Miss Malfoy,” she said. “It is a very long way up to the Astronomy Tower. One meets a great many people on the stairs.” She looked back at the rubies. Nothing moved in her face. “I should take them in tens, if I were you. It’s less conspicuous.”

She went on down to the feast, very straight, with her hand on the rail.
