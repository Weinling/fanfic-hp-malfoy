# Chapter 40: Hidden Ink

The ring did not want to come off.

It had shrunk to fit her finger under a cloister thirteen months ago, and again in a corridor in December, and it had been there ever since, and now that she wanted it gone it held on. Lyra sat on the end of her bed with the hangings drawn on the Tuesday morning and worked at it with her thumb, round and round, until the skin under it was red; and then it gave, and slid off over the knuckle, and lay in her palm. It was green. It had been green since the podium.

She wrapped it in a twist of tissue paper so that she would not have to look at what colour it went next.

The tin was from Honeydukes, the kind the shortbread came in at Christmas, with a Scottish terrier on the lid in a tartan coat. She had eaten the shortbread in March. She put the ring in, in its tissue, and sat looking at it in the bottom of the empty tin.

Then she opened the drawer of her bedside table and took out the handkerchief.

It had been washed so many times, before she ever had it, that it was soft as old silk, and she had washed it again herself, and pressed it, and kept it folded small in the drawer since a staircase in January a year and a half ago. It was his. She had known it was his since the night of the final. She had never given it back, and he had never asked.

She put it in the tin beside the ring.

She looked at it there, for some time.

Then she took it out again, and folded it smaller, and put it back in the drawer, and shut the drawer.

The note she wrote at the desk by the window, in her best hand, the one her mother had taught her to be proud of.

*Congratulations on the Cup.*

*That will be all, Potter. You were, on the whole, worth the ink.*

*L. M.*

She left a gap in the second line. Between *Potter.* and *You were*: a blank in the middle of the sentence, the width of two fingers, where nothing was written. No one who had ever seen her hand would believe she had left it by accident. She did not leave gaps.

She took out her wand and touched its tip to the blank.

The parchment gave a soft crack, like a seal breaking, and split along its own thickness into two thin layers that lifted apart at the edge, exactly as her father’s letters did; he had shown her how, years ago, for a birthday treasure hunt, and she had never once used it for anything until now. Between the layers the parchment was clean and pale, like the inside of a shell.

She wrote, very small, with the finest nib she had:

*Barty Crouch is under the Imperius. I saw his face on the podium; I know that face. He could have put your name in the Goblet. I think his son is alive. Ask Winky whose seat she was minding at the World Cup.*

She looked at it.

She read the last two sentences again. Potter had looked for Crouch’s son in Moody’s trunk. Now she knew where he had got the idea: a life in which the boy had not stayed dead. And there had been somebody in the chair Winky was guarding, somebody who could not be seen in public beside his father. Moody might have been the wrong hiding place without the son being the wrong person.

She could not prove it. Potter could take it to Dumbledore. Somebody could ask the elf.

She did not write anything about the north. She held the nib above the parchment for a long moment, and did not.

Then she touched the blank again, and the two layers came together with a small sigh and were one sheet, with a gap in the second line where nothing was written; and she folded it, and laid it on top of the tissue paper in the tin, and put on the lid with the Scottish terrier, and tied it up with green ribbon.

“Greer,” she said, through the hangings.

Goyle’s head came round the edge of them almost at once, as though it had been waiting.

“Take this to the hospital wing.” Pomfrey had kept him there since Monday night, for the leg. “Give it to Potter. Don’t say who it’s from. Don’t wait for an answer.”

Goyle looked at the tin, and at Lyra’s face, and at Lyra’s hand, which had no ring on it; and for once Greer Goyle did not say anything. She took the tin in both hands as though it might go off, and went.

She came back twenty minutes later, scarlet to the ears, out of breath from the stairs. She did not say anything then, either. She sat down on her own bed, and took a Chocolate Frog out of her pocket, and did not eat it.

On Wednesday the *Daily Prophet* had it on the front page.

**MINISTRY OFFICIAL FOUND DEAD AT HOME**

*Bartemius Crouch, Head of the Department of International Magical Co-operation and a judge of this year’s Triwizard Tournament, was found dead at his house late last night by officers of the Ministry, acting on information received. The cause of death has not been released. Mr Crouch, who lived alone, is understood to have been in poor health for some months. His house-elf was not present, having been dismissed last summer.*

*Ministry sources confirm that Igor Karkaroff, Headmaster of the Durmstrang Institute, who has not been seen since the night of the Third Task, is being sought for questioning . . .*

Lyra read it at the Slytherin table with her tea going cold, and folded it, and passed it on.

*Acting on information received.* She did not look at the Gryffindor table. She could feel where he was sitting, as she had always been able to, without the ring.

Nobody in the article had a son. Nobody had ever had one. Mr Crouch had lived alone.

Down in the kitchens, she supposed, under her feet, an elf in a stained blue skirt was crying for a master who had not been well, and who was not anything now. Lyra did not go down to see her. She sat with her hands round her cold tea and thought about it until Crabbe touched her elbow, and then she got up and went to Arithmancy, by herself, up seven flights, with nobody beside her.

The leaving feast was on the Friday night.

The Great Hall was hung in black, for Mr Crouch, everybody supposed; and nobody knew what else it was for until Dumbledore stood up at the end of the meal, with the plates cleared and the candles burning low, and the whole school went quiet without being asked.

He did not make a long speech. He spoke of the Tournament, and of the champions, and of Cedric Diggory, who had shown great courage and great fairness and was sitting at the Hufflepuff table with a bandage round his head; and of Harry Potter, who had won it. He did not say how. Then he looked out over the four tables, and was silent for a moment, and when he spoke again his voice was quite different.

“I have something to tell you,” said Professor Dumbledore, “which the Ministry of Magic would rather I did not. It is my belief, and I do not hold it lightly, that on the night of the Third Task, Lord Voldemort returned.”

Somebody at the Hufflepuff table screamed.

It was a short scream, and it stopped at once, and after it the silence in the Great Hall was worse than any noise could have been. Lyra sat at the Slytherin table with her hands folded in front of her on the cloth.

Dumbledore did not say where. He did not say how. He said that it would be the work of everybody in that room to remember, in the months to come, that the strongest weapon Lord Voldemort possessed was the ability to set people apart from one another, and that they would be well advised not to let him; and he looked, as he said it, at the Slytherin table, steadily and without any unkindness at all, and Lyra felt the whole length of the bench go still under it.

Then he sat down, and the feast was over.

Along the Slytherin table, nobody said anything at all.

The Slytherin common room, that night, was full and very quiet.

Nobody had gone up to bed. They were sitting about in the green lamplight in twos and threes, the way people sit at a funeral before the family comes in, and talking in voices so low that the fire was the loudest thing in the room; and every few minutes somebody looked up at the stone wall that was the door, as if they expected it to open and let something in. In one corner a knot of sixth-years whose fathers Lyra knew were sitting very close together, not talking at all. At the table by the window two second-years were crying, quietly, into a book, and pretending not to. Parkinson was standing by the fire with his hands in his pockets, explaining to nobody in particular that it was all nonsense, that Dumbledore was an old man who wanted attention, in the high fast voice of somebody who did not believe a word of it.

And here and there, in a face she knew, there was something that was not fear: a bright, careful stillness, like somebody holding a cup that was too full.

Lyra stood in the doorway and looked at them all.

She was tired to the bone. She had not slept properly since the lake. She wanted, more than she had ever wanted anything, to walk through that room without a word and up the stairs and draw the hangings round her bed and not come out until the train. Nobody in that room would have stopped her.

She did not do it.

“What is this?” said Lyra.

They looked round at her, all of them, every face in the room.

“Look at you. Whispering in corners like servants who think the house is on fire.” She came into the middle of the room, under the green lamp, where all of them could see her. “What do you think you are?”

Nobody answered.

“I’ll tell you what you are. Every one of you in this room carries a name that was old when the Ministry was a desk and an inkpot. Older than half this castle. Whatever is going on out there, and whatever anybody says about it, and whoever is or isn’t back, that has not changed, and it is not going to change, and I will not have you sitting in the Slytherin common room looking as though you’d like to crawl under the furniture.” She looked round at them, slowly, one face after another. “This is our house, and nobody in it lowers his voice. Not tonight. Not ever. Do you understand me?”

There was a pause.

Then one of the crying second-years wiped her face with the back of her hand and sat up straight. One of the sixth-years by the window laughed, short and surprised, and said, “Yes, Your Highness,” and two or three people laughed with him; and the room, very gradually, began to sound like a room again.

Lyra let it. She went across to the fire and sat down in her own chair, as she did every night, as though nothing whatever had happened.

Crabbe came and sat on the arm of it.

She sat there without saying anything. Then she leaned down, so that nobody else would hear, and said, very low:

“Is it over? With Potter?”

Lyra looked at the fire.

“Yes. A passing fancy. It has passed. That’s all.”

Crabbe had kept a hand on a sleeve to herself for three and a half years. She put her hand on Lyra’s arm now, and left it there.

She waited until the common room had emptied, and Crabbe had gone up, and the fire had burned down to a red seam in the grate. Then she went up the girls’ staircase, past the fourth-year dormitory, where somebody was still whispering behind the hangings, to the bathroom at the top, and went in, and turned the key.

It was very quiet. The bathroom was under the lake, like everything else down there, and the light that came in through the high green window was the colour of deep water, and moved, very slightly, all the time. There was a long mirror over the basins. She had stood in front of it every morning for four years, doing her hair.

She went to the nearest basin and put both hands on the edge of it, and looked.

She had thought, somehow, that she would look different after a week like this one. But it was the face she made every morning, smooth and pale and perfectly composed.

She watched it for a while. Then she watched it go.

It did not go at once. It went at the mouth first, at one corner, and she put her hand up over it, hard, and held it there; and then her eyes went, and she could not see the mirror any more, and her shoulders, and then the whole of her, all of it, bent over the basin with her hand clamped over her mouth so that not one sound would get out under the door, and not one did. She cried in a long, silent, dreadful heave that went on and on and would not stop, though she told it to, though she held on to the edge of the basin with her other hand until her fingers ached and told it, again and again, to *stop*.

It did not stop for a long time.

When it did, she was sitting on the cold stone floor with her back against the wall under the basins, with her knees drawn up, and her face wet, and her hand still over her mouth. She took it away. Her fingers left marks.

“I wanted to stay,” said Lyra, aloud, to nobody.

It sounded very small in the green bathroom.

After a while she got up.

She ran the cold tap, and washed her face in it, twice, until it stung. She dried it on the towel. She took the pins out of her hair, one after another, and laid them in a row on the edge of the basin, and brushed it out, and put it up again, slowly, with every pin where it should be. Then she looked in the mirror.

It was her own face. There was nothing in it anywhere.

She unlocked the door and went to bed.

Moody found her in the Entrance Hall on the Saturday morning, among the trunks.

The whole school was in it, or seemed to be: the trunks stacked by the doors for the carriages, owls in cages hooting, first-years running about after toads, Hagrid at the top of the steps bellowing at somebody about a crate. Moody came through all of it like a ship through small boats, with his own trunk floating behind him at the height of his knee, seven locks and all, and stopped in front of her.

Crabbe and Goyle drew back a step without being asked.

“Miss Malfoy,” said Moody.

“Professor.”

“Not for much longer.” He jerked his head at the trunk. “I’m off. One year, Albus asked me for. He’s had it.” The blue eye went round the Hall, once, and came back to her. “Your note.”

She did not say anything.

“Potter brought it to Albus on Tuesday, before it was dark. Two layers. Nice work. I’ve not seen that done in thirty years.” His mouth moved. “Albus sent me and two others to Crouch’s house that night. We were an hour too late.”

Lyra waited.

“The son,” said Moody, very low. “You think his son is alive. I stood at that boy’s grave.” He waited. “What made you think of him?”

“Winky was guarding a seat at the World Cup. It wasn’t empty. And Potter had been looking for your missing boy in quite the wrong place.”

“Sneakoscope wouldn’t twitch at that,” said Moody, half to himself. He looked at her, in the middle of the noise and the trunks and the owls, and then he grunted.

“I’ve been at this thirty years,” he said. “I took you for your father’s errand girl. I took that boy of yours for a fool. And I took Barty Crouch for the only honest man in the Ministry.” He shifted his weight on to the wooden leg. “Three for three.”

It was not an apology. She inclined her head to it, very slightly, as she would have to a duellist who had bowed.

“One thing more,” said Moody. He leaned closer; she could smell the flask. “Your father. Is he marked?”

Lyra looked at him.

“My father,” she said, “has never had anything on his arm in his life, Professor. He was under the Imperius. Everybody knows that.”

She said it beautifully. Her voice did not shake. She did not know, as she said it, whether it was true or not.

The blue eye rested on her.

“Watch yourself in that house,” said Moody. “Watch it better than I watched this one.”

Then he turned, and went away across the Hall with his trunk floating behind him, and out through the great doors into the sunshine, and did not look back.

On the train somebody had the *Prophet*.

It went round their compartment twice before they reached the moors, and Goyle read the best bit out, in the Minister’s voice: that the Minister deeply regretted that Professor Dumbledore had seen fit to alarm a hall full of children on the eve of their holidays with irresponsible rumours, at a time when the Ministry was doing everything in its power to bring Mr Crouch’s murderer to justice. Nobody laughed. Crabbe took it off her, after the second time, and folded it up small, and sat on it.

Lyra looked out of the window most of the way, at the hills going by and then the fields and then the backs of houses, and did not take part in the conversation, and nobody asked her to.

At King’s Cross the platform was full of steam and noise and parents. She got down with her trunk and stood for a moment on the step, in the press, getting her bearings; and at the far end of the train, through the steam, she saw a crowd of red hair, Weasleys, a great many of them, and in the middle of them a boy with untidy black hair and a trunk, standing quite still, looking at her.

She did not look back. She turned her head away, quite naturally, as one turns from a draught, and looked for her own people instead; and found them.

They were by the pillar, where they always stood. Her mother was a little behind, very upright, in grey, with her hands folded, looking at Lyra with the small, careful, unreadable face she had worn at the top of the drive every summer of Lyra’s life; and then her mother’s eyes went past her, down the platform, to where the red hair was, and stayed there a moment, and came back. Draco was hopping. He had grown an inch and lost a tooth and was waving with his whole arm.

“Lyra! *Lyra!* The gentleman came back! On Sunday! He says he’s never felt better in his life, and he’s brought me—”

“Draco,” said their mother.

And her father was smiling.

He came forward through the steam with both hands out, as he always did, as he had every summer of her life, with his silver-topped cane under his arm and his hair like pale silk and his face lit up with a joy he did not try to hide; and he put his arms round her, there on the platform, in front of everybody, and held her tight.

As he lifted his arms, his left sleeve slid back from his wrist.

It was only for a moment. Nobody else on that platform would have seen it. Lyra saw it, at the edge of her eye, as he folded her in: on the pale inside of his forearm, where she had never once thought to look, the edge of something dark.

Black.

Her father held her very close, and said into her hair, warmly, “My treasure. My clever, clever girl.”

Lyra put her arms round him, and held on.
