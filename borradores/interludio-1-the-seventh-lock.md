# Interlude: The Seventh Lock

The voice came out of the dark corner of the office, where there had been nothing a moment before, and Harry did not turn round at once.

He knew whose it was. He had known, he thought afterwards, from the creak of the wooden leg, before the voice had said a word; and he stood with his lit wand held out over the edge of the empty shaft and looked down at the swept stone floor ten feet below, where somebody ought to have been, and let himself have one more second of not having to turn round.

“Wands,” said Moody. “On the desk. Both of you. Slowly.”

Sirius was already moving. Harry heard him do it, heard the small click of wood on wood, and then there was nothing to do but straighten up and turn and put his own beside it.

Moody was standing by the window. He had the Marauder’s Map in one hand, folded small, and his wand in the other, and his face in the light of Harry’s wand was quite calm. The blue eye was on Harry. The other one was on Sirius. Neither of them moved.

“A week,” said Moody. “You’ve been watching this office for a week, Potter. Every night. Lovely bit of parchment, this; I could see you coming up the stairs and going down again like a man counting his own front steps.” He put the map away inside his coat. “And tonight you bring *him*.”

“Alastor—” said Sirius.

“Not a word from you. Not one.” Moody did not raise his voice. He had never needed to. “You’re a man the Ministry’s stopped looking for, Black. That isn’t the same thing as a man the Ministry’s finished with, and well you know it. You’re in my office at one in the morning with a champion of this Tournament, and he’s just opened the seventh lock of my trunk as if he’d been given the key.” The blue eye swivelled, very slowly, to the shaft, and back. “What were you looking for, Potter?”

Harry said nothing.

“No,” said Moody. “I didn’t think you’d say.”

He jerked his wand at the trunk.

“Down.”

It was ten feet. Sirius went first, lowering himself over the lip and dropping the last of it with a grunt, and then turned and put his hands up; and Harry sat on the edge with his legs in the dark and looked up once, at the square of lamplit ceiling, and at Moody standing over him with the wand.

“If you’re right about me,” said Harry, “you’re making a mistake.”

“And if I’m wrong,” said Moody, “I’ve lost a night’s sleep. Down.”

He went down.

The lid came over. The light went, all of it, all at once, and then in the dark they heard the locks: seven of them, one after another, each with its own small sound, a click and a turn and a click, unhurried, like a man doing up the buttons on a coat.

Then nothing.

Harry stood on the swept stone floor in the pitch black with his heart going and his hands empty.

There was no one here. There never had been. He had gone straight to the seventh lock as if he had been told where to look, and there was nobody at the bottom of it, and the floor was clean.

Somewhere close to him, Sirius let out a long breath.

“Well,” said Sirius. “That went well.”

Harry nearly laughed. It came up into his throat and stuck there, and turned into something else, and he put his back against the stone wall and slid down it until he was sitting on the floor.

*If you’re wrong—*

*Then I’ll say sorry.*

He had said it on the stairs, an hour ago, quite easily. He had been so sure that he would not have to.
