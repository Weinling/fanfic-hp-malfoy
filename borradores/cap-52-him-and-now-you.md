# Chapter 52: Him, and Now You

Granger sat down opposite her in the library on a Tuesday in the middle of November, at the table by the window, without asking, which was how Lyra had been sitting down opposite Granger since March.

She had a stack of books, as always, and she put them down, as always, between them, like a wall with a gap in it. But she did not open any of them. She folded her hands on the top one and looked at Lyra across it, and Lyra, who had been reading the same page of *Numerology and Grammatica* for twenty minutes, looked back.

“He told me,” said Granger.

Lyra did not ask who. She did not ask what. She sat with her quill in her hand and felt the whole of the library go very far away, as though somebody had put a glass over the table.

“Not all of it.” Granger’s voice was very low and very steady, and her face was pink to the ears. “I don’t think all of it. But enough. That he’s—” She stopped to choose the word. “That he’s been here before. That there was another time, and it went differently, and he remembers. That he knew about the Chamber.” She looked down at her hands. “That you weren’t in it.”

The quill was making a blot on the page. Lyra lifted it.

“When?”

“Last week. I made him.” Granger’s mouth went thin. “I’ve been writing things down since I was eleven. He stepped over a trick stair before anybody had told him it was there. He stopped being surprised by things, and this summer he started being surprised again, all the time, and I didn’t understand why that was worse. So I sat him down in an empty classroom and I didn’t let him leave until he’d told me.” She paused. “It took four hours.”

Lyra said nothing.

“Ron doesn’t know,” said Granger. “Nobody else knows. Him, and Dumbledore, and you. And now me.” She unfolded her hands, and put them flat on the books. “I thought you should know I know. That’s all. I thought it would be worse if you found out some other way.”

*Him, and now you.* In the Owlery he had said it as though he were giving her something. She had kept it all summer without anybody to talk to about it. Granger had got it out of him in four hours, and then come to tell her herself.

Lyra looked at the girl across the table with reluctant respect.

“Four hours.”

“About that.”

“I needed four years, and a beetle, and a dragon, and a cup that went nowhere.”

Granger did not smile.

“I had sandwiches,” she said. She picked up the top book from her pile, and held it, and did not open it. “That’s the part I came to tell you. Here’s the other. Wayne Hopkins can’t do up his tie. Diggory does it for him, before breakfast, in the corridor outside the kitchens, so that nobody will see.” Her voice was exactly as it had been. “The walls at Grimmauld Place are very thin. He talks in his sleep. He says your name, mostly. I’d give a great deal to be able to tell you that he doesn’t.”

She found her place, and began to read.

Lyra turned back to *Numerology and Grammatica*, and read the same page for another twenty minutes, and did not take in one word of it.

She saw how Potter got into Diggory’s group that afternoon, in the Entrance Hall, at the end of lunch, with half the school going past.

She had known about the group for a fortnight, since passing two sixth-years on rounds who were arguing about a Shield Charm Diggory had taught them. The Head Boy had decided that if Umbridge would not teach them to defend themselves, he would, and was meeting a dozen or so seventh- and sixth-years in the old duelling room behind the kitchens twice a week. Umbridge did not know yet. Lyra had kept it to herself, in case it was worth more later.

Potter was not in it. Diggory had not forgiven him for a hedge in June, and a curse in the back, and a cup; and he had said so, in front of witnesses, in the first week of term, quite pleasantly, in the voice of a man who would never say so again.

So when Potter came down the marble staircase with a heavy leather bag in his good hand, and crossed the Entrance Hall to where Diggory was standing with Cho Chang by the doors, and held the bag out to him, the whole Entrance Hall turned round to watch.

“It’s the prize money,” said Potter. He did not lower his voice. “From the Tournament. The thousand Galleons. It’s yours.”

Diggory looked at the bag. Then at Potter.

“It’s *yours*,” he said. “You won it.”

“I won it from behind. Everybody knows that. You had your hand on it first.” He held the bag out a little further. “Take it.”

For a moment Lyra thought Diggory was going to. He looked at the bag for a long time, with an expression on his decent, handsome face that she could not read; and then he took it, out of Potter’s hand, and weighed it, and turned it upside down.

A thousand Galleons came out on to the flagstones of the Entrance Hall. They went everywhere: rolling, spinning, bouncing down the steps to the dungeons, under the feet of a crowd of first-years who shrieked and scattered and then, being first-years, began to pick them up.

“I don’t want your money, Potter,” said Diggory. “I wanted the Cup.”

He dropped the empty bag at Potter’s feet, and walked out through the great doors, and Cho Chang went after him.

Potter stood in the middle of the Entrance Hall in a sea of gold, with the whole school watching, and did not pick up a single coin.

It was the ugliest thing Lyra had ever seen him do, and the bravest, and she could not for the life of her have said which was which. She went down the dungeon steps with three Galleons in the toe of her shoe, where they had rolled, and left them on the bottom step for whoever wanted them.

By Saturday, Zabini, who heard everything and repeated almost none of it, had mentioned over his cards that Potter had gone down to the old duelling room that same night and waited outside the door for two hours. Diggory had let him in because he could teach them a corporeal Patronus, and had told him that if he ever turned his back on any of them in there, Diggory would know.

“Look,” said Draco.

It was after dinner on the Saturday. He had come and sat on the arm of her chair by the common-room fire, which he never did in front of the House, and he was holding something in his closed hand against his chest, and his face was alight with a secret so enormous that it was physically difficult for him to keep it inside.

He opened his hand. There was a Galleon in it.

“It’s not a real one,” said Draco, very low, delighted. “Look at the edge. It’s got numbers on, where the goblin numbers should be, and they *change*. And when it gets hot, that means there’s a meeting, and the numbers say when.” He shut his hand on it again. “I’m in a *club*.”

Lyra looked at his fist.

“What club?”

“A Defence club. A secret one. Diggory runs it. He’s *Head Boy*.” His voice had gone up; he brought it down again with a glance at the room. “Only seventh years and sixth years. And me. I’m the only first-year in the whole thing. We learnt Disarming on Thursday, I got Summerby’s wand off him twice.”

“How did a first-year get into a club for sixth and seventh years, Draco?”

He went pink with pleasure.

“Potter,” he said. “Harry Potter. He came and found me after Charms, on Wednesday, in the corridor, on my own. He said Diggory would let me in if he said so, because Diggory owes him, sort of. He gave me the coin himself.” He leaned closer. “And he said a thing. He said, if anybody ever asks you to go anywhere—anywhere at all, in the castle, at night, on your own—you’re to tell him first. Before you go. Him, not anybody else. And he’d come with me.” Draco considered this, with his head on one side. “I think he thinks I’ll get lost. Everybody thinks I get lost.”

Lyra sat very still in her chair.

*Anywhere at all, in the castle, at night, on your own.*

He had read it in her face. In a dark classroom on the third floor, with the moon on the floor between them, she had said *No* in the voice she had learnt on Thursday evenings, and given him nothing; and he had looked at her one moment longer than he should have, and said *Right*, and gone away; and a month later he had found her brother alone in a corridor after Charms and given him a coin that got warm.

If Draco remembered to ask, somebody would go with him, somebody who might know what the old man was sending him to find. It was kind. Lyra could see how kind it was.

Potter had only to ask where Draco was going, and Draco would tell him, delighted to be asked. She looked at the coin in her brother’s hand.

“Did you tell anybody? About the corridor. On the seventh floor.”

The secret went out of Draco’s face, and the guilt came in.

“No,” he said. “Course not. He didn’t ask about that. He just said *anywhere*.” He looked at her anxiously. “You won’t tell Father? About the club? He’d say Diggory’s a blood traitor. He’d make me leave.”

“I shan’t tell Father.”

He kissed her cheek, which he had not done in public since he was eight, and went away across the common room with his fist closed on his coin against his heart, beside the list.

She found Potter on her rounds that night, because he had made sure she would: on the fifth-floor landing, at the top of the marble staircase, sitting on the top step with his back against the banister and his cloak in a bundle beside him, waiting.

It was eight days since the sixth floor. She had moved her rounds. He must have gone to some trouble. He got up when he saw her wand-light, and he went back a step, so that there was the whole width of the landing between them, and he kept his hands where she could see them; and she noticed that, and she noticed that she had noticed, and that made it a great deal easier to be angry.

“You used my brother,” said Lyra.

Her voice came out low, and very clear, and it rang in the stairwell.

“Yes.”

“He’s eleven.”

“I know how old he is.”

“He thinks you *like* him. He thinks it’s a *club*. He thinks he’s the cleverest first-year in the castle and that Harry Potter came and found him in a corridor because he’s *special*—”

“He is in danger. And you lied to me.”

She stopped.

He did not raise his voice. He stood on the landing with his back to the staircase and his scarred hand at his side, and said it as he had once said *I’d like to see him in Azkaban*, without anything over the top of it to make it easier to carry.

“In October. In that room. I asked you if you knew anything about who was collecting his things, and you looked at me and said *no*, and I couldn’t tell. For the first time in four years I couldn’t tell if you were lying to me.” His jaw moved. “And then I went down to breakfast and your brother was telling Urquhart’s brother about a door in a wall on the seventh floor. The seventh floor, Lyra. Where there’s a room I know about that I shouldn’t, with things in it I know about that I shouldn’t, and I thought, *she knows*. She knows, and she’s not going to tell me, because it’s him. So I did the only thing I could think of.”

“You put a bell on him.”

“I put a *friend* on him. Somebody who’d be there if—”

“A bell.” She came up the last two stairs on to the landing, so that they were level. “You did to him exactly what you did to me. You knew where everybody would be standing, and you made sure they stayed there, and you never told anybody why.” Her voice shook. She let it. “You asked me something in June. I have had nine answers, and you may have the sixth. *I did.* I came. I have gone up seven flights every night since September for that child, in the dark, and nobody asked me to; and the first thing you do, now that you can’t see any more, the very first thing, is go and find him in a corridor and put him on your board.”

Potter had gone white.

His right arm had come up.

She saw it before he did. It rose from his side, quite smoothly, from the shoulder, with his wand at the end of it, and she would have sworn on the Malfoy name that he had not drawn it. His lips had parted. There was a word behind them. She could see the shape of it there: something short and hard that she did not know, in no book that her father owned. It was not looking at her as he looked at her. It was not looking at her at all; it was only looking for where she stopped.

She felt her wrist, under the cuff, and the whole of the landing went very quiet.

He shut his mouth.

It took him some effort. She watched his throat move as he got it down. Then he looked along his own arm at the wand in his hand, with the most complete astonishment she had ever seen on a human face, and he lowered it, an inch at a time, as one puts down something that may go off; and he took it in his left hand, and put it away inside his robes, and stood holding his right wrist.

Neither of them mentioned it.

“That isn’t—” said Potter. It came out hoarse. He tried again. “That isn’t what—”

“It *is*.”

“He’s *eleven*,” said Potter, and his voice broke on it, and she understood, too late, that he had not meant it as an argument for her side. “He’s eleven and somebody’s given him instructions, and I don’t know what they are, and I don’t know who’s sending him, and I don’t know what happens to Draco if he finds what he’s looking for. Do you? Do you, Lyra?”

She did not.

They stood on the landing, a yard apart, in the light of her wand, and neither of them said anything for so long that the portrait of a sleeping wizard on the wall beside them woke up, and looked at them, and thought better of it, and went back to sleep.

He was frightened for Draco. She could hear it, and could not make it cancel what he had done to her.

“Don’t come near him again,” said Lyra.

“I can’t promise that.”

“I know. I didn’t think you could.”

She turned round and went down the marble staircase, and did not look back; and on the fourth flight she heard him say her name, once, quite quietly, from the top, and she did not stop.

She lay awake behind her hangings until the green light on the ceiling had gone from black to grey.

There was still a sheet of old cream paper against her brother’s heart.

At some point before the grey turned white, she decided.

She was going to look.
