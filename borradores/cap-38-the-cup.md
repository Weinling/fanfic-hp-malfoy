# Chapter 38: The Cup

“How long?” said Lyra.

She said it to a wall. It was twenty to seven on the morning before the Third Task, the Entrance Hall was empty, and she had come up from the dungeons an hour before anybody with a reason to, and stopped on the fourth step from the top.

The ring had gone pink.

It had gone pink on that step every morning for three weeks. She had seen it do so on the first day and looked, once, at the place by the wall, and there had been nobody there but a Hufflepuff doing up his shoe; and after that she had kept her thumb over it and gone on up to Arithmancy, and told herself that it was a cheap ring from a joke shop and had probably got stuck.

*In the company of their sweetheart.*

“Three weeks,” she said. “I can count, Potter. Take that thing off.”

For about as long as it takes to make up one’s mind, nothing happened. Then a piece of the air came away, and he was leaning against the wall exactly where he had leaned every morning since January, with his hands in his pockets and the cloak over his arm, looking like a boy who has been found in a larder.

“Since the morning after,” said Potter.

“*Every* morning.”

“You come up at ten to eight. Quarter to on Tuesdays. Goyle’s lost a hair ribbon and you’ve lent her one and she hasn’t noticed it’s silk.”

Lyra came up the last four steps. She had been preparing this for three weeks. It had clauses.

“You stopped,” she said. “You stood at these stairs in front of the entire school for five months, and then one morning you weren’t there, without a word to anybody, and I stood staring at a Hufflepuff’s shoe with Crabbe looking at me. And all the time you were *here*. Under a blanket. Watching me do it.”

“Yes.”

“That is the most perfectly disgusting thing anybody has ever done to me.”

“I know.” He looked at the flagstones. “You lied to me. I’d sworn, and you looked straight at me, and I don’t know what about, I only know what it looks like. I couldn’t stand here and have you say good morning.” He rubbed the back of his neck. “And I couldn’t not come. So.”

It was appalling. She was quite clear about that. She was also aware that something under her ribs had sat up and was looking extremely pleased with itself. Three weeks. Every morning. Nobody in her whole life had ever got out of bed in order to watch her walk past. She would take that out and look at it later, when she had finished being furious.

“What did Karkaroff want?” said Potter.

He said it quietly, and then he looked up, and she saw that he had been saving that as well. There was nobody in the Hall. There was nobody on the stairs. *When we are all together in the north.* It would have taken one breath.

“Nothing,” said Lyra.

“Right.”

It was the same word in the same voice, and she could not stand it. She crossed the last yard of flagstones, took him by the tie, which was crooked, and kissed him, because she could not think of anything else that would stop him saying it again.

It was cross and much too hard, and his glasses dug into her cheekbone. He made a small surprised noise. Then his arms came round her and held on.

They held on a good deal too tightly. That was what she noticed. He was holding her as people hold on to each other on platforms, and he went on doing it after the kiss had stopped, with his face against her hair, and she could feel his heart going.

She got a hand flat against his chest and pushed until she could see him.

“If you get hurt,” said Lyra, “I’ll kill you.”

He ought to have had something for that. He had had something for it in October. He only looked at her, for rather a long time, as one looks at a page one will have to hand back.

“Potter. It’s *hedges*.”

“Yeah,” he said. “Hedges.”

There were feet on the dungeon stairs: Crabbe’s, by the sound of them, going slowly, as Crabbe did when she wished to be heard coming. He pulled the cloak up over his shoulders, and the Entrance Hall was empty, and the ring went on being pink.

“Ten to eight,” said the air.

Lyra straightened her collar. She had not asked him what he thought was in there. She had been asked a question herself that morning, and she knew how that had gone.

⁂

From the top of the stands the maze looked like writing.

It had been growing all month, slowly and then, in the last week, a foot a night, and now in the long June dusk it stood twenty feet high across the whole of the Quidditch pitch: dark, dense, close-clipped walls of hedge, ruled out in lines that turned and crossed and doubled back on themselves and went nowhere, and somewhere in the middle of them, on a plinth, a small blue glimmer that was the Cup. From up here you could see the whole shape of it. You could see every turning. What you could not see was anything that was in it.

“The Cup,” Bagman was saying, through his megaphone, from the judges’ podium at the edge of the grass, “will bring its winner back to us here, ladies and gentlemen, right here, the moment it is touched! So keep your eyes on this podium! The first champion to lay a hand on it—”

Lyra had stopped listening. She sat on the top bench of the Slytherin stand with Crabbe on one side of her and Goyle on the other, in the warm blue evening, and looked at the ring on her finger.

It was purple. It had gone purple at half past seven, when the champions came out on to the grass, and it had not changed since.

*On guard.*

She had seen it purple before, all one night in August, behind her bed-hangings at the Manor, the night of the World Cup. It frightened her more now, because then she had been a hundred miles off in a four-poster bed, and now she was here, and he was down there.

The whistle went.

Potter and Diggory went in first, together, being level, through the gap in the hedge at the southern end; and the hedge closed behind them as though it had never been open. Krum went in after them, a few minutes later. Then Fleur Delacour, last, very straight, with her silver hair tied back.

After that there was nothing to see but hedges.

The stands made the noise that crowds make when they have nothing to watch: a long rustle of talk, and a few shouts of advice into the dark, and a song starting at the Hufflepuff end and dying away again. Somewhere inside the maze there was a flash of light, and a pop, and nothing else. Somewhere else a hedge shook all along its length, as though something very large had walked into it, and the Hufflepuffs screamed, and then it stopped shaking.

The ring stayed purple.

It was a little after nine when the first red sparks went up out of the maze, on the western side, high and bright against the dark blue sky.

The stands went up with them. Lyra was on her feet; then she looked at the ring, and it was purple still, not red, not red at all, and she sat down with her hand pressed flat over it.

“Delacour,” said somebody along the bench. “It’s Delacour, look, they’re bringing her out—”

They were. Two of the patrolling teachers were carrying something out through a gap in the hedge that closed behind them, and the something had silver hair.

Ten minutes later, more red sparks: on the eastern side, this time, nearer the middle.

Crabbe’s hand came across in the dark and took hold of Lyra’s, and held it.

Lyra did not look at her. She looked at the ring. Purple. She looked at the sparks, fading, and the shape of the maze, and the blue glimmer at the heart of it, and the two dark walls of hedge running towards it from the south that nobody had come out of yet; and she held on to Crabbe’s hand, quite hard, and Crabbe let her.

“That’s Krum,” said Goyle. “Has to be. That leaves—”

“I know what it leaves.”

⁂

For a long time after that, nothing happened.

The stands talked themselves quiet. Down on the grass the patrolling teachers stood at the edges of the maze with their wands lit, and Bagman stood on the podium with his megaphone hanging from one hand and nothing to say into it, and the blue glimmer at the heart of the maze went on glimmering. Inside those two walls of hedge somewhere, two boys were walking towards it. Lyra could not see either of them. She sat with Crabbe’s hand in hers and the ring under her thumb, and counted her own breathing, and lost count, and began again.

Then there was a flash on the podium.

It was blue-white and very sudden, like a cracker going off, and when it had gone there was somebody standing in the middle of the judges’ platform where nobody had been a moment before.

It was Potter.

He was alone. He had the Cup in his left hand and his wand up in his right, crouched low, his weight on the balls of his feet, braced for the ground to go out from under him. He turned all the way round on the spot, slowly, with his wand held out: the judges’ table, the stands, the black edge of the Forest, the sky. Then he was back where he had started, and still looking.

Then he looked down at the Cup in his hand.

He looked at it for a long moment, without moving, as you might look at a person who had promised you something, very solemnly, and had not done it.

The stands had not made a sound. Then Bagman found his voice.

“Harry Potter!” he roared, into the megaphone. “Harry Potter, ladies and gentlemen—the Triwizard Champion!”

The Gryffindors went up first. Then the rest of the stands, raggedly, as a crowd does when it has been given the answer to something and is not sure it has heard the question right. Potter lowered his wand. He did not lower it very far.

On Lyra’s finger, under her thumb, the ring went green.

*Safe.*

She stared at it. She looked back at the podium, where Potter was standing with the Cup in one hand and his wand in the other, still up, and the cheers coming down round him like rain; and then the cheering at the Hufflepuff end of the stands changed its note, and broke, and turned into something else.

Cedric Diggory had come out of the maze.

He came out of a gap in the hedge on the northern side, on his own feet, but only just. There were leaves in his hair and a long scratch down one side of his face, and he was swaying, and he had his wand in his hand; and as he came into the light of the patrolling teachers’ wands he raised his head and shouted something at the podium that the whole of that side of the pitch could hear.

“—from *behind*! He Stunned me from *behind*, and threw me in the—I was right there, I had my hand on it, and he—”

Moody stumped past Diggory into the gap in the hedge, his wand lit, and the gap closed behind him.

The Hufflepuffs began to boo.

It started at their end of the stands, low and ugly, and spread. It went along the Ravenclaw benches, some of them, and a few of the Durmstrang boys took it up for the noise of the thing. The Gryffindors cheered louder to drown it, and that made it worse. In the middle of it, down on the grass, Professor Sprout had reached Diggory and had an arm round him, and he was still shouting, and pointing; and up on the podium, Potter stood with the Cup in his hand and looked at Diggory, and did not say anything, and did not look away.

He had won the Triwizard Tournament, in the ugliest way it was possible to win anything, in front of three schools and the Ministry of Magic.

“He cheated,” whispered Crabbe, beside her. “Didn’t he? He—”

“He won.”

She was remembering a tent. A bench at the back of it, and a dragon outside, and Bagman’s voice coming through the canvas, *one step nearer the Triwizard Cup!*; and a boy who had sat through all the roaring without once turning his head, and had turned it then, at that, towards the voice, and kept it there a moment too long.

She had thought it was what being a champion did to a person.

⁂

Then the stands began to empty on to the grass, and Lyra went down with them.

Crabbe had let go of her hand to stand up, and then the whole Slytherin bench was moving, and the steps were full of people going down, and she was among them, going down too, with Crabbe somewhere behind her saying *Malfoy* and *wait*. At the bottom the crowd spread out across the trampled grass towards the podium like water finding a level, Gryffindors in front, shouting, and Ministry wizards trying to hold them back, and Lyra went through all of it as she went through crowds at her father’s parties, sideways, without touching anybody, until there was nobody between her and the edge of the judges’ platform but two Ministry men with their backs to her.

She was two paces from the podium. She could see everything.

Potter was standing at the front of it, still holding the Cup. Somebody had put a blanket over his shoulders that he did not seem to know about. He was standing oddly, with his weight off his left leg, and below the knee the leg of his trousers was black and wet and torn, and there was blood on the boards under his shoe.

“—a Skrewt, Albus, in the last stretch, one of Hagrid’s, it’s had a go at his leg—”

“Then he will go to Madam Pomfrey, Ludo, as soon as we have done.”

Two of the patrolling teachers had brought something up on to the platform behind the judges: a wooden crate with holes in the lid, out of which came a smell of burning and a muffled, furious ticking. Something inside it was hitting the walls of the crate with its tail, and every so often there was a small explosion and a puff of smoke out of the holes.

At the other end of the platform, Professor Sprout was calling to Dumbledore. Diggory had sat down hard on the grass and was trying to push her hands away from his head. Dumbledore turned to her.

Mr Crouch came along the platform from the far end of the judges’ table, very upright, in his neat dark suit, and stopped in front of Potter and held out his hand. Potter, after a moment, shifted the Cup into his other arm and shook it.

“Congratulations, Mr Potter,” said Mr Crouch. His voice was clipped and dry, a voice for committees. “A most remarkable performance. The Ministry is very—the Ministry is—”

He stopped.

Lyra had heard a great many men pause, at her father’s table, for effect. He simply stopped, in the middle of the word, with his hand still holding Potter’s and his eyes on nothing; and for a second, two, three, nothing happened behind his face. Then he started again, a beat late, exactly where he had left off, as though somebody had wound him up.

“—very proud. You are, I think—” another stop; another beat “—a credit to your school.”

He let go of Potter’s hand. He turned his head, very stiffly, and looked at the crate.

“That creature,” said Mr Crouch. “It drew blood?”

“It stung me,” said Potter. He was looking at Crouch now, very hard. “In the leg.”

“Then it will have to be examined. Interference with a champion in the course of a task.” Mr Crouch went to the crate and put both hands on it. “The Department will see to it. I shall take it myself.”

“Yes, Barty, only get it off the platform before it explodes,” said Bagman, and turned to shout at the Ministry men. A crowd of Gryffindors had broken through at the other end and was swarming up on to the boards. Dumbledore raised a hand to stop them; somebody shouted Diggory’s name; and in the noise Mr Crouch lifted the crate and walked away along the edge of the platform, past where Lyra was standing. Potter took a step after him, on the bad leg, and the leg went, and the Gryffindors were on him.

He passed within a yard of her. She saw his face quite clearly, in the light of a dozen wands.

It was perfectly, stupidly calm: the mouth a little slack and the eyes fixed on some point a long way in front of him.

Parkinson had worn that face in November, a moment before he began to hop across the classroom like a frog. Crabbe had worn it while she sang Celestina Warbeck. For one long second, before she threw the curse off, Lyra had felt it settling on her own face from the inside.

She knew exactly what it was.

Mr Crouch walked on down the steps of the platform with the crate in his arms and the Skrewt ticking furiously inside it, and the crowd let him through, and closed behind him, and he was gone.

Lyra stood where she was.

She looked for Dumbledore on the platform, and found him, three yards off, with his back to her, bending over Potter. She thought of the north, and her father’s green wax, and a word she had said in a classroom with the moon on the lake; and she did not go.

On the podium, over the heads of the Gryffindors, Potter had turned. He was not looking after Crouch. He was looking at her.

He had found her over several hundred heads, and he looked at her across all of them, with his weight off his bad leg and the Cup in his arms and the blanket sliding off one shoulder. She had given him the same word twice now, to his face. He did not smile. He only looked, for as long as it took Weasley to reach him and throw an arm round his neck, and then he turned his head and was gone into the red and gold.

⁂

They went back up to the castle in a long, noisy, lantern-lit stream, and Lyra went with it, with Crabbe at her elbow, not talking.

At the foot of the slope she looked back.

The stands were emptying. The maze stood dark and enormous behind them, and the podium was a little lit island at the edge of it, with a few Ministry wizards still on it and Bagman talking to one of them with his arms going. Up in the staff stand, at the end of the front row, somebody had not moved.

It was Professor Snape. He was on his feet, but only just. He was standing with his right hand clamped round his left forearm, over the sleeve, so tightly that she could see it from the bottom of the slope, and his face in the lantern-light had gone a colour she had never seen a living face go, a dreadful yellowish white, like old candle-wax. As she watched, he closed his eyes. He stood in the emptying stand, holding his own arm, with his eyes shut, listening.

Then he opened them, and let go of his arm, and walked down out of the stand and away towards the castle, very fast, without looking at anybody.

She had seen a man hold his arm like that once before, in a cabin, with the lamp swinging.

Lyra looked along the judges’ table, from habit.

Madame Maxime’s chair was empty; she had gone with her pupils. Bagman’s, Dumbledore’s, empty. Mr Crouch’s, empty. And at the far end, Professor Karkaroff’s chair was empty too, and his great fur coat was still hanging over the back of it, silvery in the lantern-light, as though he had stood up in the middle of the evening to go somewhere for a moment, and had not stopped to put it on.

It was June, and warm. But Karkaroff had worn that coat to every meal since October.

A knot of Durmstrang boys went past them up the slope, laughing, with their arms round each other’s necks, still in their red robes. One of them was Poliakoff. He saw Lyra and waved at her extravagantly, as at an old friend.

“Party!” he called. “In the castle, the big hall. All night! The headmaster says go, go up, enjoy, he does not want us on the ship tonight.” He beamed. “He is a very good headmaster tonight.”

They went on up, singing something in Bulgarian.

Lyra stood at the foot of the slope with her hand over the ring on her finger, which had been green since the podium and was green still; and looked out across the dark grounds to the lake, where the Durmstrang ship lay at anchor with one light burning in its stern, and the water black and quiet all round it.

“Malfoy,” said Crabbe again, more gently. “Come on.”

She went.

⁂

She did not sleep. She had not expected to.

The Durmstrang boys were still singing somewhere above the dungeons at three in the morning, and the Slytherin common room was full until two of people going over and over the podium, and Diggory, and whether a champion who Stunned another champion from behind could keep a cup, and whether the Hufflepuffs would ever speak to Gryffindor again. Lyra sat by the fire with a book until they had all gone to bed. Then she went to bed herself, and lay behind her hangings with the ring on her hand, and watched it be green.

It stayed green all night. Safe. Wherever he was, up in his tower with his leg bandaged and the Cup on the end of his bed, he was safe, and it said so, steadily, hour after hour, as if that settled anything.

At a little after four the window of the dormitory, which looked out under the lake, went from black to a deep greenish grey, and Lyra got up and dressed and went up through the sleeping castle and out of the front doors on to the steps.

The grounds were grey and wet and empty. There was mist lying on the grass in long low skeins, and the maze stood up out of it on the far side of the lawn, dark and dripping, and the sky over the Forest was the colour of pewter going pale. Nothing moved anywhere. It was so quiet she could hear the water.

She went down the steps and across the lawn to where she could see the lake.

It was flat and grey and perfectly still, and it went all the way out to the mountains on the other side with nothing on it.

The Durmstrang ship was gone.

Where it had lain at anchor since October, with its sails furled and its portholes lit, there was only a wide patch of water a little darker than the rest, turning very slowly round on itself, as water turns where something large has gone down through it, or out of it, not long ago.

Lyra stood on the wet grass and looked at it.

Behind her, the front doors of the castle had opened. She heard voices, and feet, and turned, and there were Durmstrang boys coming out on to the steps in ones and twos, in their red robes over their pyjamas, rumpled and yawning, some of them with blankets round their shoulders from wherever they had slept on the floor of the Great Hall. They came down the steps and stopped, one after another, and looked at the lake.

Nobody said anything.

Viktor Krum came out last. He walked down past the others, and across the lawn, and stopped a little way off from Lyra at the edge of the grass, with his arms folded, and stood looking at the empty water where his ship had been.

After a while he turned his head and looked at her. She did not know what he saw in her face. He looked at her for some time, and then he looked back at the lake, and the two of them stood there in the grey morning, a yard apart, with the mist on the grass and the water turning slowly round and round over the place where the ship had been.
