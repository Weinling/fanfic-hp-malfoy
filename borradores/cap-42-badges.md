# Chapter 42: Badges

The Hogwarts owls came on a Tuesday in August, two of them together, and Draco heard them before anybody else did and was out of his chair and at the window with his napkin still tucked into his collar.

“That’s *mine*,” he said. “That one’s mine. Lyra, that’s mine, the big one—”

It was not the big one. The big one, a tawny school owl with an air of having flown a great deal further than it liked, came down on Lyra’s side of the table and stood on the toast rack, and the small brown one went to Draco, who did not mind in the least, since it had his name on it in green ink. *Mr D. Malfoy, The Second Bedroom on the Left, Malfoy Manor, Wiltshire.* He had been waiting for that envelope since the night of his birthday, in June, when he had sat up until two in the morning in case it came early, and he held it in both hands now and did not open it, as though opening it might make it a little less his.

“Open it, darling,” said their mother.

“In a minute.”

Lyra’s was heavier. She knew why before she had broken the seal; there was something in the bottom of it, flat and hard, that slid when she tipped the envelope, and it came out on to the tablecloth beside her plate with a small definite click, like a coin put down to settle a bill.

A badge. Silver, with a serpent on it, and a large P.

There was a note from Professor McGonagall in her square, unhurried hand, which said the usual things about responsibility and example; and folded inside it, a second note, much shorter, in a hand Lyra had seen at the bottom of three years of essays and never once at the top of a letter.

*Miss Malfoy. You will find the duties tedious and the authority considerable. I trust you will not confuse the two. S. Snape.*

Her father had seen the badge from the other end of the table. He put down his cup.

“Well,” he said, “well, well,” and got up, and came round, and picked it up off the cloth and turned it to the light as he had turned the omnioculars once, to see the gold leaf on the dials. “Prefect. I was a prefect.” He smiled down at her. “Severus, I suppose. He has an eye.”

“I suppose so, Father.”

“The run of the castle,” said her father, and put the badge down in front of her, very gently, as one sets down a piece one has just been given in a game. “At any hour. Corridors nobody else may walk. Doors that open for the badge.” He touched her hair. “That will be useful.”

Lyra looked at the P on the tablecloth.

She had wanted to be a prefect since she was eleven, because it was a thing worth being, and because Granger would certainly be one. She had not expected, when it came, to think first about whom she could meet at midnight without anybody asking why.

“I got a list,” said Draco, who had at last opened his envelope and spread its contents over his eggs. “Look, Lyra. *One plain pointed hat (black) for day wear.* What else would it be for? Night wear?”

There was a third letter, which had come with the school owls without being a school letter, on very thin paper in a hand that leaned forward as though it were running somewhere. It was four lines long and came from Sofia, and Lyra knew who it was from before she had unfolded it, because one of the four lines was in an alphabet she could not read.

*Miss Malfoy. I am home. My father says the ship is a disgrace to the school and he is right. I hope you fly this summer. Please, is the girl from the library well. V. Krum.*

“Who’s that from?” said Draco.

“Nobody.” Lyra put it in her pocket. She was smiling, which she had not done at that table since July.

She would answer it. She would tell him that Granger was a prefect, which Granger certainly was, and that she was perfectly well, which Lyra had no means of knowing and no intention of finding out; and that his ship had been a disgrace from the moment it came out of the lake, and that his headmaster had left his coat behind.

It would be the first letter she had finished all summer.

She had started eleven. She wrote them at night, at the desk by the window, on the thin paper, and they all began *Potter*, and she burnt every one in the grate before she got as far as the signature, because Pobble could read a blotter. The first had run to four sides. It had gone through the whole of his conduct since 1992, under headings, and it was the finest thing she had ever written.

About a week after the Owlery it had come to her what she ought to have said to *Who came?* By the end of July she had thought of nine things. Some of them were very good. She had a particular fondness for the sixth. She lay on her back in the dark and said them to the ceiling, with pauses for him to go white.

He had put his hand into the one place where she would have sworn nobody could reach, and taken it out, and shown it to her. She hoped that he was miserable. She hoped that he was thin. She hoped that he woke up every morning in that dreadful house, wherever it was, with nobody to fight with and nothing to look at; and she had only to think of it to find that she had stopped reading, and was standing at the window, looking down the long white drive, as though anybody could possibly be coming up it.

The letters got shorter. By August they were down to a side. The last, on the Sunday, said *You still owe me a tomorrow*, and nothing else, and she looked at that for some time, and held it to the candle herself.

The handkerchief was another matter. She had got into the way of going to sleep with it in her hand. It was a disgusting habit, and she had given it up on the twentieth of July, and taken it up again on the twenty-first.

Draco caught her on the back stairs after breakfast, which he only did when he had something to show that he was not supposed to.

“Don’t tell,” he said, and held it out, and snatched it back before she could take it, and then held it out again at arm’s length, so that she could read it but not touch it. It was a sheet of old cream paper, folded small. “He gave it me last night. For school. It’s a treasure hunt.”

“Who did?”

“The gentleman.” He hopped from one foot to the other on the narrow stair. “He says when he was at Hogwarts there were all sorts of places nobody knew about, and he wants to know if they’re still there, because he hasn’t been for simply *years*. And if I find them, I’m to tell him, and he’ll tell me what they were for. It’s a secret.”

Lyra read it over his arm.

It was a list, of a kind. *A corridor on the seventh floor, opposite a tapestry of trolls in tutus. A bust of a warlock in a wig. Walk past the bare wall three times, thinking of a place for hiding things.* There was more below, which Draco’s thumb was covering, and at the bottom a drawing of something small and round that might have been a crown or might have been a cake.

The writing was fine and narrow and very even, the shapes old-fashioned but every stroke firm. She had expected something like the hand on the sealed letter she had carried to Karkaroff: long loops, unsteady at the ends. This pen had not wavered once.

“Draco, where did he—”

“It’s *mine*.” He folded it up and put it inside his shirt. “He said it’s a secret. He said you’d want to take it off me because you always want to take everything off me, and you’ve got your own things to do.”

“Have I.”

“Father says.” Draco went past her up the stairs, two at a time. “Father says you’ve got work. Like the gentleman.”

Their father was crossing the landing above, with a letter in his hand, and he looked down the stairwell at the two of them and smiled, as he smiled at them both this summer, as if they were a pair of good investments that had come in early.

“Let him have his games, my treasure,” he said. “You have your own work, while he has those.”

Her mother sent for her that afternoon, which she had not done since Lyra was small and had needed to be told about something she had broken.

Her dressing room was at the end of the west corridor, all pale green silk and long mirrors and the smell of orris root, with the blinds down against the heat; and Narcissa Malfoy was sitting at her glass with her back to the door and her hair half down, and did not turn round. In the glass Lyra could see her face, and her own behind it, in the doorway.

“Shut the door, darling. And sit down. No, here. Where I can see you without the mirror.”

Lyra sat on the low stool beside the dressing table. Her mother opened a drawer, and took out a slip of paper, and laid it on the glass top between them.

Lyra knew it at once. She had carried it fourteen months, behind the tables of values; she had unfolded it so often that it had gone soft as cloth. *P.S. Who is asking? You may tell me anything. I would rather hear it from you.* And underneath, in her own hand, as small and upright as she had been able to make it: *It was Harry Potter who was asking.*

“I have read it,” said her mother, “a good many times.”

She did not pick it up. She sat with one finger on the edge of it, as on the edge of a card she had not yet decided to play.

“You told me nothing for a year, and then you told me that. I should like the rest of it now, please. Not about the boy.” Her mouth moved very slightly. “I know about the boy. Half of Wiltshire knows about the boy; the *Prophet* saw to that. About my cousin. What did he find?”

So Lyra told her.

She told it as Potter had told it to her, in a locked classroom on Hallowe’en with his tie crooked: plainly, and in the order it had happened, without making it smaller. An old house and an old elf. A cave in a cliff, and a wall that wanted blood. A black lake, and a little boat, and a stone basin, and a boy lying on the rock beside it who had been lying there fifteen years. A locket that was not the right locket. And inside the false one, a scrap of paper folded small, in Regulus Black’s own writing, that the Headmaster of Hogwarts had put in his pocket and kept.

Her mother listened to all of it without moving. When it was finished she sat for a while with her finger on the slip of paper and her eyes on the blind.

“He taught me to fly,” she said at last. “Regulus. Did you know that? Nobody else would. Bella said girls on brooms were vulgar, and my mother agreed with her, and he was two years younger than I was and took me out on the lawn at Grimmauld Place at five o’clock in the morning, every morning for a week, until I could do it.” She was quiet. “He was a very serious little boy. He never laughed at me once. He changed his mind about something, and it killed him, and he lay on a rock for fifteen years while I wore black for him at a memorial with no coffin in it.”

Lyra kept her hands in her lap.

“I should like to see his hand once more,” said her mother. “That is all. I should like to see what he wrote, at the end, when he knew. And there is nobody in this house,” she said, quite evenly, “who could ever ask Albus Dumbledore for anything. Not now. Not this summer.”

She turned her head then, and looked at Lyra properly, and it was the face from the platform.

“I looked at that boy on the platform. You saw me. I wanted to see the boy who went into a cave for my cousin. He is very small, for what he has done.” A pause. “And I wanted to see whether he would look at you. He did.”

Lyra looked at her hands.

“I said no to a country for you once,” said her mother. “Your father wanted Durmstrang. He had it all arranged, at eleven: Igor, and the furs, and a castle so far north that the sun forgets it in winter. I said you would go to Hogwarts or you would go nowhere, and that if he sent you anyway I should go with you and never come back. He believed me.” She picked up the slip of paper at last, and folded it, and put it back in the drawer, and shut it. “I never told you. I didn’t want you grateful. I find I want you to know it now.”

“Why now?”

Her mother stood up, and went to the window, and lifted the edge of the blind with one finger, and looked down at the lawn, where the path ran east between the yew hedges to the cottage at the end.

“Because there are things in this house now,” said Narcissa Malfoy, “that I cannot say no to. And I should like you to know that I can, when it is you.”

“What things, Mother?”

Her mother let the edge of the blind fall.

“Tell Pobble we shall want the fireplace on Saturday,” she said, “and wear the blue. Your father likes you in blue in photographs.”

Lyra looked towards the door. She had been brought up in that house; she knew when not to ask again.

They went to Diagon Alley on the Saturday, the four of them, by the Ministry fireplace, and Draco wore a bow tie.

He had chosen it himself. It was silver-grey and slightly crooked and he would not be talked out of it, though Lyra tried for the whole of breakfast; and when their mother came down and saw it she put her hand to her heart and said he looked exactly like his grandfather at his christening, and retied it for him, and told him that the first time Lyra was taken to Diagon Alley she had insisted on her very best frock, and had spun round and round in the middle of the street with her skirt flying out until her father had to catch her by the collar.

“You *didn’t*,” said Draco, enchanted.

“I was eleven.”

“You were *spinning*.”

Their mother insisted on a photograph, outside Flourish and Blotts, with a man from the shop holding the camera: Lucius with his cane, and Narcissa with her hand on Draco’s shoulder, and Draco squinting, and Lyra at the end, with her prefect’s badge in her pocket because she would not wear it in the street. In the photograph, when it was developed, the four of them would stand very still for a moment, and then Draco would turn his head and say something, and they would all, even Lyra, begin to laugh; and that was how it would stay, on the piano in the drawing room, for a long time.

Afterwards their parents went to Gringotts, and left the two of them at Florean Fortescue’s with a sundae each.

Draco wanted to know everything about Slytherin, at once, between spoonfuls: whether the dormitories really were under the lake, and whether the giant squid ever came to the windows, and whether first-years were allowed brooms if their fathers wrote to Professor Snape, and what one did if one was put in Hufflepuff by mistake. Lyra answered the first three, and said that nobody in their family had ever been put anywhere by mistake, and he believed her, which was the restful thing about Draco.

Lyra saw him from her table on the terrace, through the crowd, as one sees the one face in a crowd that one has been not looking for all morning.

He was on the other side of the street, outside the apothecary, in a knot of red hair. Weasleys: the mother with a list, the twins with a box of something that was smoking, Weasley himself, taller, and Granger with a pile of books. And in the middle of it, with his hands in his pockets, thinner than in June, Potter.

He was looking at her.

He must have seen her first. He was standing quite still in the middle of the pavement, with people going round him on both sides, and he had not looked away when she found him, and he did not now. Nobody was between them but the width of the street, and a woman with a pram, and a man selling something out of a tray.

Lyra did not get up. She did not look away either. She sat on the terrace at Florean Fortescue’s with a spoon in her hand and a sundae melting in front of her, and looked back at him across Diagon Alley.

Weasley said something to him, close to his ear. Potter did not answer. Then he took his hands out of his pockets, and stepped off the pavement.

“Stay there,” said Lyra.

“Why? Who’s—”

“Finish your ice cream. If you move from that chair I shall tell Mother what happened to the bow tie you had before this one.”

She laid her spoon in the saucer, and got up.

She was gone for eleven minutes. When she came back, both sundaes had been eaten, and Draco looked up at her with deep interest.

“You’ve gone pink,” he said. “Who was that?”

“Nobody.”

“Another nobody.” He licked his spoon. “You’ve got an awful lot of nobodies.”

In the carriage going home he fell asleep against her shoulder before they had cleared the gates, with ice cream on his cuff and the bow tie under one ear; and in his fist, which he had not opened since the shop, a sheet of old cream paper folded small, that she could have taken from him quite easily, and did not.
