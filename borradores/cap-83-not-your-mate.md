# Chapter 89: Not Your Mate

Ron Weasley had a chart.

It wasn’t his fault. It had been lent to him. Hermione had ruled it for him in the first week of June, on a double sheet of squared parchment, at a moment when she’d have ruled anything for anybody if it got her out of a room, with a column for the date and a column for the reason and a little key in the corner. He had meant to give it back. He’d only taken it to keep track of how many times he’d been angry with Harry Potter, because he had a feeling it was going to come in useful, for when they had the row.

By the last week of November it had a hundred and eighty-seven entries on it, and he was on his third sheet.

He wasn’t proud of it. He kept it in his Quidditch book. It started all right. *25 May: Knew about G. Knew about G. KNEW ABOUT G.* You could tell he had been pressing on. It got quieter after that, and there was a good bit in August where it was mostly *Didn’t write*, and *Nor did I*, and a *Sorry about Sirius (mine)*, which he’d put in, and crossed out, and put in, and then a long stretch in the autumn that just said *Same*. On the seventeenth of November he had got up in the Great Hall in front of six hundred people and gone and stood next to him, because of an owl, and he didn’t know what you put for that. In the end he’d written *Hedwig*, and left the reason blank. That wasn’t forgiving him. It was an owl.

So that was one thing. There were a few others.

He was captain of Gryffindor, for a start. It was the only thing he had ever wanted that he’d been given. He had a badge for it and a whistle and a very good view of what was wrong with everybody’s left side, and they’d lost to Slytherin by ten points in the worst weather since the Flood, with the best save of his life, and then they’d flattened Ravenclaw. He ought to have been enjoying that. He got up at six, and he stood in the freezing dark with his breath going up and his team shouting at him. He’d think: *This is it, then. This is what it’s like. I’m good at something.* Then he’d look round for someone to tell, and that was when he’d get out the chart.

His sister slept with the light on. That was another. Parvati had told him. She didn’t make a thing of it; she’d just got a small steady blue flame in a jam jar on her locker, that she had asked Hermione how to do. She came down in the mornings and ate her toast and laughed at the right places, and she scored nine goals against Ravenclaw, and every so often he’d see her stop, in the middle of a sentence, and look at her own hand. She’d stopped burning things and started keeping them, a whole box under her bed, everything she’d written since the ninth of November, all dated, and she went through it on Sundays. Nobody had told him who had done that to her. He was fairly sure two people knew. He had asked one of them, in a cupboard, with a mop in his hands, and he’d got that face.

*Nov. 11: “I just know.”* He had gone over that one twice.

And there was the other thing.

He found it in the Charms book. He’d stopped being surprised. It would be there on a Tuesday, most weeks, tucked in at the Substantive Charms, and he never saw anyone put it there. It was a slip of thick ribbed paper about the size of a Chocolate Frog card, and it had one line on it, in a clear upright beautiful hand that he’d have known in the dark. *Puddlemere by two hundred. Two Sickles.*

He hadn’t answered one since May. He had decided that. He’d been standing in a doorway at the time. She had said what she had said, in that voice, with her hair coming down, and he’d heard it, and he had known what she was. There’d been a good deal of what she was. Ginny had got a light on.

They just kept coming. They didn’t say anything. They never had. It was a bet about the League. They had started up again on the eighth of October, after the Wasps game, and nobody had asked her to. He would look at it. He’d look at the way that she did her *P*.

He didn’t throw them away. He’d got six. He didn’t know what you’d put for that, either.

The thing about Harry was that he had stopped being any trouble.

You’d have thought it would be a relief.
He’d stopped laughing. That wasn’t it, exactly. He didn’t laugh early, and he didn’t laugh late. He just had a very polite face. It was thinner, and it had got sort of smoothed down; he looked as if somebody had gone over him with a damp cloth. He got up at half past five. He did his essays, and they were good. He said “Thanks” when you passed him something. He’d sit at the end of the table by himself with a book propped up on the milk jug, and he didn’t eat, he refuelled. You’d see him put it in. He had stopped going down to the dungeon stairs. He’d stopped looking at the Slytherin table at all, except for once, every morning at about ten past eight, for roughly a minute, with no expression, as you’d look at the clock.

That was when he’d started getting the books.

They came from Grimmauld Place, by parcel, in brown paper, with Kreacher’s horrible little knots. Ron saw the first one on his bed. It was bound in something that he didn’t care to think about, and it had a smell. Harry read it in the Common Room, in the corner by the window, with a pencil, in full view of everybody. Nobody stopped him. He was a sixth-year doing extra Defence. He had a black notebook that he’d been carrying about since September, and he had got to the end of that and started another.

Hermione saw inside it on the twenty-first.

She didn’t tell Ron. She had got out of the way of telling him things. They were doing rounds together on the Thursday, the fourth floor and the fifth, because that was how the rota fell; she had drawn it up herself in May, and it hadn’t occurred to either of them to change it. You walked, about a yard and a half apart, and she held the lamp. They’d been up and down those stairs together sixty times since September, and he could have told you the whole of what they’d said. “That’s the east stair done.” “There’s a Fifth-year behind that tapestry.” “Your lace is undone.” He had been going to ask her something, in May. He had told someone on a moor. It had been a really good question.

She stopped on the landing, by the armour with no head.

He hadn’t been expecting that. Nobody used that window. It was very cold. She stood with the lamp held down at her side, and the light went up under her chin. She had got so thin that you could see the bones of her wrists.

“I’ve seen his notes,” said Hermione.

He looked at the armour. He looked at a place on the armour where there was a dent.

“It’s not what I thought. I thought it would be spells. It’s very neat. He’s got it all in columns, he’s got it divided up by what you’d need; he’s been reading about the Middle Ages. He’s looked up how long a person can stay conscious. He’s got tables. What hurts more, and for how much longer, and what you’d have to do so that they didn’t lose their reason too soon. There’s a page on what you can do to someone who’s afraid of dying. He’s done it the way that I’d do it, Ron. It’s the same tabs.” Her voice was perfectly dry. It had a kind of a shake at the very bottom, like a cup on a saucer. “It isn’t rage. I’d know what to do about that. He isn’t even unhappy. It’s the first thing he’s been good at since June.”

There was a draught coming from somewhere. It moved the flame.

He ought to have said something. He’d got a lot of things. He’d had a hundred and eighty days, and they were all in his book. He turned round, and he looked at her, at the small white straight face. He had been looking at that face since he was eleven. He knew what it did when it had got something wrong in Arithmancy. He’d seen it across a fender at three o’clock in the morning, with its hand on his hair.

“How long did you know?” said Ron.

She didn’t pretend not to understand. He’d give her that.

“From the eleventh of November,” said Hermione. “Last year. Till the twenty-fifth of May. A hundred and ninety-six days.”

“You counted.”

“Of course I counted.”

“You sat on my *bed*. You let me fall asleep on your— You watched me eat a chip.” It had started to come up. It had been down there for six months, and he’d thought he had got it nailed. “He’d told you he had let it happen. To *Ginny*. You knew that. You were at Grimmauld Place for Christmas, you sat there with my mum, she gave you a *jumper*. You looked at my sister across the table, and you passed her the sprouts! Every single day! And I said to you, I actually said, in March, I said, *I’m glad that you’re— that it’s all* — You said, *Yes*. You looked me in the face!”

His voice was going round the stairwell. He stopped.

She hadn’t moved. Her eyes had filled, slowly, right up to the edge, and they stayed there. She didn’t do anything about it.

“Yes,” said Hermione.

He waited.

“I’m not going to say I’m sorry I knew. I want to be clear. I made him tell me, I locked a door. I’d do that again. He’d have gone mad, and I don’t think you can imagine how close it was, and he was my friend. I said I’d lie to you, and he made me swear it, in writing, and it was the right decision on the evidence that I had. I’ve been through that, Ron. I’ve been through it more than you’d believe.” She took a small breath. It caught on something. “But I could have looked at the floor. That’s what I’ve got. I could have just gone on knowing it, and looked at the floor, or gone out of the room. I didn’t need to look you in the face. I found I could do that, and it worked, and you believed me. You’ve got such an honest— You thought I was happy. I *was* happy. I was sitting there with your head on my knee, with that in my notebook, and I was so happy. That’s what I can’t get to come out.”

It had run over. It went down by the side of her nose, and she let it.

“I’m sorry about your face,” said Hermione.

He hadn’t got any idea what you did. Nobody tells you. There was a thing that you’d do, if it was a film, or Bill. He had got a lump in his throat like a Bludger, and his hands were enormous. He’d got them hanging on the ends of his arms. He looked at the bones of her wrist. He’d have liked to pick that up. He’d have liked to pick up the whole of her and put her in his pocket, and at the same time he’d have liked to walk straight out of a window; and there was his sister, upstairs, with a jam jar.

He felt in his robes. It was there, with the Puddlemere fixtures. He took it out. It had gone soft along the folds. Three sheets of squared parchment, ruled in two colours, with a small neat key.

“I’ve been meaning to give you that back,” said Ron.

She looked at his hand, holding it out, not very steady. Very slowly she put up her thin scarred arm and wiped her face on her sleeve, and she took the thing from him and unfolded it and held it to the lamp. He saw her see *Same*. He saw her go down the whole of that autumn, line by line, with her lips pressed together, to a date at the bottom, the twenty-first of November, that he hadn’t filled in.

She didn’t say anything. She got out her pen, laid the sheet on the headless shoulder of the armour, and ruled a new line, very straight, and a fresh column at the end of it. She blew on it, and held it out.

“You’d run out of room,” said Hermione.

He looked at it for a bit. Then he took it back.

“That’s the fourth floor done,” said Ron.

“Your lace is undone,” said Hermione.

He was starving, so he went down to the kitchens.

It was the Sunday after, about half past nine. You could do that, these days. You went along by the Hufflepuff barrels and tickled the pear; and if you turned left you got the long room, with the Society’s notice on the door. If you turned right you got four house-elves bowing, and a smell that would have made a Dementor sit down and cry. He turned right. He’d been doing a lot of that.

“They *like* it,” someone was saying, in a slow voice.

“That’s not an argument, Greer! They liked the Cruciatus, I expect, if they’d been brought up to think it was for their own good!”

“Pass us the nutmeg.”

There were two people at the end of the long table. One was very large and had flour on her eyebrows. She was doing something to a slab of pastry, with her sleeves rolled up over her big soft arms; it was a slow, thoughtful, leaning kind of motion, like somebody pushing a cow. The other was sitting up on a stool, with a bag of woolly hats at her feet and a burnt arm, and her hair had started to come down; and she had got a wooden spoon in her hand, and was shaking it. Three elves stood in a row along the wall, looking at her, with their huge eyes shining. They had the expressions of people watching a really good firework.

Ron stopped. He had been in that kitchen a fair number of times, and he’d seen Hermione Granger try to give a hat to a house-elf. He hadn’t seen her enjoying it. She’d got a bit of colour. She looked about fifteen.

“It’s *structural*,” Hermione was saying. “That’s what you won’t see. You think that if you’re kind to them, one at a time—”

“You put too much sugar in that,” said Greer Goyle.

“I—what? In what?”

“The custard. Friday. You always do. You think it wants more because you’re cross.” She turned the pastry a quarter of the way round. She hadn’t looked up. “Hello, Weasley. There’s a pie.”

There was. It was on a rack by the range, the size of a small shield, with leaves on it: steak and something, with a crust that broke with a noise like walking on frost, and he sat down on the end of the bench with a plate, and for about four minutes he wasn’t anybody’s captain. He ate it with his eyes shut. When he opened them she was standing on the other side of the table with her hands in a cloth, looking at him, with her head a bit on one side. She’d got small slow eyes, and he had never really looked at them. She was Malfoy’s, she’d always been Malfoy’s, she had sat next to it for five and a half years.

“Well?” said Goyle.

“That’s the best thing I’ve ever eaten,” said Ron thickly. “I mean it. That’s better than Mum’s. Don’t tell Mum.”

Something happened to her big pale face. It took a long time. It started somewhere around the chin, and it went on going up. She took out a pencil from her hair, and got a fat black notebook from her apron, and she licked the point, and she wrote something down.

“Sixpence a slice,” said Goyle. “When we’ve got the shop.”

He’d got the fork halfway to his mouth. He looked at Hermione, and Hermione looked at the ceiling. She’d got a very small smile on her, that she was trying to do something about.

“What shop?” said Ron.

But she’d gone back to her pastry.

It was the twenty-eighth of November, and he knew what it was going to be as soon as he came in.

He’d gone along because he went. It was a Thursday, and there were about forty of them by now, in the long room behind the kitchens, what with the first-years and the Ravenclaws and everyone who’d read about Madam Bones. They were doing Disarming, down at the far end. Neville was going up and down with his sleeves rolled and that pot under his arm, saying “Sorry. Your other left.” It smelt of cold dust and bacon. There was a good noise, a lot of people shouting *Expelliarmus* and missing. Somebody’d got Dean’s wand in the rafters.

Harry had finished with the Patronus lot at eight. He’d had them for an hour, and he’d been very clear, very patient and very good. He hadn’t gone. He was standing on the white line, looking at something. Ron followed his eye. It was the dummy, a big stuffed canvas thing, in an old Auror’s coat, that Cedric had got from somewhere, with a turnip for a head.

“D’you want to see something?” said Harry.

He didn’t say it very loudly. He’d said it to a couple of fourth-years. That was the funny thing. It was just his ordinary voice. It carried right down the room, and they all stopped.

Ron couldn’t have told you what he did. That was what he’d think about. He had seen Harry Potter send a Bludger through a window and hit a dragon in the eye, and he’d have said he knew what it looked like, and it wasn’t any of that. There wasn’t a flash. Harry walked up to it and looked at it for a moment, with his head a little on one side, the way you’d look at a shelf you were going to put up. He said something, very quietly, and moved his wand about an inch.

He said something else, and moved it again. He was explaining. That was what got you. He was talking the whole time, in that nice clear tired voice, the one he’d used for *happy memories*. You’d want to start here, he said. You didn’t want the big muscles; people always went for the big muscles and it was a waste. It was better to find something with a lot of nerves, and to take your time. If you did this, it would hold, and then you could do this. There was no blood, of course, only canvas and straw, and a slow sort of creeping and parting in the coat. The turnip had started very slowly to come apart, in thin neat curling strips, from the crown down. You’d want to leave the eyes till last.

Nobody was making any noise at all.

There was a first-year next to Ron. She was about four foot high, and she had got hold of his sleeve. He didn’t think she knew. He could hear someone breathing, very fast, through their mouth, and he looked round, and it was Seamus. Ron’s stomach had gone cold. He’d been on a moor with this person. He had been told that it was a good idea, once, to stand on a chair with him and sing about a hippogriff.

Harry stepped back. He looked at what he’d done, with his wand held down by his side. He didn’t look pleased. That was what was so bad. He looked like Hermione with a sum.

“It’s not a thing that you need a lot of power for,” he said. “That’s what nobody tells you. You just need to want to. Who’d like to have a go? I’d need someone to hold the wand arm. Anyone?”

He looked along the row. He had very green eyes, and they weren’t looking at anybody much. They came to rest on someone long and mild and spotty, standing against the wall.

“Hopkins,” said Harry. “You’ve got a reason, if anyone has.”

There wasn’t a sound. Wayne Hopkins looked at him, with a wand in his left hand. He looked at the dummy, and the strips of turnip lying on the boards, and he took his time; he’d got into the way of it. He had that small puzzled line between his eyebrows, as if someone had asked him the way to somewhere he’d never heard of. Ron thought he’d say *Sorry*. He’d have put money on it.

“No, thank you,” said Hopkins.

He put his wand in his sleeve. He picked up his bag, with his left hand, and he got the strap over his shoulder, which took a little while. He didn’t hurry. He walked up the length of the room, past Harry, about a yard away from him, not looking; and he opened the door, and went out, and he shut it carefully, so that it wouldn’t bang.

Susan Bones went next. She didn’t even look at the dummy. Then it was a big Ravenclaw, and the Patil twin. It wasn’t anything that you could have put your finger on. They just started to go, by twos and threes, not saying anything, finding their bags, and keeping close to the wall. Somebody was crying, one of the little ones.

Neville had come up the room.

He had got rather white. It made his jaw look more crooked than ever. He put the pot down on the floor, and he stood there on the white line, about four feet from Harry, and for a moment Ron thought he was going to go for him. He didn’t. He turned his back. He stood with his big clumsy hands hanging, and looked at the people who were left, the second-years and a couple of Gryffindors and a very small boy in a bow tie; and his voice shook, and it went on.

“Right,” said Neville. “Sorry. Where were we? It’s all right, Tamsin. It’s only a turnip. Wands up. Elbows in. We were doing your other left.”

They did it, God knows how. Ron got his wand out and stood up next to a first-year, and his hand wasn’t all that steady. *Expelliarmus*. Harry stayed where he was for a bit, and then picked up his book and walked out. Only one person didn’t move. He was standing by himself near the wall, with his hands at his sides and his tie perfectly straight, and he wasn’t looking at Neville. He had come a little way forward, and he had his head on one side, and he was looking at the turnip with the most careful and thoughtful attention, the way you’d look at something you might want to remember.

The next Thursday, at five to seven, Neville was standing in the passage outside the long room.

Ron saw it. He had come along early to help with the benches. There was the notice, with Lupin’s stamp, and the smell of bread, and Neville, by himself, in front of the door, with his hands behind him. He’d got his robes done up wrong. He’d been standing there for some time. You could tell; he had that look of having practised on a plant. Harry came down the passage from the stairs, with his bag on his shoulder and his hands in his pockets. He was reading something. He got to the door, and looked up.

Nobody said anything.

Neville had gone a slow, deep, painful red, from the collar up, and he looked as if he was going to be sick. He’d got thirty-seven people in that room, and some of them were eleven. He took a breath, and it went in a long way.

“Not you,” said Neville.

It came out as hardly anything. He swallowed, and he said it again, looking at a place on Harry’s tie.

“Sorry. Not you, Harry.”

Ron didn’t know what he’d been expecting. He thought there might be a look. Harry stood there with his finger in his book. His face didn’t change. He looked at Neville, for quite a long time, with those polite, tired, very green eyes, and it was the oddest thing: for just a moment, Ron thought, he looked relieved.

“No,” said Harry. “All right.”

He turned round, and went back the way that he had come.

They sat down at the corner table, that night, after the common room had thinned out.

It wasn’t a meeting. Nobody had called it. Ron had just been sitting there with a Puddlemere fixture list, and Neville had come in and lowered himself on to the sofa end like an old man. Ginny was on the floor with her back against the short-legged table, with her jam jar and her box, and Hermione had a Rune dictionary open at nothing. The fire had got low.

“I told him,” said Neville.

“We know,” said Ron.

“I’ve never— He sat with me. At the door, that time. For two hours. I didn’t even know why I’d sat down. He got me my—” He looked at his hands. There was a bit of bonemeal on one of them. “I can’t have him in there. There’s a little girl who won’t go past the turnips in the kitchen garden.”

Nobody said anything. The fire settled.

“He’s planning to kill Voldemort,” said Hermione. She said it to the dictionary. “That’s all it is, I suppose. It’s what we’ve all been hoping that somebody would do. I keep going over that.”

“He wants it to take a long time,” said Neville.

Ron looked at him. So did Hermione. He didn’t add anything to that. He’d got his round mild face turned towards the embers, and something at the side of it was jumping.

“Well, I’m not doing anything,” said Ron. It came out louder than he’d meant. A second-year looked round from the stairs. “I’m just saying. I’m not. What’s he ever— He stood there! I asked him! I asked him in a cupboard, about *her*, he looked me in the eye with my sister sitting there and said *I just know*. I’ve got it written down! He let her get the book! He let her walk around with it, for a year, because he needed a *tooth*. And now he’s gone off his head, right, fine, I’m sorry about that, I’m sorry about his owl. What’s that got to do with me? He’s got a nice new hobby. I don’t owe him anything. I went to Rannoch for him. I fixed his *roof*. What are you supposed to do with a person like that?”

No one answered. He sat there, breathing. He’d started to feel a bit stupid.

There was a small sound by the fender. Ginny had taken the lid off her jam jar, and put it on again. She was sitting with her knees up. He’d thought she wasn’t listening. The blue light lay along the side of her jaw, and she had a bit of a sticking plaster on one finger. She looked about nine, and she looked about forty.

“I don’t forgive him,” said Ginny.

She said it to the jar. It was quite conversational.

“I’m never going to. I’ve thought about it, and I don’t think it’s the kind of thing you can do. I’ve got him on a list. If you want to know what you’re supposed to do with someone like that, I couldn’t tell you. I was in a bathroom, on the second floor, for most of a year. I had the most wonderful friend. He was so clever, and he understood me, and I’d never been so happy in my life. I went about the whole of that school with chicken feathers on my robes. And you know what everybody did? They were all very nice. They said I looked peaky. Mum sent a jumper. Percy told me to have an early night.” She looked up. It went round them, one at a time, and it stopped on Ron. “Nobody sat down. That’s all. Nobody came and sat down next to me and stayed there and wouldn’t go away, and ate their tea. I’d have told them to get lost. I would have been horrible. I wouldn’t have said anything. I don’t know if it would’ve made any difference.”

She put the jar down, on the boards, quite gently.

“You don’t leave him on his own with it,” said Ginny. “I don’t know anything else. That’s just what I know. That’s what nobody did.”

He’d worked out what he was going to say. He had worked it out in the shower, with his forehead against the tiles.

It was Friday, at dinner, with a lot of noise and rain on the high windows, and something brown with dumplings. Harry was at the very end of the table, by the doors, where the draught came in, with a book propped on the salt. There was a space on the bench beside him about six feet long, and nobody had sat in it since September.

Ron took his plate. He’d got the whole team looking at him; he felt it between his shoulder blades. It was about the longest walk he’d ever done, and he had walked out in front of a Slytherin stand. He stepped over the bench and sat down, about a foot away, which was too close, and he didn’t move. He put his plate on the table and picked up his fork.

Harry didn’t look up. He finished his paragraph. He made a very small mark in the margin, with a pencil. He turned his head. The eyes came round, polite and green and rather tired. There was a thin white scar, at the corner of his mouth, that Ron had never noticed.

“What are you doing?” said Harry.

It wasn’t rude. He just seemed to want to know.

Ron looked at his dumpling.

He’d had a lot of good stuff ready. There had been a bit about Ginny. There was a thing that he’d been going to say about the roof. He could feel his ears. He’d got the most terrible feeling that he might cry, in front of a Hufflepuff; he took hold of his fork rather hard, and he looked straight ahead of him, at the wall, as he had once looked at the wall of a post office.

“I’m not your mate,” said Ron. “Just so you know. I’m just sitting here.”

There was a pause.

He cut up the dumpling. He’d got a potato on the side of his plate, a good one, a bit brown on the top. He stuck his fork in it. He leaned over, without looking, and put it down on the bare wood in front of Harry’s book. He went on eating.

He could feel it being looked at. It went on for a long time. Someone had dropped something, up by the staff table. He chewed. It tasted of nothing whatever.

“What happens after?” said Ron.

He hadn’t been going to say that. It just came out, with his mouth full. He swallowed. He still didn’t turn his head.

“When you’ve done it. All that. With the tables. Say it goes really well. Say you’ve got him tied to something and it’s taken three weeks. Then he’s dead. Fine. What are you going to do on the Thursday?”

He waited.

He’d known Harry Potter for five years and three months, or however you wanted to count it. He heard a very small sound beside him. It was the pencil. It had been laid down on the book.

Nothing else happened.

It went on not happening. He looked round, in the end; he couldn’t help it. Harry was looking at the potato, with his hands on either side of it. He just looked like a person who has gone up a long flight of stairs in the dark, and put his foot out for one more step, and it wasn’t there.

Ron finished his dinner. He ate the whole thing. He got up, and stepped back over the bench, and took his plate.

The next night he was there at six, with a Quidditch magazine. He didn’t say anything, and neither did Harry, and at some point the potato went. On the Sunday it was Neville, with a pot, and he talked to it. On the Monday it was Ginny, who sat down across from him and looked at him, with her very direct eyes, for forty minutes, and ate a whole treacle tart. There was a rota, pinned up inside the lid of Hermione’s Rune dictionary, in three colours, and she had Tuesdays. Nobody said it was for anything. Nobody forgave anybody. It was just that, from the first week of December onward, at every meal, whichever end of the table he went to and however fast he ate, there was someone sitting there, doing nothing, not being his mate.

*Dec. 6*, Ron wrote, on the fourth sheet, in the new column. He looked at it. He sucked his quill. *Sitting*.

He saw it on the thirteenth.

It was Friday, in the last double Potions of term, and it had been snowing since dawn. By three they’d lit the lamps, and the dungeon was all steam and green shadows, with everybody’s Draught of Peace going a bad colour. He’d only got in there by the skin of his teeth, on an E, because Snape was short of people who could still read. They had been told to bottle up and clear away. There was a queue for the sink. Slytherin were doing that thing where they all stood up at once.

He had his head in his bag. He’d lost his stirring rod. He looked up, for no reason, with his hand in a lot of old Chocolate Frog cards. He could see along the bench.

She had gone to the front with her phial and left her bag on the stool, that grey thing with the silver clasp, standing a bit open, with no one near it. Well. There was one person. He was going by, on his way to the sink, with his hands full of dirty glass and that polite shut tired face on, not looking at anything. He didn’t so much as slow down. It was only that as he went past the stool, his left hand opened, down by his side, and something about the size of a thumb dropped out of the sleeve. It had a kind of a gleam, like a bit of the inside of a watch, gold, and it went in at the top of the bag without a sound. He’d gone on. He was running the tap.

It had taken no time at all.

Ron stayed exactly as he was, with his hand in his bag.

He was a prefect. He had the badge on, with a dent in it. He’d been told about this, at some length, on the first of September in his fifth year, by somebody who’d got it out of a book: there was to be no passing of unlabelled substances, in class, at any time. He looked at the bag. He could go up there now and say, *Excuse me, Professor*. It was his job, and he knew what Hermione would have said; there’d have been a reference. He knew exactly what that stuff was. He’d seen it in a book, with a picture. It took six months. That meant June. Someone had started stirring it in the week that Sirius died, and hadn’t told a living soul.

And he had just given it away.

Malfoy had come back. She took up her bag, and hung it from her shoulder, the right one. She didn’t look in it. She was very thin. She’d got a pair of old grey woollen gloves on, for some reason, in that heat. She went by Ron’s bench, about a yard away, looking straight ahead of her with her chin up.

He found his stirring rod.

He had been a prefect for a year and four months, and it was the first thing he’d ever decided not to see. He didn’t put it on the chart. He didn’t know what you’d put. He got up, and he went and stood in the queue for the sink, behind a person he wasn’t speaking to, and he waited for his turn.
