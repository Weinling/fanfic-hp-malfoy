# Chapter 42: Badges

The Hogwarts owls came on a Tuesday in August, two of them together, and Draco heard them before anybody else did and was out of his chair and at the window with his napkin still tucked into his collar.

“That’s *mine*,” he said. “That one’s mine. Lyra, that’s mine, the big one—”

It was not the big one. The big one, a tawny school owl with an air of having flown a great deal further than it liked, came down on Lyra’s side of the table and stood on the toast rack, and the small brown one went to Draco, who did not mind in the least, since it had his name on it in green ink. *Mr D. Malfoy, The Second Bedroom on the Left, Malfoy Manor, Wiltshire.* He had been waiting for that envelope since the night of his birthday, in June, when he had sat up until two in the morning in case it came early, and he held it in both hands now and did not open it, as though opening it might make it a little less his.

“Open it, darling,” said their mother.

“In a minute.”

Lyra’s was heavier. She knew why before she had broken the seal; there was something in the bottom of it, flat and hard, that slid when she tipped the envelope, and it came out on to the tablecloth beside her plate with a small definite click, like a coin put down to settle a bill.

A badge. Silver, with a serpent on it, and a large P.

There was a note from Professor McGonagall in her square, unhurried hand, which said the usual things about responsibility and example; and folded inside it, a second note, much shorter, in a hand Lyra had seen at the bottom of three years of essays and never once at the top of a letter.

*Miss Malfoy. You will find the duties tedious and the authority considerable. I trust you will not confuse the two. S. Snape.*

Her father had seen the badge from the other end of the table. He put down his cup.

“Well,” he said, “well, well,” and got up, and came round, and picked it up off the cloth and turned it to the light as he had turned the omnioculars once, to see the gold leaf on the dials. “Prefect. I was a prefect.” He smiled down at her. “Severus, I suppose. He has an eye.”

“I suppose so, Father.”

“The run of the castle,” said her father, and put the badge down in front of her, very gently, as one sets down a piece one has just been given in a game. “At any hour. Corridors nobody else may walk. Doors that open for the badge.” He touched her hair. “That will be useful.”

Lyra looked at the P on the tablecloth.

It would. She could see exactly how useful. A girl who could walk the corridors of Hogwarts at midnight with a perfectly good reason could go anywhere in that castle, and see anybody, and nobody would think it worth remarking on. She had wanted to be a prefect since she was eleven, because it was a thing worth being, and because Granger would certainly be one. She had not expected, when it came, to look at it and think first about the corridors at night.

“I got a list,” said Draco, who had at last opened his envelope and spread its contents over his eggs. “Look, Lyra. *One plain pointed hat (black) for day wear.* What else would it be for? Night wear?”

There was a third letter, which had come with the school owls without being a school letter, on very thin paper in a hand that leaned forward as though it were running somewhere. It was four lines long and came from Sofia, and Lyra knew who it was from before she had unfolded it, because one of the four lines was in an alphabet she could not read.

*Miss Malfoy. I am home. My father says the ship is a disgrace to the school and he is right. I hope you fly this summer. Please, is the girl from the library well. V. Krum.*

“Who’s that from?” said Draco.

“Nobody,” said Lyra, and put it in her pocket, and found that she was smiling, which she had not done at that table since July.

She would answer it. She would tell him that Granger was a prefect, which Granger certainly was, and that she was perfectly well, which Lyra had no means of knowing and no intention of finding out; and that his ship had been a disgrace from the moment it came out of the lake, and that his headmaster had left his coat behind.

Draco caught her on the back stairs after breakfast, which he only did when he had something to show that he was not supposed to.

“Don’t tell,” he said, and held it out, and snatched it back before she could take it, and then held it out again at arm’s length, so that she could read it but not touch it. It was a sheet of old cream paper, folded small. “He gave it me last night. For school. It’s a treasure hunt.”

“Who did?”

“The gentleman.” He hopped from one foot to the other on the narrow stair. “He says when he was at Hogwarts there were all sorts of places nobody knew about, and he wants to know if they’re still there, because he hasn’t been for simply *years*. And if I find them, I’m to tell him, and he’ll tell me what they were for. It’s a secret.”

Lyra read it over his arm.

It was a list, of a kind. *A corridor on the seventh floor, opposite a tapestry of trolls in tutus. A bust of a warlock in a wig. Walk past the bare wall three times, thinking of a place for hiding things.* There was more below, which Draco’s thumb was covering, and at the bottom a drawing of something small and round that might have been a crown or might have been a cake.

It was not the gentleman’s hand. She had seen the gentleman’s hand once, on the front of a letter she had carried up a gangplank, long-looped and a little unsteady, the hand of an old man. This was fine and narrow and very even, a hand that had never shaken in its life; old in its shapes, and young in the pressure of the pen, as though it had been written a long time ago by somebody who was not old yet.

“Draco, where did he—”

“It’s *mine*.” He folded it up and put it inside his shirt. “He said it’s a secret. He said you’d want to take it off me because you always want to take everything off me, and you’ve got your own things to do.”

“Have I.”

“Father says.” Draco went past her up the stairs, two at a time. “Father says you’ve got work. Like the gentleman.”

Their father was crossing the landing above, with a letter in his hand, and he looked down the stairwell at the two of them and smiled, as he smiled at them both this summer, as if they were a pair of good investments that had come in early.

“Let him have his games, my treasure,” he said. “You have your own work, while he has those.”
