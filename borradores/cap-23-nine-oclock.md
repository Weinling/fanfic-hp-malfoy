# Chapter 23: Nine o’Clock

It came by six owls, at breakfast, and it was a broom.

They came down the Great Hall in a line, carrying it between them like a standard in a procession, and laid it across the Slytherin table among the toast racks; and the seventh owl, her father’s, landed on the marmalade and held out its leg with the air of a herald.

*My dear Lyra,*

*It seems the hunt for Sirius Black is called off. I confess there is something about the business I do not altogether like, but the Ministry has decided he is no longer anybody’s concern, and I understand Dumbledore had a hand in deciding it.*

*A clever girl like you will already have stepped back and be watching from a safe distance; I mention it only in case you were curious.*

*I have sent you something. Call it a late Christmas present. And you are not to mope about the Quidditch. One loses a match in January in order to win the next one handsomely. Next time, simply crush them.*

*Your mother sends her love, and reminds you that you owe her a letter.*

*Your loving father*

So that was how the Ministry meant to say it: by not saying it. Lyra had read every page of the *Daily Prophet* at breakfast for nine days running, and found a great deal about Quidditch and a goblin dispute in Bristol, and not one word about Peter Pettigrew, who was alive, or about Sirius Black, who had done nothing at all. The Ministry did not care to tell anybody that it had locked the wrong man in Azkaban for twelve years and then spent most of a year failing to catch him again; and whatever oath or charm or tangle of old magic lay behind the mistake, it would hardly look better in print.

So there would be no announcement. One morning the Dementors had simply been gone from the gates, and the cold had gone out of the drive with them. The Aurors had gone back to London. The search was, in the Ministry’s own phrase, suspended pending review. Albus Dumbledore had gone up to town twice in the same week, which a few people noticed, and come back looking pleased with himself, which nobody could help noticing.

And her father, who had friends all over the Ministry and heard everything first, had written the day the search was called off. Her father had never in his life told her where to stand unless somebody had first told him where she had been standing. *A clever girl like you will already have stepped back.* Lyra thought of Snape on the shore, with his fingers at his chin, looking at the feathers on her robes.

Then she looked at the present, and decided to be pleased about that instead.

It was a Firebolt. She knew what it was before the last of the paper was off: she had seen it in the window in Diagon Alley in the summer, with a crowd of grown wizards in front of it, breathing on the glass. Birch, ash, a handle like dark water, a braking charm nobody had ever broken, and no price on the card at all, only *on application*, which in her father’s world meant exactly what it said. He had bought the whole team new Nimbuses last year. She suspected this one broom had cost more than all seven.

He had seen her moping about the house at Christmas, and heard about Hufflepuff in January, and put the two together and got the wrong answer.

*It was Harry Potter and Sirius Black*, thought Lyra, running her thumb along the handle. *And that’s all been sorted out since.*

She did not correct him. A Firebolt was, when one came to think about it, the only broom really suited to her, and she was secretly delighted. She would write him four pages of thanks in her best hand, that evening. Just now there was the Great Hall to think of.

The Great Hall went to pieces.

The Slytherins came first, crowding down the bench; then half the second-years from the other Houses, and a row of Ravenclaws standing on their bench to see over the heads. Marcus Flint had planted himself beside the broom with his arms out and was batting away hands like a man defending the last cake at a funeral.

“Merlin. A *Firebolt*. Parkinson, if you touch it I will break your fingers off and post them to your mother.”

Panther Parkinson snatched his hand back and went on gazing.

“We’ll murder Ravenclaw with this,” he said, quite dreamily. “Chang flies a *Comet*.”

“Lyra, can I just—?”

Ordinarily she would not have dreamed of it. But it was a particularly good morning, and she let them, graciously, one at a time. Flint stood over every one of them like a bailiff.

“*Wipe your hands.* You—you’ve had a kipper, I can smell it from here, *get back*—”

Every time somebody sighed over it, Lyra sat up a little straighter.

A broom did not make a flyer; she knew that perfectly well. But a broom like this would let a flyer show every last thing she could do, and if anybody wanted to say that the Malfoys had bought her a place at the top, they were welcome. Some people had talent. Some people had money. It had never been explained to her why the second was supposed to be the more embarrassing.

She was enjoying all of it very much when a voice said, “Oh, is that a Firebolt?”

Harry Potter came over the end of the Slytherin bench like somebody stepping over a garden wall. Flint’s face set.

“Not you. You don’t touch it. You don’t *breathe* on it.”

“Come off it, Flint. You’ve already beaten us. I only want a look.”

“After the last time—”

Flint glanced at Lyra as he said it. He did not know what had gone on between the two of them, but he knew it had put his Seeker off her game for most of a term, and he had Ravenclaw coming up.

“Leave him, Marcus,” said Lyra. “Let him look, if he wants to.”

Nothing pleased her more than letting an enemy see exactly what she had. Flint knew that, and stood aside, though he went on glaring. Potter came through the crowd. Parkinson put a shoulder in his way, not quite by accident, and Potter hooked a foot round his ankle so that he sat down suddenly on the bench, and arrived at Lyra’s side quite unruffled.

“It really is.” For once, even he looked impressed. “That’s beautiful. Look at the line of it. It looks as if it’s going fast standing still.”

Naturally he was impressed. Anybody would be, seeing a Firebolt for the first time; one paid a work of art its due, rival or no rival.

Lyra was, at that moment, the most pleased person in the Great Hall.

“What a pity I didn’t have it in November. I’d have flattened you, and you wouldn’t even have had the weather to blame.”

“I always thought your Nimbus was a bit ordinary for you,” said Potter. “Half the school’s got one. This, though.” He nodded at it. “This is about right.”

So many pleasant things had happened lately that Lyra was quite carried away. She gave him her most generous smile.

“If you ask me very nicely, I might even let you have a go on it.”

“Oh, I’m honoured. Very kind. But no need.”

He stepped back out of the crowd as easily as he had come into it, patted Parkinson consolingly on the head on his way past, waved a hand in front of Flint’s nose, and went off down the Hall.

Before he sat down at the Gryffindor table, though, he turned round, and smiled at her across the whole of the Great Hall.

“Practise hard, Malfoy.”

She could feel her face trying to do something and would not let it.

“You’d better do some practising yourself,” she called back, “or you’ll look very silly next to this.”

Potter laughed, and turned away. Lyra sniffed, and that was all anybody saw.

A little way down the table, Goyle had noticed something in the air. She leaned across to Crabbe.

“When did those two make it up?”

“No idea,” said Crabbe. She looked, for Crabbe, extraordinarily pleased with herself. “But I think I made a rather good Cupid.”

“A rather good *what?*”

Goyle stabbed a sausage and ate it in two bites. Anyway, it meant no more of Lyra’s moods, which was something; and the pair of them began to edge along the bench towards her, to find out what in the world had happened.

They reached her by the end of breakfast. Goyle asked a great many questions and got no answers. Crabbe asked nothing. But when the bell went and they were gathering up their bags, Lyra said to her, without looking round, “You asked me once whether I was in trouble.”

Crabbe stopped with her bag half on her shoulder. “In October. With the owl.”

“I’m not,” said Lyra. “Not any more.”

It was not quite true. There was a goblet she had not paid for. But it was truer than it had been since October, and Crabbe, who had waited four months for an answer without once asking again, looked at her and nodded. It would do.

Professor Lupin had missed a week of lessons after the full moon. Lyra had taken the long way round to Charms all that week, so as not to pass his door.

On the Monday he came back, she was up before anybody else, and went the short way after all. She left a bar of dark chocolate outside his office door: Honeydukes’ best, in gold paper. She did not leave a note. She had never liked dark chocolate; it tasted of medicine. It was the only thing she could think of that he would understand and nobody else would.

In Defence that afternoon he looked grey and tired and kind, and did not look at her once. Only at the end, while they were packing up, he said to the room in general, quite mildly, “Thank you,” and went on stacking his books.

It paid nothing. She knew that. It was a receipt, not a payment.

In Potions, Snape did not say *Faultless*. He did not say anything to her, that week or the next. Now and then she caught him looking at her across the dungeon, as though she were a sum he had nearly finished checking.

The Ravenclaw match was closer than it had any business being.

Ravenclaw had come out with a plan. It was a beautiful plan, all angles and decoys, and they had plainly practised it until they could fly it asleep, and for the first hour it took the Slytherin Chasers apart and put them back together the wrong way round. At one point the board said twenty to ninety. From up near the clouds, Lyra watched another goal go through the Slytherin hoops and did the sum. Half an hour more of that and the Snitch would not be enough.

She had been leaving the points to the team. She stopped.

Everybody knew where Ravenclaw was soft. They drilled better than anybody and thought faster than anybody, but their Seeker was a fourth-year on an old Comet who had not caught the Snitch once all season. When Lyra saw it, a fleck of gold low over the far hoops, Cho Chang saw it too, and went for it, and was very nearly quick enough. Lyra went past her as though she had been parked, and took the Snitch out of the air a yard from the grass.

Afterwards Chang sat on the pitch with her face in her knees and cried, and some of the Ravenclaws stood round patting her without much conviction.

It was hard on her, really. She was not a bad Seeker, for a fourth-year on a Comet.

“It was only that it was me,” said Lyra, to nobody in particular.

The score was nothing to boast about. But a team, as far as Lyra was concerned, was mostly there to set her off to advantage, and she had pulled the match out of the fire on her own. She came down off the Firebolt into a roar of green and silver, and did not let it show, much.

She thought that, just then, she could have cast a Patronus without anybody holding her hand.

The Cup came down to the last match of the season: Gryffindor against Hufflepuff.

The arithmetic was horrible. Slytherin had won two and lost one, and Ravenclaw had lost all three. Hufflepuff had won two. Gryffindor had won one and lost one, and had lost it to Slytherin, in the storm, by two hundred and forty points. If Gryffindor won by more than two hundred, the Cup went to Gryffindor. If Gryffindor won by two hundred or less, it went to Slytherin. If Hufflepuff won, it went to Hufflepuff.

So Slytherin found itself cheering for Gryffindor, which was unnatural, but only up to a point, after which it would have to cheer for Hufflepuff, which was worse. A sixth-year solved it by charming two hundred little banners, scarlet on one side and yellow on the other, and handing them round the stand. You waved the red side for as long as it was safe, and turned it over the moment Gryffindor looked like winning by too much.

“Honestly,” said Lyra, sitting down with hers. “If it weren’t for the points, I would sooner be sick over the barrier than cheer for Harry Potter.”

Crabbe and Goyle looked at each other, and held a long, silent conference about how much of that was true. They came, after careful deliberation, to Crabbe’s conclusion: that something rather nice had happened between those two.

Down on the grass, the two Seekers stood side by side.

Potter was on a school broom, one of the old Cleansweeps from the shed, with its bristles thin and its paint worn off. Lyra had heard Wood had nearly wept about it. Diggory held out his hand with his best smile.

“All right, Harry? Good luck. Best man wins.”

Potter did not smile back.

“Clever trick, that, in January,” he said. “Chatting to the other Seeker. I’d never have thought of it. I thought I’d try it.”

Something went across Diggory’s face. “You mean Malfoy.”

“Got it in one. I think we’ve got a few things to talk about. Shall we have a walk before we start?”

Madam Hooch’s whistle went. “Mount your brooms! Three—two—one—”

Twelve players rose into the air. The two Seekers did not. They walked off across the pitch side by side, each with a broom over one shoulder, like two men going to look at a field they were thinking of buying.

Both teams stared down at them.

“*Harry!* What are you *doing?*”

“*Cedric?*”

But a Seeker could go where he liked, and nothing in the rules said he had to be off the ground. Madam Hooch threw up the Quaffle, the Chasers fell on it, and within a minute everybody had forgotten all about the Seekers, who went on strolling across the grass, talking.

Nobody heard what they said. Lyra watched them from the stand, and could see Diggory’s face, which kept changing, and Potter’s, which did not.

Then Potter got on his broom, and Diggory got on his, and they kicked off together and went up like rockets.

It took two hours.

Lyra had come to watch a Seeker, and for most of the afternoon there was no Seeker to watch. Potter was everywhere else. He flew wing to wing with the Weasley twins, a red wedge that went straight through the Hufflepuff Chasers. He went into a howling dive after nothing, and pulled Diggory and both Hufflepuff Beaters down after him, and left them in a knot ten feet above the grass while he slid quietly away. Every time Diggory so much as narrowed his eyes, a Bludger came at him out of nowhere, and the twin who had hit it was always looking at Potter. Diggory lost the Snitch five times.

Hufflepuff could only win one way, and everybody on the pitch knew it: Diggory had to catch the Snitch. So the Hufflepuff Beaters hung about Diggory like a pair of nursemaids, and nobody was minding the Quaffle, and Potter, who was a very good Seeker, turned out to be a passable Chaser, a passable Beater, and a thoroughly annoying Keeper as well. He never once touched the Quaffle. He only flew into the middle of the Hufflepuff side and stayed there, opening holes, and once, when it suited him, he stopped a pass with his chest and looked very surprised about it.

Down in the Slytherin stand, the banners went red, and red, and redder, and then began, nervously, to turn yellow.

By the time Diggory changed his mind and went for the Quaffle himself, Gryffindor were nearly a hundred and fifty up. After that it was a brawl from one end of the sky to the other, and Hufflepuff clawed it back to sixty; and Potter went straight up through the middle of everything and closed his hand.

“*GRYFFINDOR WIN THE CUP!* Three hundred and seventy to a hundred and sixty! *Gryffindor win the Cup!*”

The Slytherin stand sat with its banners half turned over and its mouths open.

Ten points fewer and the Cup would have been Slytherin’s. Lyra could not pretend she did not mind.

It was only then, with her banner gone yellow in her lap, that she did the sum he had been doing all afternoon. More than two hundred, he had needed, and the Snitch was worth a hundred and fifty. He had not been looking for it at all. He had been waiting for sixty. Lyra had sat through two hours of it, a Seeker herself, and seen none of it.

And yet, somewhere a long way down, something in her was fizzing.

He had been angry with Diggory. It was perfectly plain why: the trick in January, the friendly chatter, the hand on her arm. Lyra thought tricks like that were part of the game, and still thought so. It did not stop her enjoying, very much, the sight of Cedric Diggory being run ragged for two hours.

The players came down. Both teams lay about on the grass like the survivors of a shipwreck, Gryffindor rather more cheerfully. Diggory was sitting on the ground, soaked through. Potter walked over to him.

Diggory looked up, and gave a great helpless laugh.

“That was a complete thrashing. So that’s how it’s done.”

“Well, now you know.”

Potter held out his hand. Diggory took it, and began to pull himself up—

—and Potter let go.

Diggory sat down hard on the grass.

It was a dreadful insult to the losing side, or it would have been, if it had not been so completely childish. People stood about with their mouths open. Goyle, who had been hanging over the barrier, told her afterwards what Potter had said, bending down to him.

“That’s from Lyra. What were you doing with your hand on her arm?”

“Ha.” Diggory rubbed his elbow. “You’re a piece of work, aren’t you. I thought you didn’t care about anything. You’re more faithful than you look.”

He got up on his own. This time it was Diggory who held out his hand, and Potter who shook it.

Lyra was walking across the grass towards him by then.

Not to congratulate him, naturally. Gryffindor had won the Cup by ten points; this was a moment for Slytherin to be bitter in. She was only going because he looked a disgrace, and he might as well have a handkerchief, and she might as well tell him while she was at it that next year she would not be handing him the Cup on a plate.

It was in her pocket. She had washed it herself in the end, with a charm, and pressed it, and kept it in the drawer where the ring used to be. She had known since a February night whose it must have been. There had only ever been one person in the castle who could stand on a staircase without being seen.

She was turning it over in her fingers as she came up behind them, and so it happened that she heard.

“I think you’ve got the wrong idea, Cedric,” Potter was saying. “It isn’t like you and Cho. I’m not going out with Lyra.”

“What?”

“Just that. We’re not going out.”

“But the way you—”

Lyra stopped dead.

As a matter of plain fact, it was true. Nothing had ever been said between them that you could have taken to a solicitor. If anybody asked whether they were going out, the honest answer was nearer *no* than *yes*.

And yet she felt robbed, and oddly winded, as though somebody had shut a door in her face. Nobody had promised her anything. Nobody had put anything into words.

But there had been the forest. There had been the brooms, and *only in front of me*. There had been a silver fox on the shingle, made out of the two of them. If that was not plain, what was?

*Very well. Let him think what he likes.*

She felt a perfect fool for having fizzed.

She turned on her heel. Behind her, she thought she heard Potter’s voice, calling something. She did not look back.

The noise of the Gryffindors pouring down onto the pitch swallowed her footsteps.

She was halfway up to the castle, going fast, when Potter came out from behind a pillar of the cloister, quite out of breath.

“Shouldn’t the hero of the hour be down there being carried about?”

“Lyra—”

“I heard,” said Lyra. She did not stop walking, so he had to walk with her. “We’re not going out. Quite right. I should never have let it look as though we were, and I shan’t again. You may tell Diggory so from me.”

“That isn’t—”

“Isn’t it? It sounded remarkably like it.”

“Sorry.”

“What for? You were perfectly accurate.”

She did not look at him. He was breathing hard.

“I’m doing something a bit dangerous. You know roughly what. It’s to do with stopping him coming back.”

The *everything* Dumbledore had talked about. The thing so large that Dementors and an escape from Azkaban were only an incident in it. Harry Potter was in the middle of something that might change the world, and Lyra knew it, and had respected it enough to walk away from Dumbledore without asking. She had an everything of her own, the size of a rat’s footprint.

“And?”

“And if people know how I feel, it’s dangerous. Not for me. For all of us. It’s better, for now, if nobody can see it. Officially.” He hesitated. “Especially next year. If students come from other schools.”

Other schools? Lyra put that away to be wondered about later; she had better things to do with it just now. Because it was the same thing again. *It’s dangerous, so I’ll keep you at arm’s length.* The oldest excuse there was for pushing somebody away, with a ribbon round it so it looked like kindness.

She was sick of it. Lyra Malfoy was not a girl who got turned down, and she would sooner die than be put aside the same way twice; she had shut the door herself this time, before he could, and she was not going to stand here while he explained the hinges. She drew breath to tell Harry Potter precisely what she thought of a boy who imagined he could move her about like a piece of furniture—

And a small ring swallowed it all.

“But every night at nine o’clock,” said Potter, and held up his hand, with the other ring on it, “I’m going to put this on and smile. After lights out. I’ll lie there looking at it like an idiot, and I won’t be able to stop, even if I try.”

In the silence he took her hand. The ring opened its mouth, very carefully, and closed over the fourth finger of her left hand, and shrank to fit.

It held on. It was a nice sort of tightness.

“I hope you’ll do the same,” said Harry.

It was a very feeble sort of declaration. A ring on a girl’s finger: it was the oldest, stuffiest, most hackneyed thing in the world, and there was nothing original about it whatsoever. Lyra Malfoy had no interest in romance, and had said so, at length, to anybody who would listen, for three years. If anybody ever tried anything of the kind on her, she would turn him down flat without a second thought.

That went for Harry Potter too. She was, she noticed, still letting him hold the hand.

“Hmph. Do you imagine I’ll go to pieces because you’ve given me a ring?” She took the hand back. “A boy who comes with as much luggage as you do is the last thing I want.”

If there was any turning down to be done, Lyra Malfoy would do it. She tossed her pale hair back over her shoulder with the hand that wore the ring, where he could not possibly miss it, and kept a very firm hold on the corners of her mouth, which wanted to do something silly.

“I shan’t wait long. I’m not a person with time on her hands. Sort it out quickly.”

And she swept past him, with the ring still on her finger.
