# Interlude: The Wrong Night

There were two rings in Dudley’s second bedroom, and neither of them was any use.

One was on Harry’s right hand, where it had been since a cloister in May a year ago. It was plain silver and it had been plain silver for a month, and it would go on being plain silver, because the ring it answered to was in a biscuit tin at the bottom of his trunk, wrapped in tissue paper, and there was nobody wearing it to be looked for.

He knew exactly what colour that one was without opening the tin. Green. Safe. It had been green when she took it off.

He had read the note in the hospital wing with his leg in a bandage and Ron asking what Goyle wanted. Her best hand, the one she kept for Snape’s essays and for being right, and in the middle of the second line a gap the width of two fingers. She did not leave gaps. He had touched it with his wand because it was the only thing on the page that made no sense, and the parchment had come apart in his fingers like a shell, and inside, very small, was the thing he had spent a year looking for in the wrong place.

*His son is alive.*

He had folded it shut again. Hermione had been sitting on the next bed with a book open on her knee that she was not reading, watching his wand, and his face, and the parchment.

“What is it?”

“Nothing,” he had said. “It’s from Malfoy. She’s finished it. That’s all.”

Hermione had not said anything. She had gone on looking at the wand.

Nobody, in either of his lives, had ever sent him anything hidden. He lay on Dudley’s spare bed in the July heat, with the window open on the smell of cut grass and somebody’s barbecue, and thought about that, and then made himself stop.

Uncle Vernon’s *Daily Prophet* came to the house because Harry paid for it, and Uncle Vernon put it in the bin unread every morning to make a point. Harry fished it out every evening. It was mostly Karkaroff, who had killed Barty Crouch for reasons of his own and was being hunted across Europe, and Dumbledore, who had frightened a hall full of children with an old man’s nonsense and ought to think about retiring; and on Sunday, at the bottom of page nine, under a piece about a Kneazle show, there were two lines.

*Muggle authorities in the village of Little Hangleton are investigating damage to a grave in the parish churchyard. Nothing is believed to have been taken.*

Harry read it, and put the paper down, and looked at the ceiling.

Somebody had taken a bone out of that grave, the night of the Third Task, while he stood on a podium with his wand up and a cup in his hand, waiting for a churchyard that never came. He had been right. He had been right about the bone and the blood and nearly everything, and none of it was any use to him, because he did not know where it had been done. Not here. Not the house on the hill, not the yew, not the gravestone with the name on it. Somewhere else, that he had never seen in either life.

He did the count instead. He did it most nights, the way other people counted sheep.

The diary, two summers ago, with a fang out of the Chamber. The locket, in a cave, last August. The diadem, the same week, in a room on the seventh floor full of other people’s junk, with the same fang; he had gone up alone at three in the morning, and asked the wall for the place where things were hidden, and found it where he had known it would be, on the head of an ugly stone bust; and he had put the pieces back afterwards, where nobody would look, and told Dumbledore at breakfast, and Dumbledore had said nothing at all for so long that Harry had eaten two pieces of toast. Three, then.

The ring, in a shack in Little Hangleton, under a floorboard. He knew where it was. He knew what it did to the hand that put it on, because he had watched it blacken one, in the other life, and Dumbledore knew that he knew, because Harry had told him. *Not that one*, Dumbledore had said. *Not yet. When the time comes.* He had said it the summer before last, and again at Christmas, and again in May, and every time a little less as if he believed it.

The cup, in a vault at Gringotts, in a family Harry could not get into.

The snake.

And the one he did not count.

Three left, then, and one more. He had them all by heart. He had the shape of the year coming by heart as well, and the date the Dementors would come down Magnolia Crescent, and the hour. The second of August. A little after half past eight. He had been waiting for it since the train.

He would be in the right place, with his wand. He would get Dudley home. He knew what the letter from the Ministry would say before it was written.

It was the one thing, this summer, that he still knew.

On the thirty-first of July he was fifteen, and nobody at Number Four mentioned it, which was traditional.

Two owls came in the morning, to the window of the second bedroom, while Aunt Petunia was hoovering the landing: Errol, with a letter from Ron that was mostly about the Chudley Cannons and a squashed slab of Mrs Weasley’s fudge, and a smart little brown owl from Hermione with a book about Defensive magical theory and a card that said *Don’t do anything stupid, I mean it*. Nothing came from Wiltshire. He had not expected anything to come from Wiltshire. He looked at the window for some time after the brown owl had gone, all the same.

In the evening he went out, because the house was full of the television and Uncle Vernon was in a temper about the price of petrol, and walked to the park at the end of Magnolia Crescent, and sat on the one swing the gang had not broken, and thought about the second of August.

It was very warm. The streetlamps had come on, orange, along the edge of the park. Somewhere a dog was barking. Two days, and then the alleyway, and the cold, and Dudley on his knees on the tarmac; he had been through it so often that it felt less like a thing that would happen than a thing he had to remember to do, like a dentist’s appointment.

The dog stopped barking.

Harry noticed it the way you notice a clock that has stopped: not the silence, but the place where the noise had been. Then the streetlamp nearest the swings went out. Then the next. The warm air went out of the evening all at once, as if somebody had opened a door on to January, and his breath came out of him white.

*No*, he thought, quite clearly. *Not tonight. It’s Monday.*

He was off the swing with his wand out before he had finished thinking it. Across the grass, at the mouth of the alley that ran down to Wisteria Walk, somebody was shouting, and then not shouting, and then making a high, thin noise that Harry had never heard come out of his cousin in either life.

He ran.

Dudley was on his knees at the end of the alley with his arms over his head. Over him, tall and hooded and patient, two shapes that blotted out the lamplight, bending; and as Harry came down the alley the cold came up to meet him like water, and with it the voices, the old ones, a woman screaming and a high cold laugh, and under them, newer, a girl’s voice saying *that was the last one*, quite steadily, from somewhere very close.

He did not go looking for a happy memory. He did not need to look. He had one sitting at the front of his head and had been trying for a month not to take it out.

A tent. A canvas roof, and a dragon somewhere on the other side of it, roaring, and a hand he could not see laid over his on the edge of a bench.

“*Expecto Patronum!*”

The fox came out of his wand at a run, silver and low and furious, and went straight through the nearer Dementor as if it were smoke, and turned on the second, and the second went up and back and away over the rooftops like a sheet blown off a line. The cold went with them. The lamps came on along the alley, one by one, as though nothing had happened.

The fox came trotting back. It sat down in front of him on the tarmac, very upright, with its brush round its feet and its chin up, and looked at him in the way it always looked at him afterwards: as if it had done something rather fine and was waiting for him to say so.

“Yeah,” said Harry. His voice came out wrong. “I know. Thanks.”

It went on looking at him. It had her chin.

“Lyra,” he said, to nobody, and the fox went out like a candle.

Dudley was being sick against the wall.

Harry got him up. It took a while; Dudley was a great deal bigger than he had been in the other life at fifteen, and wetter, and he kept trying to sit down again. Harry got an arm under him and walked him back along Magnolia Crescent under the orange lamps, with Dudley’s weight on his shoulder and Dudley’s breath going in great shuddering gulps beside his ear, and all the way he thought about the date.

The thirty-first. Not the second. Two days early, on a Monday, in a park instead of an alleyway, with no warning at all.

He had been ready for the Dementors. He had been ready for them for three weeks. What he had not been ready for was to be wrong about a Monday.

The Ministry’s letter came at half past nine, by a screech owl that nearly took Aunt Petunia’s eye out, and said everything he had known it would say. Dumbledore’s came twenty minutes later, and said *stay in the house*, and *you will be collected*, and nothing whatever about how the Dementors had known to come on the wrong night. Harry read both at the kitchen table with Uncle Vernon shouting at him from the doorway and Dudley grey and silent on the sofa with a bar of chocolate Harry had given him, eating it very slowly, as if he had forgotten what it was for.
