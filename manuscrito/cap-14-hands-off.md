# Chapter 14: Hands Off

Lyra’s wand was halfway out of her sleeve.

It was not going to be enough, and she knew it. Sirius Black had killed twelve Muggles and a wizard with a single curse in the middle of a crowded street, and Lyra was thirteen and top of her year in Charms, which was not the same thing at all. Every sensible part of her said to run. She would have, very probably, if her pride had not been planted so firmly in the brambles, and if Potter had not been standing a yard from a murderer with his hands in his pockets, smiling as if he had introduced her to somebody’s aunt.

She reached for him. She did not know what she meant to do: pull him back, or get between them, or simply hold on to something. “Potter, get *away* from—”

“Steady, Malfoy.”

He caught her hand before it reached his sleeve, quite gently, and held it between both of his.

“Trust me,” he said. “He isn’t an enemy. And he isn’t a murderer.”

There was no reason on earth to believe him. The *Prophet* had been printing Sirius Black’s face since July; her father had talked about him at dinner, and Professor Snape in his office, and neither of them had had a good word to say. To take Potter’s word against all of that was absurd, and Lyra knew it was absurd, quite clearly, with the whole of her head.

The rest of her did not seem to have been told. Potter was only holding her hand. His were warm and dry, with ink on one thumb, and while he held hers she found she could not make it shake.

She took her hand back, and glared at Black instead.

“Right,” said Potter. “Inside, I think. It’s starting to rain.”

It was. A thin, freezing rain had come in off the hills while nobody was looking, and the frost on the brambles was turning to slush. Potter went to the back door of the Shack, which was boarded over like everything else, said something to the boards, and they swung outward like a gate. Lyra went in after him without once taking her eyes off Black, and Black came in last without once taking his off her.

Inside, it was worse than the outside had promised. Every stick of furniture in the place had been broken, and not by ghosts: the legs of the chairs were scored with long parallel gouges, as if something with claws had taken against them. The wallpaper hung in strips. In one corner the roof let the rain through in a thin, steady trickle onto the boards. Potter set an upturned chair on its feet for Black, and dragged a crate across for Lyra, and put them at opposite ends of the room. He need not have bothered. They would have ended up there anyway, like two magnets the wrong way round.

Black spoke first. His voice was hoarse and rusty, as though it had not been used for a long time.

“Harry. The Malfoys have been Voldemort’s most devoted followers for as long as there has been a Voldemort to follow. Are you quite sure it’s wise to let her see me?”

Lyra’s shoulders went up at the name. Malfoys did not say it, and were not, as a rule, obliged to hear it said across a room.

“Of course it isn’t *wise*!” Her hand had found her wand again inside her robes. “If I had known that dog was Sirius Black, I wouldn’t have thrown it a chicken bone!”

“And if I had known you were Lucius Malfoy’s daughter,” said Black, “I wouldn’t have eaten it.” His eyes narrowed. “Narcissa’s girl. I should have seen it. You’ve got her nose.”

He was her mother’s cousin. Lyra had always known it, the way one knew the names on a family tree, and had never once thought of it as anything but ink. It sat differently now that the fact was looking at her. He was Regulus’s brother, too. She knew two things about Regulus Black that this man, very probably, did not.

“I should have known when you ate the chocolate,” she said coldly. “I looked it up afterwards. Chocolate is poisonous to dogs. Dark chocolate especially.”

“Is it?” said Black. “Then I suppose you were trying to poison me.”

They glared at each other across the room. On the edge of the window seat, Potter’s eyebrows went up.

“Hang on. Chocolate? When was this?”

This was precisely the conversation she had gone to some trouble not to have.

“After the match,” said Lyra. “After you fell off your broom. I was walking by the lake, and the creature simply appeared—”

“You don’t eat dark chocolate. You pulled a face at Lupin’s on the train like it was medicine.” Potter was looking at her with growing interest. “So you were walking about with a bar of something you wouldn’t eat, the day I was in the hospital wing, and then you gave it to a dog.” He snapped his fingers. “Aha. It was for me.”

“Don’t flatter yourself!”

“It wasn’t?”

“It was— not *exactly*.” Lyra could feel her ears going hot, and there was nothing whatever to be done about ears. “I went to look at you being pathetic, that was all. And then I couldn’t be bothered, and there happened to be a dog.”

“Shame,” said Potter, with deep regret. “Still. If it was Sirius who ate it, I’ll let it go.”

Black was staring at him. His mouth had come slightly open, and he was looking at Potter as if Potter had just done something Black had seen somebody do a very long time ago, and had not expected to see again.

“Right,” said Potter, turning to her as though nothing had happened. “Malfoy. You said you had something to tell me about Sirius. Still want to?”

Lyra looked at Black, and at the leak in the corner, and back at Potter.

“No.”

“How much do you know?”

“That he was your parents’…” She found she did not want to say it in front of the man himself, which was ridiculous. “Secret Keeper.”

“Hm.” Potter tilted his head. “Who told you that much? One of the staff, it’d have to be. Snape?”

Lyra looked back at him with polite interest. Underneath the polite interest something went cold, because Professor Snape had told her that in a room with a charm on the door, and Professor Snape could write to her father whenever he pleased. But Potter did not press. With an Animagus sitting on a broken chair across the room, how she had come by the rest of it was very small change.

“If you know that much, you’ll know about the Fidelius Charm.”

She nodded.

“That makes it easier,” said Potter. “So, yes. My dad chose Sirius.”

“And Sirius betrayed him.”

“Don’t you *dare*—” Black was half out of his chair, and his voice cracked down the middle like a dropped plate. “I am *not* a traitor!”

“Sirius.” Potter did not raise his voice, and did not seem to need to. “Let me tell her. Sit for a minute.” He waited until Black sat, slowly, and then turned back to Lyra. “He isn’t a traitor, Malfoy. He couldn’t have been. Sirius turned it down.”

“What?”

“Everybody knew he was my dad’s best friend. If there was a Secret Keeper, it’d be Sirius; any idiot could work that out, and Voldemort wasn’t an idiot. So Sirius said: let me be the one everybody hunts. Give the real secret to somebody nobody would think of.”

It was a good plan. Lyra had been raised on her father’s notion of a good plan, and she could find nothing wrong with this one: put the obvious man in the window, and the valuable thing somewhere dull.

“Dumbledore and Sirius chose the real one between them,” Potter went on. “And straight afterwards, the real one got frightened. Voldemort would go after Sirius, he said. What if Sirius was caught? What if he was made to talk?”

“Because the charm hides the house,” said Lyra slowly. “Not the person keeping it.”

Potter looked at her. “Yes. Exactly that. So Dumbledore and Sirius put a binding on themselves, so that neither of them could ever give the Keeper away. They went to all that trouble.” His voice did not change. “And the Keeper was the one who sold them.”

Lyra was no longer quite listening. She was back in a dungeon office with a charm on the door, and Professor Snape’s voice was going on, dry as paper: a week, Black had kept their secret one week. And afterwards a street, and a wizard who had gone after Black shouting and been blown apart for it, and who was buried alongside twelve Muggles in every version of the story she had ever heard. The only other friend. The one nobody would think of.

“Pettigrew,” she said.

Potter nodded.

“But he’s dead.”

“He isn’t. He knew Sirius and Dumbledore could never say a word about him, so he blew up the street and made it look as though Sirius had done it. Nobody could prove otherwise. The only two people who knew couldn’t open their mouths.”

“He opened his mouth just now,” said Lyra, with a nod at Black. “Quite loudly.”

“He can say he isn’t the traitor. He’s been saying it for twelve years, and nobody listens. What he can’t do is point. Not a name, not a hint, nothing that would lead you to the one who was.”

Lyra turned on her crate to face Black.

“Who was your Secret Keeper, Mr Black?”

Black opened his mouth. Nothing came out of it. His face emptied as she watched, every line in it going smooth and blank, until he sat there like a man painted on a wall, and only his eyes were left, red-rimmed and fixed on hers, with everything behind them that he was not allowed to say.

Lyra looked away first.

“Then why didn’t Dumbledore *say* something? Before they took him to Azkaban? He’s Dumbledore. He could have stood up in front of the whole Wizengamot and—”

“Same binding. He couldn’t.”

“But he told *you*!”

“Well. He’s Dumbledore.” Potter shrugged. “He knows ways round most things. And I was the secret. I’m the only one left who was ever inside that charm, so I was the only person he could tell at all, and even then he had to go a very long way round to do it.”

A very long way round, to tell a boy. And in twelve years he had not found a way round, long or short, to get an innocent man out of Azkaban. Lyra did not say so. She put it away somewhere safe, with the other things she knew about Albus Dumbledore, for later.

There was something else to put with it. *Dumbledore told me.* There it was at last, the answer she had not asked for in the library, handed over for nothing. It accounted for the dog, and the charm, and Pettigrew. It did not account for a house in Northumberland that was on no map and in no register, that her father had never named to a living soul, and that Potter had brought up in May without so much as blinking. Dumbledore might know a great many things. She did not see how he could know that.

She kept that too.

“Fine,” said Lyra. “I admit it. I lose.”

“I wasn’t trying to win.”

He meant it kindly. She felt the heat start at the back of her neck and climb, and there was nothing to be done about it. And now everything Potter had done that term began to drop into place, one piece after another. Hogsmeade without a form. *Just missed him*, on Hallowe’en. A big black dog that would be hungry.

“That’s why you went to Hogsmeade,” she said. “To find him.”

“Mm. Easier than in the castle. Fewer eyes.”

“And the dog. On the train.”

“Dumbledore told me that as well. That Sirius could turn into one.”

“Then you must have laughed yourself sick.” It came out smaller than she had meant it to. “Me. Telling you not to go to Hogsmeade.”

Potter shook his head.

“I didn’t laugh. I thought it was nice. That you worried.”

“You’re being sarcastic.”

“I’ve never lied to you, Malfoy.”

She could think of one lie at least, to do with the preface of *The Monster Book of Monsters*, and found that she did not want to bring it up.

“So,” she said. “What happens now? What’s the plan?”

Potter smiled at her. It was a kind smile, patient and fond, the smile you might give a child who wanted to stay up past her bedtime.

“About that,” he said. “Hands off, Malfoy.”

“…What?”

“I told you about Sirius because if I hadn’t, you’d have gone on digging, and you dig very well. That’s all. You don’t need to do anything else. You’re done.”

He was pushing her out.

She had not seen it coming. She had got this close to it, closer than anybody, and he was shutting the door with her foot still in it. Lyra heard herself stammer.

“You— you’ve told me all this, and now you want me to keep out of it?”

“Malfoy, I know it sounds like an enormous secret. It isn’t, really. Not next to the diary. An escaped prisoner who’s an unregistered Animagus, that’s all it comes to.”

*The diary.* Hearing it in Potter’s mouth, Lyra felt something drop out of the bottom of her.

“Now that we’ve found Sirius, Dumbledore will hide him somewhere. When Dumbledore puts his mind to hiding something, nobody finds it. Sirius is safe.”

“And— and me?”

“You stop here. Whatever comes next, you don’t need to be in it. It’s better if you aren’t. For both of us.”

“Why? I’m already in it. I’m in it up to my neck. I—”

*Tell me*, she wanted to say. *Don’t leave me out here not knowing.* She had never in her life asked anybody for anything in that voice, and she heard herself doing it, and did not stop. Her pride was the only thing she had ever really owned, and she had just put it down on the floor between them.

“I didn’t want to say this,” said Potter.

When it came, it was harder than she would have believed he could be.

“It isn’t only you. Your family could get hurt. Think about your family, Malfoy. Whatever anybody else is, Draco hasn’t done anything. Would you be all right if something happened to Draco?”

On the broken chair, Black had turned his head and was staring at his godson. Whatever he thought of it, he kept it to himself; but it was not the way he had looked at Potter before.

Draco. She had never once said her brother’s name to him. Draco, who wrote her letters that ran down both sides of the page about nothing at all, and was learning to play cards from an old man in the east cottage, and had not answered her in seven weeks.

It was a real threat. That was what she could not get past. It was not a bluff and it was not a game: it was the kind of threat you could not refuse, because it had her brother in it. Having a weapon and using it were two different things, and every Malfoy knew it. Potter had held the diary since May and had never once swung it. He was swinging it now. He would not touch Draco himself; she did not think that of him. He would not need to. He had only to let the diary go where it could do the most harm, to the Ministry or the *Prophet*, and the house would come down on her parents with Draco standing underneath it.

“Do you mean it?” She asked anyway. She wanted him to take it back.

“Yes,” said Potter. His voice was perfectly steady, and quite cold. “There’s no room for you in this.”

Lyra Malfoy did not cry. To cry in front of somebody was to show them where to hit you and ask them to feel sorry for you in the same breath, and she had been brought up better than that. She had cried in front of two Houses in September, but that had been a hippogriff and a great deal of blood, and did not count. So the wet on her face was rain. The roof leaked. It had been leaking all afternoon, in the corner, and it must have found her somehow.

“From now on,” she said.

She wanted to hurt him. She wanted to leave a mark on him that he would carry for the rest of his life, and he already had one of those, and she could not think of a single thing to put beside it. She went looking for the cruellest words she knew and came back with nothing. So in the end she said the only true thing she had.

“Don’t smile in front of me.”

It worked. She saw it work: the smile went out of him, the kind, patient one, and for a second what was left underneath looked about eleven years old. It was no comfort at all.

She pulled the button warmer off her collar and threw it. It hit the boards, bounced, and rolled away across the room in a long, wobbling curve, and dropped through a gap between two floorboards into the dark underneath. She did not wait to hear it land.

She turned round and walked out of the Shrieking Shack.

Outside, it really was raining.
