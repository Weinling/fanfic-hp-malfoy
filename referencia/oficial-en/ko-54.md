It felt like she'd been dreaming. Carina blinked slowly.

She couldn't remember what it had been about, but the ache of longing it left behind told her it must have been something pleasant.

So I can still be happy in dreams. Carina smiled, faintly.

She rose from bed at a leisurely pace. She stared blankly at the sunlight slanting through the gap in the curtains, then climbed out on the strength of old habit alone.

As always, she stood before the mirror and drew a comb through her hair. A ritual worn smooth by years of repetition. The enchanted comb caught her slightly dishevelled strands and smoothed them into a sleek fall.

But there was no Carina who lingered to admire her reflection.

No turning this way and that to assess the day's outfit.

Once, she had fastened every accessory with a quiet thought for how it might look through a certain pair of eyes. Now the only adornment she possessed was a faint mark on her ring finger—a ghost left behind by something removed.

Her heart, too, had been emptied of everything but the mark. Taken away with a single flower and the ring tied to it.

"Well done, Carina."

She lifted her chin. Every trace of confusion and dread pressed down deep beneath the surface, Carina Malfoy stepped out of her room with perfect composure.

"You're late, Carina!"

She had barely reached the dining room when Draco banged the table with the hand still clutching his fork.

"Today's the day we go to Diagon Alley! You should have been up earlier than usual!"

His eyes were bright with excitement and barely contained glee as he said it.

As though Voldemort's return and the shadow gathering over the Malfoy name had nothing whatsoever to do with him—the guileless impatience of it made Carina smile before she could stop herself.

"Draco," she said, "aren't you the one who isn't ready? Were you actually planning to walk into Diagon Alley wearing that bow tie?"

"W-what's wrong with a bow tie?"

Draco was dressed in a child's formal suit, complete with a bow tie knotted at his throat. He was trying to look as though he hadn't made any special effort, but the effect was entirely undone by how obviously he'd dressed up at the prospect of the outing.

Carina's tone was lightly teasing.

"Mother will find it absolutely adorable. You'll want to change the moment breakfast is over—unless you'd like to be teased about it for years to come."

"There's nothing wrong with a bow tie! You wore a dress the first time you went to Diagon Alley!"

Carina paused. She had, in fact—she'd been just as giddy as Draco was now, and had put on the prettiest dress she owned for the occasion. She'd gone spinning through Diagon Alley in it, and that was the day she'd met Harry Potter, and from then—

She stopped herself there. She built a dam against the current of her own thoughts and shut off the flood of feeling with practised efficiency.

She steadied her expression and spoke.

"As your elder sister, I cannot in good conscience allow you to repeat my mistakes."

"I don't care! I'm going like this!"

"You'll regret it. Especially once Mother sees you."

Narcissa entered the dining room at that moment, Lucius at her side.

Both of them wore the pallor of people carrying a great weight—Narcissa had always been pale, so that was nothing new, but Lucius looked like a man who had been given a date for his own death. When her eyes fell on Draco and Carina, however, colour returned to Narcissa's face.

"Oh, Draco, darling. Don't you look smart. Did you choose that especially for Diagon Alley today?"

"I did!"

Draco puffed up and thrust out his bow tie. Narcissa crossed to him naturally and began retying it.

"Carina wore the most beautiful little dress the first time she went, you know. She went spinning round and round Diagon Alley with the skirt flying out around her! She was so excited that if you took your eyes off her for a moment she'd simply vanish!"

Draco looked over at his sister with an expression that plainly asked, Really? Carina turned her head and said nothing.

Narcissa, meanwhile, continued to lavish praise on her son without restraint.

"It suits you wonderfully. You must take after your sister in that. And quite right—once you're at Hogwarts there won't be many occasions to dress like this! We ought to take a family photograph while we have the chance!"

Draco was too pleased with himself to realise he was writing the opening chapter of his own embarrassing history, one that would be recounted at every opportunity for the next five years at least.

Carina watched her brother with a look of quiet sympathy.

It was then that Lucius called to her.

"Carina. Come here a moment."

Carina had been looking for an excuse to leave the dining room anyway, and followed him out without hesitation.

Lucius closed the door behind them and dropped his voice to barely above a whisper, as though afraid of being overheard.

"Carina. When you return to school this term, I need you to see to the things I asked of you."

Since Voldemort's return, Lucius had been searching desperately for the diary. But the only person who could move freely within Hogwarts was Carina, and there was no one he could explain the full situation to—so he had no choice but to rely on her entirely.

It was why Carina felt the weight of a stone settle in her chest every time she faced her father.

Because Dumbledore and Harry Potter had already destroyed the diary. They had examined the remains and drawn out Voldemort's secrets from them.

Two years on, and he was still searching for it. What would happen to her father if he ever learned the truth?

"I looked for it last year as well, but I couldn't find it anywhere."

"Dumbledore would never simply discard it—that's not his way. However destroyed it may be, it contained some of the most powerful Dark Magic ever cast—enough to open the Chamber of Secrets. It must be somewhere in Hogwarts. It has to be."

It was an accurate guess. As a Horcrux, the diary would be something Dumbledore guarded with the utmost care.

Carina chose her words carefully.

"Father. If Dumbledore had the diary in his possession and took it apart piece by piece to search for a weakness—"

"Don't say that, Carina!"

Lucius's voice cracked, his face going white.

"That's impossible. Dumbledore has no real understanding of Dark Magic. He must have simply stored it somewhere and left it alone... We have to get it back. If we can just get it back, we can still manage this."

He muttered it like a vow made to himself.

The worst possibility was too terrible to contemplate. He refused to let himself imagine it. If they could only recover the diary, everything would still be salvageable—he had to believe that.

Carina felt a quiet grief settle over her.
Dumbledore had already identified it as a Horcrux. He had used it as a thread to find and destroy the others. The truth was simply too heavy for her father to bear.

Given that, silence was the only thing that could still protect the Malfoy name. Carina nodded without a word.

"All right. Is that everything?"

"No. There's one more thing."

Lucius shifted his tone, drawing himself up with something closer to his usual authority.

"The fools at the Ministry don't yet know that the Dark Lord has returned. That works in our favour—there are things we can still accomplish. I will soon be undertaking a task he has set for me. If I succeed, I can restore our standing..."

And cover the loss of the diary.

Carina could finish the sentence without being told. Lucius hesitated for a moment, then continued.

"You, too, have an opportunity to distinguish yourself. It seems the Dark Lord has taken a favourable view of you. Karkaroff mentioned that your role in bringing him to Bartemius—the fact that you made it possible—caught the Dark Lord's attention. He noted your competence."

"Me?"

"Yes. I am extraordinarily proud of you, Carina. You are the first child of a Death Eater he has ever singled out by name."

Why? What reason could Voldemort have for taking notice of her?

The question unsettled her, but Carina read the emotion in her father's eyes and accepted it with a show of pleased surprise.

Lucius looked at her with something close to desperate reliance.

"My own daughter... I don't know what I would do if you weren't as capable as you are..."

Her shoulders felt heavy. Something solid and immovable pressed down on her chest.

An oblivious younger brother. Parents cornered and afraid. A family teetering on the edge. A tyrant to be served.

The burdens were stacking themselves on a girl who had not yet come of age, one by one, until she felt she might be crushed beneath the weight of obligation.

"The Ministry still doesn't know of his return. But among the Slytherin students, there will be some who've heard and are already excited. They may say careless things. Carina—I need you to manage them. If you handle it well, you could earn your place as a Death Eater, and our family's position would be all the more secure."

But the burden could never be set down.

It was the shackle she had been born into as a Malfoy—the duty she would carry until she died.

As a Malfoy, as a pureblood, Carina was exactly what she had told Harry Potter she was: even if Voldemort threatened her, she could not stand against him.

She kept her voice light.

"The Dark Mark is rather an ugly design. I'd prefer not to have it, if there's any alternative."

"Carina. Mind your tongue."

Anxiety is contagious—but so, it turns out, is composure.

Her easy manner seemed to have restored a little of Lucius's equilibrium. A faint smile crossed his face.

"Even so—take it when the opportunity comes. It is a mark of loyalty to the Dark Lord. A proof of it."

"In that case, I suppose I can endure it."

"You'll accept it gladly."

Lucius patted her shoulder and smiled. The gesture was light, almost tender—but to Carina it felt heavier than anything else in the world.

They returned to the dining room together. Narcissa was there, pale and smiling, and Draco was still radiant with unclouded happiness.

The smiles that Lucius had been trying to protect. The smiles that Carina had to protect now.

Harry lay on his back in the attic, staring up at his own hand.

The large ring was still on his finger. If Carina had still been wearing hers, it would have glowed blue—the colour of longing. But now it was less than an ordinary ring. Just a useless trinket.

And yet Harry hadn't taken it off. He knew perfectly well it wouldn't light up, and still he found himself staring at it for thirty minutes every day.

Waiting for some miracle to bring the light back.

No miracle came today, either. Harry shoved himself off the floor, kicked open the attic door, and wandered through the house with his hands jammed deep in his pockets.

He walked into the kitchen without ceremony. Two pieces of chicken breast toast sat on a plate—Dudley's. Harry picked them up, chewed as he went, and headed for the front door.

"You! Where do you think you're sneaking off to now!"

Vernon had spotted him. Harry couldn't answer; his mouth was full.

"You've stolen Dudley's toast again, haven't you! You ungrateful little rat—we take you in and you repay us by stealing food off our plates!"

Harry swallowed and replied without particular feeling.

"Dudley's the one who steals food. Just last week he shook down a boy called Jonny for his pocket money and spent it on crisps and sweets. Jonny's eleven years old, in case you weren't aware."

"Dudley would never do something like that! He's a bit slow, maybe, but he's a fair boy!"

"He certainly takes after his father in the slow department," Harry said. "Fair enough to get into a one-on-one scuffle with a child several years younger than him."

He let the sarcasm sit for a moment.

"If anything like it happens again, Jonny's parents won't let it go quietly. They're already considering filing a complaint with the school's anti-bullying board."

"What? How would you know that?"

"Because I'm the one who told them."

Vernon's eyes went wide. His expression was that of a man who very much wanted to grab Harry by the collar and squeeze.

The reason he didn't was not any particular sense of decency or restraint—it was because he had tried it once before and Harry had thrown him off.

Muggle violence was nothing compared to the dangers of the wizarding world. After everything Harry had survived, the Dursleys were a threat so negligible as to be almost beneath notice.

That fact, naturally, enraged Vernon enormously.

"You've been telling lies about Dudley! You used magic to tamper with their memories!"

"Now you're just making things up. I can't do that."

"Why not? Because you're not allowed to use magic outside school? You've broken that rule plenty of times! I expect you've wriggled out of it again somehow!"

"No. If I were going to use a Memory Charm on anyone, you'd be first on the list."

Harry let his hand drift toward the wand at his hip, just enough to be visible.
"So if you can still speak in complete sentences, Vernon, that means you're fine. Perfectly safe. Though I'll admit you don't look particularly fine at the moment."

Vernon snapped his mouth shut, visibly rattled. His moustache was still quivering with suppressed fury—once the immediate fear wore off, there would be a spectacular tantrum—but for now he was silent.

"Right. I'm heading out."

Harry left before the explosion could happen.

He walked aimlessly through the neighbourhood, turning things over in his mind.

The Dursleys were, in their own way, remarkable people.

Carina had been right: hold your head up, smile with confidence, and most people will think better of you. The only exception Harry had ever found was Snape—but even Snape had treated him marginally better once Harry stopped flinching. With almost everyone else, it worked.

The Dursleys were the exception to every rule. They had kept up the abuse even after learning he was a wizard. They feared magic and despised him for it anyway. Nothing reached them—not conversation, not the implicit threat of retaliation. They were a constant. If there was anything immutable in this world, it was Vernon and Petunia Dursley.

Harry was beginning to find himself genuinely curious. Why did they keep him at all? Obligation? Fear?

Probably neither. If they truly understood what a wizard could do, they wouldn't treat someone who was two years from adulthood the way they treated him.

"Forget it. Their problem."

Whatever their reasons, they weren't his concern. If anything, being thrown out would have suited him better.

The reason Harry came back to Privet Drive every year was his mother's protection charm.

As long as blood family sheltered him, no dark force could touch that household. Whether he was protecting them or they were protecting him was a matter of perspective—but the advice he'd been given was that every layer of protection counted, so he stayed.

Fourteen years, and he felt nothing for this place. He'd been warmer in the Burrow after a few days. If Number Four, Privet Drive burned to the ground—if an adder found its way inside and bit the Dursleys—he doubted he would grieve.

Not grieve? Is that really all?

Harry had received nothing from them.

No—worse than nothing. They had given him abuse and contempt.

Ten years of learning to make himself small. Ten years of keeping his head down and his shoulders in.

Always the leftover food. Dudley could eat himself sick at every meal without a word said, but if Harry reached for a second helping it was treated as a crime against nature.

If the housework wasn't done, he was lazy. If he wanted rest, he could rest permanently—in the cupboard under the stairs.

He had received things, technically. A birthday cake drawn in the sand, and the memory of blowing out its candle. While Dudley tore through Christmas wrapping paper, Harry had gathered up the boxes and carried them outside. He'd kept a couple of ribbons once. He'd been called a thief for it.

Something coiled inside him whispered.

Be honest, Harry Potter. It's more than that, isn't it?

Honestly. Completely honestly.

If they died, he thought he might feel glad.

Dudley was still a minor—that was something. But Vernon? Petunia?

If they died in regret, screaming in the most wretched agony imaginable, begging Harry to save them—that was a fantasy with a certain dark appeal.

And it wasn't even out of reach. He could Parseltongue a snake into the house right now. He could have them dead before morning.

The Muggle police would call it an accident. The Ministry might sense something off, but given their track record, they'd take ages to piece it together.

It would feel good.

Something dark and ancient offered him the fruit and told him to take a bite. Harry opened his mouth—

"...It's all just fantasy, anyway."

He let out a long breath and muttered it to the empty street.

There was a kind of thought like that, wasn't there—the kind that flared up in a moment of real anger, I wish they were dead, and then faded, and you were grateful it had. That was all this was.

Harry had already decided what he would actually do. The moment he came of age, he would cast a Memory Charm on the lot of them and walk out. He would make sure they forgot he had ever existed. That they could never think of him again.

Assuming he survived that long.

"If I die, Voldemort wins—which means their lives won't be particularly happy either. Well. That's something, at least."

He said it to himself, and then, without quite meaning to, thought of Carina.

The girl who had told him that magic alone made him extraordinary—who had pushed him to stand straight and stop shrinking from the world.

She had been his goal, once. He had nearly reached her. And then Voldemort had returned, and the distance between them had simply opened up again, as naturally and terribly as a wound.

Yes. Because that noseless, half-dead wretch had crawled back from wherever he'd been rotting.

"Ha. Voldemort, you bastard."

If not for that orphaned monster and his particular brand of ruin, Harry's parents would be alive. He would never have ended up at the Dursleys'. He and Carina would never have had to part.

Harry would take his bite of the fruit eventually. But his first kill was already spoken for.

"I'll put the Killing Curse right between those noseless eyes."

He would destroy the sixty-year-old murderer who called himself a lord, and then he would get Carina back. That was the plan. That was the promise.

The voice inside him—the one that had been urging him toward violence just moments ago—had gone quiet.

Then the cold came.

It swept through the street all at once, sudden and total. The stars dimmed and vanished. A creeping chill moved toward him like something alive.

The feeling of every happy thought being drained from the world. Harry recognised it. His hand went to his wand.

"Dementors? Why are there Dementors in Little Whinging? They're supposed to be at Azkaban."

He remembered what Dumbledore had told him. Voldemort had been drawing magical creatures to his side, building his forces—and Dementors were among those who might be turned.

If the Dementors had been turned, then the ones here in Little Whinging had almost certainly come for him.

Harry exhaled.

"Well... Mum, I'm not blaming you, but the protection charm has some real coverage gaps."

A scream came from the alley ahead.

Harry tightened his grip on his wand and moved toward it.
