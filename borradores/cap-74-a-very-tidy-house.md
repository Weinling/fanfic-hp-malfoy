# Chapter 80: A Very Tidy House

The Aurors had gone to the cellars at last.

Pobble told her so in the hall, taking her cloak, with her ears flat. They had come on the Thursday and again on the Friday, nine of them, in boots. They had taken up the flags in the wine vault, and had the back off the cupboard under the still-room stairs, where nobody had been since her great-grandmother. They had found four hundred bottles of the ’47 and a quantity of dead mice. In 1992 Arthur Weasley would have sold one of his children for that afternoon, and he’d had several to spare; and they had gone away on the Friday evening with mud on the Aubusson, looking tired. There had been nothing to find, and nobody at home to find it. Everyone had left for the north before breakfast on the Wednesday, Pobble said, and the master’s study had a purple ribbon across the door, and would Miss Lyra be wanting a bath.

She went up and looked. She didn’t take off her gloves. She walked the whole length of the south wing, with her heels going ahead of her down the boards, and all the shutters had been folded back and the windows stood open on the evening. There was a smell of cut grass. In the end room somebody had stripped the bed and squared the mattress and left the blankets folded at the foot, with the corners true. He had been brought up in an institution. She stood and looked at those corners for a little while, and there wasn’t a hair on the pillow-ticking, or a ring from a glass. You could not have said that anyone had ever been there.

Her mother was on the stairs. She was wearing the grey, and she stood with one hand on the rail, looking out of the tall window at nothing, and she looked as though someone had been at her with a very fine rubber. The cold light lay along her collarbone. There was nothing there. There had been a thin gold chain at the neck of that dress every day since Christmas, with something small and flat on the end of it, and now there was a faint pale line.

Neither of them looked at it.

“You’ve grown,” said her mother. “Draco’s in his room. We shall have to do something about your eyes.”

He was sitting on his bed with his hands on his knees. He had come home from King’s Cross beside her in the carriage, with his bag on his lap, and looked out of the window for the whole of Wiltshire. On the chest of drawers, in the exact centre of a clean handkerchief, he had placed a tin. It was a plain square biscuit tin with a dent in the lid, tied round with kitchen string, and a label in big slow round letters. *For the hols. G.* He had carried that on his knees from Hogwarts, under the bag. She had seen him take it out of the rack himself, and he had looked at the porter who reached for it, with her grandfather’s eyes.

“Aren’t you going to open it?” said Lyra.

He went on looking at the wall. “No,” said Draco.

On the Sunday they had nobody.

She woke late, and lay and looked at it. The house had a sound she had forgotten. A pigeon was going on in the limes. Somewhere below, a long way off, a door stood open and it was banging a little in the wind, and no one had come to see about it, and no one had flinched. She got up and went downstairs in her dressing gown, which she had not done since she was ten. The dining-room table had been pushed against the wall, for the Aurors, and it had a long raw scratch on it that nobody could account for; and at noon her mother came and stood in the doorway and looked at it, and at the ceiling.

“I don’t believe that I can sit in here,” said Narcissa Malfoy. “Where does one eat?”

Lyra had to take her. She had been in the kitchens of Hogwarts, on rounds, at an hour when she had no business to be, and she knew the way down here because she had come to steal things at seven. Her mother had lived in that house for seventeen years. She came down the back stairs with her skirt in one hand, slowly, looking about her, as one might go down into a foreign port. It was a long stone room with a high barred window, and the sun came in at a slant. Pobble dropped a colander, and was begged to get up, and wept into the tablecloth. It took some time, and in the end she was sent away to see to the silver, and there they were, the three of them, at the end of a scrubbed table, with a loaf.

“One cuts it, I suppose,” said her mother.

She took up the knife. She held it as she would have held something that belonged to somebody else. Lyra had never seen anything like it. She made a long slow unhappy sawing motion, with her wrist turned out and her lips pressed together, and there came away from the loaf a thing like a doorstop at one end, and at the other a sort of lace.

Draco looked at it. He looked for some time, with that clean, incurious face. A very small crease had appeared at the corner of his mouth.

“That’s not a slice,” said Draco.

“I am aware of that.”

“It’s a *wedge*.”

“Thank you, Draco. Perhaps you would like to tell Madam Malkin about her hemming.”

She put down the knife. There was butter on her thumb. She looked at it in astonishment, and then, since there was nobody there, she put it in her mouth. Lyra heard a small dry noise, and it was a moment before she knew what it was; she had only heard it three or four times in her life, through doors. Her mother was laughing. She made no sound to speak of. She had put the back of her wrist against her lips, and her thin shoulders had started to go, and she looked about nineteen. It was the most frightening thing that Lyra had seen all year, and she found she had started too.

Draco got down from the bench. He went out, and they heard him on the stairs, going up, not fast. They heard him coming down. He came back along the table with the tin in both hands, and set it in front of him, and undid the knot; he didn’t cut it, he picked it out with his nails, and wound the string round two fingers and put it in his pocket. He took off the lid.

They were small and round and rather uneven, and they had been packed in greaseproof paper, in rows. They smelt of butter. He counted them. His lips moved. He had taken to doing that. There were twelve in each layer, and he lifted the top paper by the corner to see the ones below.

He took out one and put it down in front of his mother. He took out one for himself. Then he sat with his hands on either side of the tin, and he looked across at Lyra. It was the first time he had looked at her since a boiled egg. She held quite still. He seemed to be going through it, from the top. He put out one finger, and pushed the tin an inch towards her across the boards, without turning his head.

She had seen that done. She knew who had taught him. She took one.

“It’s got orange in it,” said Draco, with his mouth full.

It had. It had gone a little soft, in the heat of the train, and it was by some way the best thing she had eaten in that house. She ate it slowly, with the sun on her arm and the crumbs on her dressing gown, and when she looked up her mother had cut another piece of bread, or done something to it, and had held it out to her on the end of the knife. Nobody said anything about a fountain. Nobody said anything about the Cobb. Lyra ate that as well, and she ate an egg, and some cold ham that Pobble came in and laid at her elbow, sobbing with joy; and Draco showed their mother, with some contempt, how you did a slice. They sat on until the sun had gone off the table. The door went on banging, up above, and she thought she would go and see about it, and she didn’t.

He came back at seven, on foot.

She saw him from the landing window. He walked up the middle of the drive, in the long gold light, with his hands behind him, looking about at the limes like a man who is thinking of taking a lease. There was something moving through the grass, a little way behind him. At the bottom of the steps he stopped and cleaned his boots on the scraper, the left and then the right, and he took some trouble over it. Then he gave his hat to Pobble, and thanked her.

They were all in the hall by then. She didn’t know how that had come about. Her mother had changed her dress. Aunt Bella came in at the great door behind him, with her head back, sniffing the house; and she saw Lyra on the stairs and gave a small scream of delight, and came up them two at a time with her hair flying.

“There she is! Oh, let me look, have you heard? You haven’t. Look at it. *Look*.”

She took Lyra’s chin in her dirty fingers and turned her own face to the light.

It ran from the left temple to the corner of the mouth. Lyra had seen a great many cuts; she had kept a jar. This one was as fine as a pen stroke, and quite straight, and it had not closed. It stood very slightly open along the whole of its length, with a dark wet shine on it, and as her aunt grinned it moved. Someone had tried to stitch it. Someone had tried a good deal else.

“Your young man,” said Aunt Bella. She was breathing on her, in little quick delighted breaths, and her eyes were like a child’s on its birthday. “In the Atrium! I’d just done for Sirius, I was skipping. And out he comes, all by himself, with no expression at all, and he says one word. Half of one. *Half a word*, my treasure, he didn’t even finish it. Imagine the rest.”

She let go. She patted Lyra’s cheek, twice, and ran down the stairs again.

Lyra stood with her hand on the banister.

*Is that all of it?* he had asked, in that room, with his one sock. *Is that everything?* He had looked her in the eye. She had looked at him, with a burnt letter under her tongue, and said yes, and he’d stood there with this in his mouth and told her he didn’t know. So they had each made sure of that. It had a sort of finish to it.

She found she’d put a hand up to her own face, and she took it down.

There were six at table. He had the chair at the head. They had put the table back, with a cloth on it, and Draco sat at his right hand, with his hair combed flat.

He ate nothing. He turned his glass by the stem and he let them get through the soup. Bellatrix was telling a story about a Healer in Knockturn Alley. Lyra had her back straight and her hands where they should be, and she had taken two spoonfuls, and she was thinking that one could do this; it was a question of breathing through the nose. She had given him the whole of the breakfast table, with the sun on it. He hadn’t even looked yet.

“I find I owe you an apology, Miss Malfoy,” said the Dark Lord.

Bellatrix stopped.

“I have been remiss. I went away on the Wednesday in such a hurry. And one should always thank people at the time; it’s so easily neglected, and I have had the advantage of knowing what it is to go without.” It was a light, pleasant, considering voice. He might have been going down a list of subscribers. “The nineteenth of May. *He dreams of his godfather’s death.* I had been trying for a year to think how one got in at that door. I’d looked at it from every side. And you sent me the key, on the second-best paper, in such a pretty hand. I did tell your father how much I admired it.”

“Thank you, my lord.”

“The note with the lemon.”

She had a spoon in her hand. She looked down, and saw it was perfectly steady, and she was rather interested. There was a small gold rim on the plate.

“The hem,” he said. “I did enjoy that. You gave me the gap, for my trouble, and six lines about his breakfast, and you must have thought I would be so pleased with those. It’s an elegant thing, a paper pattern. Your mother’s, I believe. But, my dear, I was brought up by a charitable institution, in south London, during the slump. We did it with milk. We did it with anything we could get. There isn’t a boy in that dormitory who wouldn’t have held it up to a candle before he had finished his tea.”

No one was moving. Across the table, her mother had laid down her spoon, without any sound, and put her hands in her lap.

“And Lyme,” said the Dark Lord. He smiled at her. It was a small smile, and it had real warmth in it. “That was what I wanted to say. When the moment came, you see, I knew the boy would look for him. It was the one thing that he would look for. He’d had that godfather on a string for years, he’d have had some little trick, an owl or a mirror, and I could have sent him a hundred dreams and he would only have had to check. I confess that I couldn’t see my way round it. And on the sixth of June you took up your pen. There was nowhere he could have looked. You took that man out of every place on earth where anyone could have found him, on the very day. You did it over the top of everybody’s head. Nobody else could have done it. Nobody would have dared.” He raised the glass, an inch, and set it down. “You moved him for me.”

She could hear a very small sound, regular and exact. Draco was cutting his bread into strips.

She had moved a piece on a board that neither of them could see. She had lain with her hands folded on her chest. She’d been able to feel her feet.

“Your father knows how to value that,” he went on. “He told them so. He stopped in front of the fountain, you know, in the middle of the Ministry of Magic, with his hands tied, and my friends in a line behind him, and he said it to the boy’s face. *By four o’clock, as I asked. You may tell my daughter it was very well done.* I thought it quite the best thing that he’s ever done. It has gone all over the county. He’s in a cell under the Wizengamot with a blanket, and I’m told that he’s a great deal easier in his mind.”

So that was what that was.

She had heard about it on the train; Parkinson had told it to her with tears in his eyes. She’d thought it was the last of his vanity, or the last of his malice. She looked at it. He had burnt the whole of what he had left, in front of every family that mattered, to buy her a chair at this table. He had known that she’d done nothing. He must have known. He had been cleaned out, and he had felt in all his pockets, and that was what he’d found. She kept her eyes on the gold rim. If she blinked, she thought, she would have to do something about it.

“Thank you, my lord,” said Lyra. “It’s very good of you to say so.”

“It’s nothing at all. Wormtail.”

She hadn’t seen the door open. It was the service door, in the panelling behind the sideboard. He came out of it sideways, a small soft dirty man in a good coat that had been made for somebody else. His eyes went round the table and they went away from hers at once. He had a nose that twitched. His hands were full of paper.

He held it out. He came all the way up the room, keeping close to the wall, and he stood at the end of the table and held it out in both hands, and it shook. It was a handful of bits and pieces, creased and dirty, some with a scorched edge. The Dark Lord took them from him, and shook out his napkin, and began laying them on the cloth, one at a time, smoothing each with his long finger.

“*He has nits*,” he read, “*and it preys upon him.*” He looked up. “I preferred it the way he said it, I think. It had more feeling.”

She knew the paper. She knew the ink. She kept it for her father. It was the sheet that she’d spoiled in January, on her stomach, with her feet in the air. She had crumpled it up. She had thrown it at somebody’s head.

“This has a date on it. *Sat. 17th. 8.30, not 9. Wear the socks.* I don’t quite follow that. In your hand. I imagine it’s a joke.” He laid it beside the first, and squared it. “Yes. This came out of a pocket. They do get so full, don’t they, boys’ pockets?” And he had the last of them between his finger and thumb, a long torn strip with a line ruled through it. He tilted it to the candles, and he read that as well, with interest, and for a moment he said nothing. It was very quiet. Even Bellatrix had stopped breathing through her mouth.

*A fear of the Bishop of Bath and Wells.*

“What a pity about the Bishop of Bath and Wells,” said the Dark Lord.

She didn’t feel it. She’d read about that. There was a thing that happened, she understood, in battles. The room stayed just where it was, and she sat in it, with her spoon; and a long way down, under the floor, something very big had started quietly to give.

Nobody knew about the Bishop. There was nobody in the world. It had been a stupid joke, on a stupid rug, with snow going past the window, and he had laughed so much that he got hiccups. Two people had known it. She had thought there was one room. She’d had the whole of her life in rooms that people could walk into, with their boots wiped, and there had been just the one, with a bad chair in it. It had a door that you could only find if you needed it. She’d put her feet up there. *I love you*, she had said. He’d cried there, with his glasses off. She had told him about *Please come*. She had told him what was in her head, and what she’d done to Hopkins, and what was the matter with her father. He had told her how the other one ended, or as near as he could, and when, and what was on the other side of the door marked *Can’t*—

There had been a rat. She had seen it in January, on the seventh floor, by the skirting. She had looked straight at it, and thought about something else.

“He has been most attentive,” said the Dark Lord. His eyes rested on the small soft man. “Five months, and never once a complaint. You wouldn’t believe the places that a little creature can get to. And you do keep it so warm up there.”

Wormtail looked at the carpet. His lips had drawn back from his front teeth in a kind of smile.

“You mustn’t reproach yourself. I shouldn’t like you to think I was displeased. I thought it was charming.” He gathered up the three bits of paper, and tapped their edges level on the cloth, and put them by his plate. “You keep a very tidy house, Miss Malfoy. You always have. I looked into it at this table, and I thought so then. I’ve been so hoping that you’d keep this one.”

The linen cupboard on the second floor, behind the old nurseries, was as deep as a small room, with slatted shelves up to the ceiling and a smell of lavender and hot pipes, and she had last been in it when she was eight.

She got the door shut. She got in at the back, on the floor, between the winter blankets. She took a double sheet down from the shelf, one of the good ones with the crest on it, and she folded it in four, and then she put her face in it, and she opened her mouth as far as it would go.

She didn’t know how long that was. It went on until there wasn’t any more air, and then it went on anyway. She had cried in an Owlery, with her nose running, and she knew how that went. This came from a good deal lower down, and it had no words in it, and it tore. Her arms hurt. She bit the sheet. She rocked. She thought, in some small clear place at the very top of her head, that nobody at all would have recognised the noise; that she’d have liked to go to him, and tell him; and that it was the only thing in the whole of that house that would not be going in a report.

When it stopped, she lay for a while with her cheek on the boards. She could see her own hand, a long way away. There was a pale ring round the fourth finger, and the ring was on it.

The sheet was wet through, and she had bitten a hole in the crest. She folded it, with the hole inside. She put it back at the bottom of the pile, with the corners true.

He told her why at nine o’clock the next morning, on the top step.

It was a soft bright day, and all the peacocks had come out on the lawn. He’d had a small table brought, with his coffee on it, which he didn’t drink. It was only sensible, he said. The Ministry had been through the house; they would not be so foolish as to leave it alone, now that poor Cornelius had seen what he’d seen. It would have to be taken off the map. It wanted a Fidelius, and a Fidelius wanted a Keeper, and he had given some thought to who that should be. Bellatrix had asked. So had others. He understood it had been done rather badly once before.

“The boy will come to you,” said the Dark Lord. “You see that, of course. Whatever you’ve told him; whatever he has been told. I had a look at the inside of his head in the Atrium, for a moment. It wasn’t a long visit. He’ll come through a wall. He can’t help it; he’s never in his life had anything else that he wanted. And when he has done with all his schemes and his little old wizards, and he has found nothing left that’s worth destroying, and he has worked out at last where I must be, there’ll be one person on earth who can tell him the way in.” He looked at her, with his head a little on one side. “I do so want him to find the door.”

It didn’t hurt, as she had thought it would. He stood her on the step with her back to the house, and laid one long white finger on her breastbone, and it was like being given something to swallow, very cold and very large, that went down and went on going. She felt the whole of the place come in at the top of her chest and settle, stair by stair: the limes, and the lake, and the long gallery, the kitchens with a loaf on the table, her mother and her brother. She had to put out a hand. It sat under her ribs, on the left, where she kept things; and when she looked up the drive was going away in the sun, with a little blue haze at the end of it, and the gates had gone.

After that she stood in the door.

They had to be told; it was the only way. He had made it so that it would not go on paper. Paper went astray, he said; a thing like this wanted saying. They came up from the road, one at a time, to the place where the gravel stopped, and stood in the sunshine looking about them at an empty field, and she walked down and said it: to Bellatrix, who kissed her; to Avery; to old Nott, who took off his hat. It took nine words. Towards three she walked down the drive for a thin man in black, with a long yellow face, who stood with his hands in his sleeves and looked at the air above her head. She told him where he was. He looked at her then, once, and she saw him take in her eyes, and what had been done about them.

“Miss Malfoy,” said Snape. He walked past her towards the house.

She went back and stood on the top step, in the shadow of the porch, and waited for the next. She had her hands folded. She had been told to stay there, and she was good at that. Nobody could fault her on it. The sun went round. *A Malfoy does not stand in doorways*, somebody had said to her once, in a warm low room with a fire in it. *She comes in, or she goes.*

The box came in with the coffee, at dinner. It was still Monday. She had lost hold of the day some time ago.

It was not large. It was made of some pale clean wood, like the inside of an almond, and it stood on the cloth in front of the Dark Lord with its lid off, and a card in a slot on the inside of the lid. They were lying in two layers, in pleated paper, each with a tiny curl of something on the top. They were very beautifully done. You could smell the chocolate from where she sat.

“Bella?” he said. “Will you have one?”

Aunt Bella had eaten everything that had been put in front of her since January. She had eaten a candle once, for a bet. She was sitting with her long hands in her lap, and she looked at the box, and then at him, and she began slowly to smile; it pulled at the corner of the cut, and a thin dark bead came out and ran.

“No, my lord,” said Aunt Bella.

He seemed to be amused. He turned his head. Draco was sitting very straight at his right hand, with his hair flat, and he had his eyes on the box, and they were shining. She had seen him look like that once. It had been at a very small dented tin.

“You know what you’re to do,” said the Dark Lord.

“Yes, my lord.” It was a clear small treble. He took a breath. “I keep it at the bottom of my trunk, and I don’t tell anybody. I don’t open it. On the last night of term, at the feast, I carry it up to the High Table, and I give it to the Headmaster, and I say it’s from all of us. In the second year. Because of how hard he’s worked.”

“Very good. And what is in the box, Draco?”

Lyra had put out a hand for her glass. It stayed where it was.

Draco looked at the box. He looked up, at the long white face that was bending towards him, with an expression of courteous inquiry. She had never seen him so happy. She hadn’t seen it since the snow.

“I don’t know,” said Draco. “That’s why.”
