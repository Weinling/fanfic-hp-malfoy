# Chapter 104: Not a Day After

He lay on the carpet by the end of the table, with one hand under him and the other flung out towards the fire, and he looked smaller than he had any right to.

That was the first thing. Harry had seen him dead before, in another life, on the floor of a hall in the morning, with half the school standing round; and he had looked small then, too, an ordinary long thin man with his robes rucked up, nothing that anybody need ever have been frightened of. That had surprised him then, and it surprised him again. The long white face was turned up to the ceiling with its eyes open, and there was nothing in them, not even surprise; and the pale old wand had rolled out of his fingers and lain down by the fender, beside the coal-scuttle, as if it had been put there to be cleaned.

Nobody moved. Nine people at a table, and a boy, and a dead man in black on the hearthrug, and a snake under the cloth with a fang in its head, and nobody moved at all.

Then something under the table made a sound.

Not loud: a small, high, astonished sound, like somebody who has been holding a door shut against a wind for a very long time and has felt the wind stop; and the cloth was dragged aside, and the Cloak came off with it, all silver and creased, and she was on her knees on the carpet among the chair-legs with her left arm held out in front of her in both hands, staring at it.

He could see it from where he stood. He could see it without his glasses. The grey-green line lay on the thin blue skin of her wrist in its two slow curves, with its small flat head on the pulse; and as he watched, as she watched, the colour went out of it. It went as colour goes out of a bruise, only very fast, grey and then paler than grey; and the eye, which had been open since the first of August, which had looked at the ceiling of a hospital wing and a stone floor and a pastry-cook’s shop and her, closed. The lid came down from the top and up from the bottom and met in the middle. It did not droop or stop halfway. It shut, quite tidily, like a box; and then it was not an eye any more, it was a mark, a faint raised shape like an old scar under the skin, the shape of a snake that had been drawn there once by somebody and rubbed out.

She put her other hand over it. She took it away. She looked.

Then she raised her head, and he saw her face, and he had never in his life seen it like that and did not think that he would again: open right through to the back, like a house with every door in it standing wide, and all the lamps lit.

She did not look at him. He had not expected her to. She looked along the floor, under the table, past the legs of the chairs, to where a small boy in a clean grey suit was standing by their mother with his wrist in her fist and his face the colour of the cloth.

“Draco,” said Lyra.

Her voice came out cracked and very loud. She did not seem to have expected it to come out at all.

“You can stop counting now.”

That did not last; it could not have. There was a house full of people, and not all of them had been sitting still because they were frightened.

Aunt Bella had been the first on her feet when the snake screamed, and she was the first to move now. She came round the end of the table in a rush, with her hair flying and her mouth open, not at Harry, not at the girl on the carpet, but at the thing on the floor by the fender; and she went down beside it on her knees and took the long white head in her hands, and shook it, as a child shakes a doll that will not wake, and began to make a noise that he would remember for the rest of his life.

Avery was gone. Harry had not seen him go; there was a door at the end of the room standing open, and the candle-flames by it all lying over the one way. Old Nott was standing quite still at his place with his hat in his hands, looking at the body with an expression of the most profound and private relief, as of a man who has been in debt for forty years and has just watched the bailiff fall downstairs. And a small soft dirty man by the service door had become, between one breath and the next, a rat, and gone.

“Narcissa,” said Harry. His own voice. He had forgotten it. “Take him out.”

She had already begun to. She had Draco by the wrist and she was going backwards, towards the door into the hall, with her body between the boy and the room and her eyes on her sister; and she had got as far as the door when Aunt Bella lifted her head from the dead face in her lap and saw where they were going.

“*You*,” said Aunt Bella.

She had her wand up. She was on her knees, with tears and paint running down her face, and her other hand still in the dead man’s hair, and she pointed the wand past all of them, past Harry and the girl on the floor and the table, at her sister’s back.

He knew the word before she said it. He heard her get her breath for it, the long breath that you had to have, and he knew where she had had it from: from a rug, in February, and a piece of pork, and an ear that had been listening. *I have that*, the Dark Lord had said, at a dinner-table. He had had it, and he had given it to the one person in this house who had wanted it since June.

She said the whole of it.

Narcissa Malfoy came round in the doorway as though somebody had caught her by the shoulder and turned her; and the grey silk opened from her collarbone down across her breast to the waist, as clean as a draper’s scissors, and she put her hand to it, and looked down at the red coming through her fingers with an expression of mild annoyance, as at a spill at table; and sat down on the threshold.

Draco did not make a sound.

Harry had the wand up. He did not know when. He said the word that took wands out of hands, and Aunt Bella’s went up out of her fingers and turned over twice in the candlelight and came down somewhere among the dishes, and he said another, and she went over sideways across the dead man’s legs and lay still, with her mouth open and her eyes open and her hand still in his hair.

He turned round.

Lyra was already there.

She had got up off the floor and across the room without his seeing her do it, and she was on her knees on the threshold in front of her mother, with the Cloak still trailing off one shoulder, and her hands flat on the grey silk, on either side of the red. She did not look up. She did not look at him or at anybody. She had her wand out, her own wand, his wand, that she had taken off him somehow on the way across the room without his noticing that either, and she held it low over her mother’s chest, an inch above the cut, and she took a breath.

It went right down. He saw it go. She had been taught where to put it, on a bald rug, by a boy with a toasting fork, beating time; and she had been taught by somebody before that, in a chapel in Aberystwyth, at second hand. She drew it in until her shoulders came up and then made them come down again, as she had been told, and she opened her mouth, and sang.

It was not good; he had known it would not be. A quarter of a tone under, at the start, as it always had been, and it wavered on the second note like a governess at a piano, and it was thin; and he stood in the middle of the Malfoys’ dining room in a borrowed dress with his own feet coming out of the ends of her stockings and listened to it, and could not move.

The edges of the cut began to come together.

They came slowly, a little at a time, under the wand, as the edges of a piece of pork had come together on a rug, with the red going back into them. She held the note. She held it well past the place where she ran out of breath, where she had always run out of breath, all through February, and gone red, and stopped, and thrown the toasting fork at him; she held it now with her eyes shut and the tears running down her face and her voice going thinner and thinner and not stopping, like a kettle on a stove that somebody has forgotten; and the seam came together under it from the waist to the collarbone, raised and pale, and stayed.

She stopped. She sat back on her heels.

Her mother opened her eyes. She looked at the seam, and at her daughter, and at the red on her own hand; and she wiped the hand on the carpet, carefully, and then she put it up, still not quite clean, and laid it flat on the top of Lyra’s head.

“You were flat,” said Narcissa Malfoy.

“I know.”

“Your father was always flat.” Her voice had gone very quiet and very far away. “I used to like it. It meant one always knew where he was in a church.”

Draco was sitting on the floor of the hall just outside the door, against the wall, with his knees drawn up. He was hiccupping. He did it quite steadily, about once every four seconds, with great concentration, and nobody told him to stop.

She sent the fox at a quarter to midnight, from the front steps.

He went out with her. She had asked him to, in so many words, aloud; he had heard her say *Potter, come with me*, in the middle of the hall, in a voice that had nobody listening to it, and he had found that he could not get over that, and went. The night was clear and very cold. The lamps were lit along the drive, and the limes stood up black on either side of it; and somewhere out on the lawn, in the dark, a peacock was making the noise that peacocks make, like a child being murdered at a great distance, over and over, quite unconcerned.

She stood on the top step with her wand out. He saw her think of something. He did not know what. Her mouth moved, a little, and she shut her eyes; and the fox came out of the end of the wand in a long silver rush and stood on the gravel below them, looking up at her with its brush out behind it and its ears pricked, bright enough to throw shadows.

“Go to Remus Lupin,” said Lyra. “Tell him this.”

And she said it.

She said it aloud, on her own front steps, to the night, in the clear carrying voice that he had heard read the whole of Herodotus to nobody: nine words, and the name of a county. She said it slowly. She said it as though it were a thing she was allowed to say. The fox put its head on one side and listened to every word of it, and then it turned and went away down the drive between the limes, low and fast, like a flame along a fuse, and out at the gates, and was gone.

She stood looking after it.

Then she said, quite deliberately, in the same clear voice, a word that he had not known she knew. She said it to the lawn. She said it again, louder, with great care over the consonants, and then another one, worse, that she could only have had from Ron; and then she said his name, *Harry*, for no reason at all, to see what it was like to say it out of doors; and she started to laugh, on the top step of Malfoy Manor in her stockinged feet, with her arms round herself, and could not stop.

Lupin came first, a little after one.

He came up the drive from the gates on foot, in his old coat, with his wand lit and no hat; and he stopped where the gravel stopped, as everybody always did, and looked about him with his mouth a little open, at the house coming up out of the dark. He looked at it for a long time. Then he saw them on the steps, and came on.

He did not ask anything. He looked at Harry, in a grey silk dress with a square neck and somebody else’s stockings and his own face, with no glasses, holding a girl’s wand; and at Lyra beside him, with her arm bare and the mark on it shut; and at the open door behind them, and what lay beyond it on the hearthrug, which could be seen from the steps. And whatever he had been going to say went out of his face, and he took off his coat, and put it round Harry’s shoulders, and kept his hands there for a moment, quite hard.

“Right,” said Lupin. “Right. I’ve got you.”

The others came after. Moody, by the sound of it, stumping up the gravel and swearing. Kingsley. Some people Harry did not know, and then some that he did. Lights went on in the house, one window after another, along the whole of the long front, as people walked through it who had never been inside it and opened every door.

He did not go back in. He could not have said why. He sat down on the steps, on the second from the top, in Lupin’s coat, and stayed there.

It got light at about six.

It came up grey behind the house, and then yellow, and the frost on the lawn went pink at the edges and then white again; and the peacocks came out from wherever they had spent the night and stood about on the grass in the cold with their tails down, looking for breakfast and finding the lawn full of Aurors. One of them had got as far as the bottom step. It stood a yard from Harry’s foot and looked at him with one eye, and then with the other, as if it were trying to decide which of them it believed.

Draco was asleep against Lupin, on the step below. He had gone over at some point in the night, sideways, still sitting up, and Lupin had let him, and had not moved since, though his arm must have died hours ago. The bow tie was still under the boy’s ear. Somebody had put a blanket round him with a crest on it.

They had won.

He sat on the step and said that to himself, to see what it was like. He had been waiting for it for two lives. The thing in the dining room was dead. The diary and the locket and the crown and the cup and the ring and the snake: six, and every one of them gone. There was a seventh, and he had always known where that one was, and last night he had stood on a hearthrug and lifted his chin to have it taken out of him in the only way that he knew; and somebody had stepped in the way. He did not know yet what that meant. He had done it, all the same. Not in a Great Hall in the morning with half the world watching, but in a borrowed dress on a carpet, by himself, with a girl’s wand, and he had meant it. It was done.

He waited to feel something.

He did not feel anything. He sat in the cold with the sun coming up and a peacock looking at him and waited, as you wait at the end of a long journey to feel the ground under you, and there was nothing. He had expected that, a little. He had thought it might take a while.

And then he did feel something.

It came quite slowly, from a long way down, as a thing comes up through deep water: a warmth, very slight, somewhere behind his right ear. Not unpleasant; rather pleasant, in fact: the feeling of a man who has come home after a long time away and found the house still standing, and the fire laid, and the chair where he had left it; and who sits down in the chair, with a little sigh, and stretches out his legs, and looks about him, and finds everything most satisfactory.

It was not his.

He knew that at once, the way you know a voice in the next room. He did not feel like that. He had never in his life felt like that about anything, and there was nothing in the whole of this cold grey morning for him to feel like that about; and he sat very still on the step with Lupin’s coat round him and the warmth spreading, slowly, comfortably, through the back of his head, and understood, with the whole of him, exactly where the last of it had gone.

There had been nowhere else to go.

Dumbledore came last, a little after seven, when the sun was full up.

He came alone, up the drive, between the limes, walking slowly, with his robes the colour of the morning sky and his left arm in its black sling and his beard silver in the light; and the Aurors on the lawn stopped what they were doing to watch him come, as people always had. He did not look at them. He came to the foot of the steps and stopped there, beside the peacock.

He looked at the open door first, and through it, along the hall, to the dining room, where they had not yet moved anything from the hearthrug. Harry watched him see it. He saw him see both of them: the long black shape lying face down, and the other, beyond, on its back with its eyes open, by the fender. He saw him look for a long time at the first.

Then he looked at Harry.

He looked at him as he had looked at a box of chocolates on his desk in December, without touching it, for a little while; and Harry watched him understand. He watched him understand all of it, in order, as if he were reading it off a page: the snake and the cup and the ring, and whose wand it was, and whose arm, and the order in which things had happened in that room last night, and what that order meant. The blue eyes went over Harry’s face, slowly, from one side to the other, and stopped.

Harry could see himself in the half-moon spectacles. Two small figures, one in each lens, with the morning behind them: a boy on a step, in an old coat, with his hair on end and a girl’s wand in his lap. He looked at them. They looked back.

He did not know the face. That was the thing. It was his, and he did not know it. It was patient, and quite cold, and there was no hurry in it at all; it looked as if it had all the time in the world, and already knew how everything would end.

He heard Lyra, behind him in the doorway, draw a breath.

He felt his arm move.

It came up from the shoulder. He had not lifted it. He felt it go: smooth and level, as easy as a thing on a wire, with no weight in it at all, and the girl’s wand in the hand at the end of it, pointing down the steps at the old man in blue. It knew where it was going. It had been waiting a long time to go there. And something in the back of his head, warm and comfortable and very pleased, leaned forward in its chair.

He threw everything he had against it. Everything. It was like throwing yourself against the side of a house.

“*Mine*,” said his mouth.

Not a whisper: quite clear, and quite loud, and it carried across the gravel to the Aurors on the lawn, and it was not his voice, though it came out of him; it was high, and cold, and it had been saying that word to him behind his right ear since a corridor when he was eleven, and it was saying it now out loud, in the sunshine, to the whole of England, and it was delighted.

Dumbledore had his wand in his hand.

Harry saw it. He saw it come out of the sleeve, quite quick, the long knobbled wand that had never yet lost him a duel, and he saw the old man’s hand close on it; and he saw that there was time. There was all the time in the world. Dumbledore could have done anything at all. He was the greatest wizard of the age and he was standing at the foot of a flight of steps with his wand drawn and a boy above him who could not, for his life, move his own arm; and he looked up at Harry, at the face in his own spectacles, for as long as it takes to read a line of print.

Then he lowered it.

He did it quite deliberately. He let the hand fall to his side, with the wand pointing at the gravel, and he did not raise it again. He looked up the steps, past the arm, past the wand, at Harry, as though there were somebody in there whom he could still see perfectly well; and his face did not show fear. Harry would remember that. It showed something much worse, something that he had seen on it once before, across a desk, by the light of a fire, and had not then been able to name.

It was fond.

The green had already begun at the end of the wand. Harry felt it go. He felt the word come up through him, the whole of it, both halves, in that high pleased voice, and he could not stop one letter of it.

“Until you are seventeen, Harry,” said Albus Dumbledore. “And not a day after.”
