# Chapter 29: Every Ounce

Weasley was waiting at the foot of the marble staircase on Monday morning with his ears the colour of the Gryffindor banners, and he was plainly not waiting for breakfast.

Lyra saw him from the top of the dungeon steps. She had come up early with Crabbe, Goyle having got as far as one stocking and told them to go on without her, and she stopped where she was. One did not walk into the middle of a thing like that. Besides, the dungeon steps were in shadow and the Entrance Hall was not.

Potter came down the marble staircase with Granger. He was talking, and Granger was answering him over an armful of books, and then he saw who was standing at the bottom and stopped talking, and came down the last few steps more slowly.

Weasley said something. Lyra could not hear it over the noise of people going in to breakfast, and in any case he said it mostly to the floor: a short mumble with a shrug at the end, which was how boys of his sort said anything that had cost them something.

Potter did not let him finish. He came off the bottom step and put both arms round him, hard, as though he had been standing at the top of that staircase for a fortnight waiting to be allowed to. Weasley staggered, and said “Gerroff,” and did not push him off.

Then Granger burst into tears.

She did it all at once, over the top of her books, and then she dropped the books and put an arm round each of them and held on, saying something wet and furious that seemed to be about both of them being idiots. Weasley stood in the middle of it with his hands up at the height of his shoulders, as though somebody had pointed a wand at him, opening and shutting his fingers, with no idea where to put them.

Gryffindors, Lyra reflected, did everything in public. They apologised in Entrance Halls, and wept on staircases, and left their schoolbooks on the flagstones for anybody to tread on, and seemed to feel a great deal better for it. It was a wonder any of them had a scrap of dignity left by the fourth year.

Over Weasley’s shoulder, Granger opened her wet eyes and saw her.

It lasted a moment. Granger looked straight across the Hall to the dungeon steps, to where Lyra stood in the shadow with Crabbe, and there was something in her face that Lyra did not care for at all. It was not quite gratitude; Granger had learned better than to offer Lyra that. It was knowing. Weasley had told them where he had got it from. Of course he had. Weasley could not have kept a secret in a locked trunk.

Lyra examined the ceiling.

“That was you,” said Crabbe, beside her.

“I have no idea what you mean.”

“All right,” said Crabbe, and went on in to breakfast.

Lyra followed more slowly. She was glad. That was the irritating part. For the last week Potter had been cheerful in a loud, careless way that had taken in the entire school, and now he would not have to be; he had them back, both of them, at every meal and in every lesson and in the next bed, and she had done that, and she was glad, and it put her in a temper all through breakfast.

Because she had something for him too. She had been carrying it since last night, since the shingle and the blue eye in the dark: three things off Karkaroff’s table, and nowhere to set them down. Potter was going to spend the whole day inside a knot of Gryffindors, and the next day, and the day after that. She could hardly walk up to him in the Great Hall and say, in front of four Houses and two foreign schools, *Crouch sent his son to Azkaban to die*.

She buttered a piece of toast, and looked at it, and arrived at a decision.

Three crumbs off a man’s table were not worth the risk of handing them over. Her father had never in his life gone into a room holding half a hand, and nor would she. There was a first task in ten days that nobody in the castle was supposed to know anything about. Somebody did. Somebody always did. And by the time she got Potter on his own, she meant to have the whole of it.

She began that evening, with a chessboard.

The Durmstrang students had been eating at the Slytherin table for three weeks, and in all that time nobody at it had found out anything about them except that they were large, and always hungry, and talked among themselves in a language with a great many more consonants than it needed. Lyra borrowed Montague’s chessboard after dinner, set it up at the end of the table, and began a game against herself. Within ten minutes two Durmstrang seventh-years were standing at her shoulder, unable to bear what she was doing with the black pieces. Within twenty she was losing to both of them, carefully, a piece at a time, and they were talking.

A girl alone at a chessboard with two foreign boys looked like what it was, so on Tuesday there were three boards, and Theodore Nott and Blaise Zabini had been told they were needed to make up the numbers. Nott, who in four years had addressed perhaps ten words to Crabbe, said eleven that evening, all of them about her knight. Zabini lost to Goyle three times running and did not appear to mind.

The Durmstrang boys knew less than she had hoped. Their headmaster had them studying, they said, in the evenings, in his cabin. One of them pulled a face about it, and the other kicked him under the table and said they were not supposed to say what. Lyra put her queen where it would be taken, and said she quite understood.

The Beauxbatons girls were easier. They were cold, all the time, and miserable about it, and dying to complain to somebody who would understand what it was to be sent to Scotland in November in silk; and Lyra’s French was very good. She sat with three of them in the library on Monday and Tuesday, comparing charms for keeping hair smooth in damp weather, and learned that their headmistress went out walking at night. In the grounds. With the *garde-chasse*.

“The giant,” said one of them, and shuddered elegantly.

Lyra said that she believed Professor Hagrid was very fond of animals, and kept the rest. A headmistress did not go walking in a Scottish November for her health. And a man who had a hundred Blast-Ended Skrewts in boxes and did not know what they ate was exactly the man somebody would put in charge of something large and dangerous, and just the man who would want to show it to a lady.

On Tuesday afternoon, Viktor Krum stopped beside her at the end of lunch.

“Perhaps one day we fly,” he said, as though resuming a conversation they had broken off a minute ago instead of nine days. “Today?”

The Firebolt had not been out of its case since September. Lyra fetched it.

The pitch was bare. There was no Cup this year and no reason for anybody to be there, and the stands stood round it empty, with the wind going through them. Krum took the Firebolt from her as a jeweller takes a stone, and turned it over, and ran his thumb along the handle; and then he held out his own. It was black and plain, with no name on it anywhere. A man in Sofia had made it, he said. That man made brooms only for him.

Lyra had flown a great many brooms. She had never flown anything like that one.

The Firebolt did what you meant a moment before you meant it. Krum’s broom did what you meant and something of its own besides. It pulled to the left. It turned on a Knut, and kept turning if you let it, and twice in the first minute it very nearly had her off. Then she found out what it wanted, and gave it that, and after that they got on. She had not been off the ground since the spring. She took the black broom up until the castle was a toy and the lake a dish of pewter, and stayed there, with nobody near enough to speak to her, and let the wind have her hair.

Krum flew the Firebolt below her the whole time, and watched.

When they came down he did not look at her face. He looked at her hands.

“On the turn, you hold too tight,” he said. “Is fine. You fly like Bulgarian.”

Lyra took it as the compliment it plainly was, and asked, as they walked back across the grass, whether his headmaster was not very busy just now, with the first task so close.

“Professor Karkaroff says, we wait.” Krum shrugged. He did not know, then; or did not care to know, which in Krum came to the same thing. She had decided on the ship that he was a genius on a broom and nowhere else, and nothing that afternoon changed her mind.

At the edge of the pitch he handed her back the Firebolt, and stopped.

“The girl in the library,” he said. “With the hair.” He made a gesture round his own head that took in a great deal of hair. “She is Potter’s?”

“She is not Potter’s anything,” said Lyra.

It came out much too fast.

Krum considered that.

“Good,” he said, and took his black broom, and went off down the slope towards the lake without another word; and Lyra stood at the edge of the pitch with the Firebolt over her shoulder and no idea at all what was good about it.

That night, with a Durmstrang boy halfway through explaining a Bulgarian opening to her, the ring began to pulse in her pocket: warm, and then cool, and then warm. He was looking for her. Lyra moved a bishop, and lost it, and did not get up.

Double Potions on Wednesday was with the Gryffindors, and half the Slytherin side of the dungeon came down to it wearing badges.

Zacharias Smith, it appeared, had found a market outside his own House. Parkinson had bought three. He leaned across the bench and offered Lyra one, pressing it with his thumb so that SUPPORT CEDRIC DIGGORY slid away into POTTER STINKS and back again.

“I have a badge, Panther.”

She had. It was in her pocket, where it had been since Hallowe’en, beside a ring she no longer put on at nine and a sugar quill that had gone from one set of robes to the next for more than a year. None of the three was any business of Parkinson’s. He looked at her pocket, and at her face, and put his badges away.

They were brewing antidotes. Snape came down the Slytherin bench halfway through, looked into Lyra’s cauldron for some time, and said, “Adequate,” and moved on. He had not said *Faultless* since the spring. Lyra had stopped waiting for it, more or less.

Across the dungeon, Potter was sitting with Weasley again, and Weasley was saying something out of the side of his mouth that kept making Potter laugh into his sleeve. It was a perfectly ordinary laugh. Lyra had not heard it for a fortnight, and she put more powdered bezoar into her antidote than it wanted, and had to fix it.

At the end of the lesson they took their flasks up to Snape’s desk to be marked. Potter went up early. His way there took him past the end of the Slytherin bench, and as he passed, a scrap of folded parchment came to rest against Lyra’s mortar, so neatly that Crabbe, on her left, did not look up from her cauldron.

This time Lyra saw him do it, and he knew she had.

*Seven. The broom shed.*

There was no signature. There was only one broom shed he could mean. She had been in it once, at the beginning of the longest evening of her life, among a lot of Shooting Stars with their twigs going every way at once.

At seven o’clock tonight she had a chessboard and two Durmstrang seventh-years, one of whom had begun, last night, to look like a boy with something on the tip of his tongue. And she was not going to the broom shed with three crumbs.

She tore a corner off her parchment and wrote on it, and folded it, and on her way up with her own flask she laid it on the end of the Gryffindor bench, beside his elbow, without looking at him.

*Prior engagement. Sunday, two o’clock.*

He took it the way he took Snitches, without appearing to look. By the time she had handed her flask to Snape and turned round, he had it open under the bench.

He read it twice. Then he folded it very small and put it in his pocket and sat for a moment with his hand flat on the pocket, looking at nothing, and Weasley glanced at him and stopped talking. Then Potter pushed back his stool. He was going to come across the dungeon at the bell, she saw; he was going to walk her out of the door and down the corridor and under that cloak, in front of Snape and Parkinson and every badge in the room, and argue with her there.

The dungeon door opened.

It was a small boy with mousy hair and a camera round his neck, and he went straight up to Snape’s desk, beaming like somebody sent with good news.

“Please, sir, I’m to take Harry Potter upstairs. All the champions, sir. Mr Bagman says it’s the weighing of the wands, and he’s to bring his things.”

Snape looked across the dungeon at Potter, who was standing behind his stool with one hand still on his pocket, and Lyra watched him enjoy it.

“Potter has finished his potion,” said Snape. “By all means. Take him.”

Potter picked up his bag. The small boy chattered at him all the way to the door, and at the door Potter looked back once, over the top of the boy’s head, across the whole length of the dungeon; and then he was gone, and the door shut.

Lyra went back to her bench and scoured her cauldron until she could see her face in it.

Sunday. Four days. She meant to have the whole of it by then.

On Sunday morning she wrote to her mother, as she did every Sunday: two pages about Arithmancy and the weather and what the Beauxbatons girls did to their hair, and nothing whatever about anything. She did not ask about Durmstrang. Her mother had once, apparently, said no to a whole country on Lyra’s behalf and never mentioned it, and Lyra could not find a way of putting the question that did not sound like an accusation, or else like thanks. The postscript behind the tables of values went unanswered for another week. Under the letter to her mother lay a sheet that said *Dear Professor Lupin*, and nothing after it.

Draco had written by return about the autograph: two whole sides, all of it Krum, until the very bottom.

*The gentleman says Krum shows his feint too often. He says a man who shows you his best trick has not got another one.*

Lyra read that twice, and put the letter away under the others.

At half past eleven a Durmstrang boy brought a note down the Slytherin table. Professor Karkaroff would be honoured if Lucius’s daughter would take luncheon on the ship.

He had been up half the night. She saw it as soon as she sat down, in his eyes, and in the way he kept laughing at nothing, like a man who has won at cards and may not tell anybody how much. He held out through the soup. Over the fish he called her *one of us*, twice, the way her father had once written it to her inside a letter; and somewhere between the fish and the pudding the whole of it came out of him, and Lyra let it, and admired him for it, and ate the pudding. She had been brought up at tables like that one. She knew how to sit at them and what to say, and how to make a man like Igor Karkaroff feel that he was among his own. He was.

Then she carried every word of it off the ship, and up the slope, to Harry Potter, as fast as she could run.

It was nearly half past two when she reached the broom shed. Potter was there. He was not under the cloak. He was leaning against the side of the shed in the thin sunshine, in plain view of the lawn and of half the school, which was out on it, it being Sunday and dry; down on the lake, the Durmstrang boys were diving off the ship.

“You might have looked at your map,” said Lyra, getting her breath, “and seen me coming.”

“Haven’t got it. Moody borrowed it, after the Goblet. He wants to see who’s moving about at night.”

“He can see through walls.”

“Not names.”

Lyra did not like it. She could not have said why, except that she did not like Moody, and that a man who could see through walls and now had every name in the castle as well seemed to her to have a great deal more than anybody needed. She pushed her hair back off her face. She was pink from running and very pleased with herself, and she was waiting for him to ask.

He did not ask. He was looking at the lake.

“You’ve been busy,” he said.

“One of us has to be.”

“Ron saw you on the pitch on Tuesday. From the tower. On Krum’s broom.”

“It pulls to the left,” said Lyra. “Did you get my note?”

“I got it.” He did not look round. “Prior engagement.”

He said it as though it tasted of something. Lyra had run all the way up from the lake, and had been charming to a Death Eater through three courses, and she was in no mood to be spoken to in that voice by a boy who had spent the week doing—what, exactly?

“And what have *you* been doing? To get ready?”

“For Thursday?” Potter shrugged. “Nothing. You can’t. Nobody knows what it is till the day.”

Lyra stared at him.

“Nobody knows— Potter. Did you listen to a single word Dumbledore said at Hallowe’en?”

“Er.”

“*I am sure I can count upon all of you to give your champions every ounce of support you can muster.*” She came a step nearer. “Every ounce. It does not mean *cheering*.”

“Doesn’t it?”

“It means everything you have. Every friend, every favour, every door you can get your foot in. Why do you suppose Madame Maxime and Karkaroff brought their whole top years with them? To sit in the stands and wave? It’s your castle. It’s your ground. You’ve got two champions to their one, and you heard them scream about it, and they are going to do *anything* to win this, and you—” another step, “—you are going to walk out on Thursday and wait to find out what is going to set you on fire. I told you not to try too hard. I didn’t tell you to go in knowing *nothing*!”

She had come right up to him. Behind her, a knot of second-years was coming along the path round the end of the greenhouses. Potter looked at them over her shoulder, and then something silvery and cool came down over both of them at once, and the second-years went by a yard off, arguing about Exploding Snap, and saw nobody.

It was very close under the cloak, and dim, and quiet. Somewhere up in the castle there was an eye that could see through this as if it were a windowpane. Let it.

“Do you know,” said Lyra, low, “what I have been doing all week?”

“Krum,” said Potter.

“*Karkaroff*.”

She told him. That it had not been Karkaroff: she had watched him rage about the Goblet, and she knew what lying looked like, and that had not been it. That he had said Barty Crouch would cut out his own tongue before he bent a rule; a man who had sent his own son to Azkaban, to die there. And then, *and when they did for the Longbottoms*—and had stopped, and looked at the door.

Potter did not ask her what had been done to the Longbottoms. He did not ask anything. He stood under the cloak a few inches from her and was quiet for long enough that she understood he had no need to ask; and she put that with all the other things he knew and did not say, and for once she did not ask him either.

“All week,” he said at last. “The chess. Ron said you were at your table every night with half of Durmstrang.”

“Where else would I find them?”

He let out a long breath, like somebody setting down a thing he had carried up a great many stairs. She had no idea what it was, and no time to wonder.

“And that isn’t the half of it. The first task—”

“Lyra.”

“What?”

“Can I kiss you?”

She stopped.

The last time he had asked her that, she had been a ferret, and had shown him her teeth. There had always been a reason, every time before. A Dementor. A word he would not let her finish. A tail. There was no reason now. Nobody needed stopping, and nothing was going to wear off, and if she said yes, then she would simply have said it.

“Yes,” said Lyra.

It was the shortest thing she had ever said to him. Nothing came after it. She had always had something to put after things.

They both leaned the same way, and their noses met, and she said, “Other way,” exactly as she might have said it to an elf with a tray, and he laughed, and she felt him laugh; and then it was the other way, and it was not funny in the least. It went on. His hands were on her shoulders, and hers had hold of his sleeves, and the cloak slid off one of her shoulders and she pulled it back up without stopping. Down on the lake somebody from Durmstrang dived off the ship and came up shouting about the cold, and climbed out, and by the sound of it dived in again.

When it stopped, she spoke first, before he could.

“That one counted.”

He did not say anything clever. For a moment he did not say anything. Then: “You were saying. The first task.”

She had no idea what she had been saying. She had to go back for it, the way one goes back up a staircase for a dropped glove.

“Dragons,” said Lyra.

Then the whole of it, at once, in one breath, like a dispatch from a battle. Madame Maxime had gone into the Forest at midnight on Hagrid’s arm, in her best, and Karkaroff had followed them through the trees. There was a clearing. There were four of them, in pens, with wizards to hold them: one for each champion. They were mothers, sitting on nests; they had been brought with their eggs, and Karkaroff said that made them worse. One was Welsh, and one Swedish, and one Chinese, and one was a Hungarian Horntail, which Karkaroff said was the worst of the lot, and he had said it as though he were recommending a wine.

Potter heard all of it. When she ran out of breath he said, “Dragons,” quite quietly, and nothing else; and she could not have told from his face whether it was news, and she did not try.

“Lyra. That’s— I owe you for—”

“Don’t.”

He stopped.

She did not want it owed. She wanted him to have it.

He looked at her for a moment longer under the cloak. Then he said he had to go, Ron and Hermione were waiting, and he would have to tell them. At the edge of the cloak, with one hand on it, he stopped.

“I told them. On Monday. About the Horcruxes. Dumbledore said I could, if there was anybody I trusted.”

Granger, then. Granger knew now, and Weasley, all of it: the diary and the number and the cave. It came up in Lyra quick and sharp, before she could do anything about it.

“How lovely for them.”

“You knew first,” said Potter.

He lifted the cloak off her shoulders, and she was standing by the broom shed in the sunshine on her own, and a ripple went away across the grass towards the castle.

She went to the library after dinner, because a person who meant to sit in a stand on Thursday and watch somebody walk out to meet a dragon ought at least to know what one was; and Granger was there before her.

She was at a table by the window, behind a wall of books: *Dragon Species of Great Britain and Ireland*, and *From Egg to Inferno*, and something called *Men Who Love Dragons Too Much*. So he had told her already. It had taken him less than three hours. Lyra had expected that, and it went in a little all the same.

Two tables further along sat Viktor Krum, on his own, with a book open in front of him at a page he was not reading. He was looking over the top of it at Granger. He was not looking at her hands.

*She is Potter’s?*

*Good.*

Lyra stood quite still among the shelves and very nearly laughed out loud in Madam Pince’s library, which would have been the end of her.

Then she went and pulled out the chair opposite Granger and sat down in it without asking. Granger looked up, saw who it was, and looked down again. Neither of them said a word.

After a minute, Granger took the top book off her pile, opened it, found a place, and pushed it across the table without lifting her eyes from her own.

“Page three hundred and twelve,” she said. “Horntails.”
