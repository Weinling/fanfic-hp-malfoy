# Year One, Part 2: Nobody Asked You for Christmas

Oliver Wood showed him the Golden Snitch on a Wednesday evening, on the empty pitch, with the light going.

“The Chasers, the Beaters, the Keeper,” said Wood, “all that’s very important, and I’ll make you learn every bit of it. But there’s one position that makes the rest of it look silly. That’s you.” He held up a walnut-sized golden ball with silver wings, fluttering against his fingers. “Seeker catches the Snitch, a hundred and fifty points and the game’s over. Unless one side’s miles better than the other, that’s the match. So we only give it to somebody special.” He looked at Harry as though he were already somebody special, which Harry was not used to. “Go on, then. Catch it.”

He let go. Harry kicked off.

It took him under five minutes. He came down with the Snitch beating in his fist like a heart, and Wood stood there with his mouth open.

“That’s it,” Wood said faintly. “That’s it. We’re winning the Cup this year.”

Harry looked at the little gold ball in his hand and felt proud all the way through.

Flying was brilliant. Hunting the Snitch while it darted away from him was brilliant. And quite apart from that—

Being the hero of a Quidditch match, with everybody looking up at him; and Lyra Malfoy, in the Slytherin stand, having to look at him again.

That sounded brilliant too.

At Hallowe’en, he and Ron, with a lot of luck and a bit of Hermione, knocked out a mountain troll that had got into a girls’ lavatory. After that they were famous in Gryffindor Tower, and the name that had followed him about since the Leaky Cauldron—*the Boy Who Lived*—began to follow him round the corridors as well.

In his first match his broom went mad underneath him for a few minutes, bucking and rolling as if it wanted him off, and he hung on to it by one hand, fifty feet up, with the whole school watching. Then it stopped, as suddenly as it had started, and he went after the Snitch and caught it, more or less in his mouth.

Gryffindor had beaten Slytherin. The rest of the school had been on the edge of their seats the whole time he was dangling, and they cheered him all the way back to the castle. Only the Slytherins were sour about it.

Lyra Malfoy did not like it at all.

She had been used to being the centre of things. Now people were looking at him. So she took to being horrible to him and Ron whenever she could, in corridors, at meals, outside lessons, as though she could make the looking stop.

A week before Christmas, he and Ron were helping Hagrid drag a fir tree up to the castle when a cold, sneering voice came from behind them.

“Trying to earn a bit of extra money, Weasley? Hoping to be gamekeeper yourself when you leave, I suppose? That hut of Hagrid’s must look like a palace to you, after what your family’s used to.”

Malfoy had not forgiven Gryffindor for the match. She had been picking fights with Harry and Ron every time she saw them, and today was no different. She stood there in her good winter cloak, with Crabbe and Goyle at her shoulders, looking at Ron as if he were something she had stepped in.

“I expect your family’d be glad of one less mouth to feed. Wouldn’t they?”

Ron went for her. Harry caught him by the back of his robes just in time.

“Leave it, Ron. If you go for her, she’ll only enjoy it more.”

“But she—my *family*—”

“Getting angry’s just what she wants.”

Malfoy clicked her tongue, and turned to Harry instead.

“Well, at least you’ve got somewhere to go, Weasley. Some people have nobody who wants them at Christmas, so they have to stay at school.”

It was meant for Harry, and everybody knew it. Ron’s face went from red to purple.

But Harry patted Ron on the shoulder, and walked straight up to Malfoy.

She was standing there looking enormously pleased with herself, because it had worked. It was a clear, childish, superior sort of smile, like a cat’s.

Harry stopped in front of her, and to everybody’s astonishment, he smiled back. It was a perfectly friendly smile.

“Can’t be helped,” he said. “My mum and dad died stopping Voldemort.”

*Voldemort.*

Malfoy flinched as though he had thrown something at her. So did Crabbe and Goyle. All three of them took a step back.

In a world where wizards believed names had power, you did not say the name of the most evil wizard who had ever lived. Harry had grown up among Muggles and had no fear of it; but to a pureblood girl like Malfoy it was something you did not even like to think.

Harry did not stop there. He took another step.

“And because of them, Voldemort’s gone. Isn’t that what everybody says? So everybody ought to be grateful to my mum and dad. Shouldn’t they? You too, Malfoy.”

She had lost already. Harry could talk about Voldemort and his parents’ deaths as though he were talking about the weather. Malfoy could not hear it as calmly as he said it.

Harry kept smiling, and finished it.

“Or do you hate me? For getting rid of Voldemort?”

Malfoy turned on her heel and almost ran. Crabbe and Goyle scurried after her.

It was a win on points, clean and complete. Ron, who seemed to have forgotten he had ever been angry, came bounding up.

“Harry, that was *brilliant!* You shut *Malfoy* up!”

“It was nothing much.”

Harry watched her go, her fair hair bright against the snow.

“You just answer back straight, and smile first. Then you win.”

“That’s what’s brilliant about it! She says all that, and you *smile* at her. How do you even do that?”

“Dunno,” said Harry. “You get used to it?”

Back in the common room, Ron told everybody how Harry had seen Malfoy off. A great many of them had had a bad time from Malfoy, and they all wanted to hear it twice. Neville, who had had the worst time of all, looked at Harry as though he were a hero out of a book.

Harry found he could not enjoy it much.

When everybody hated something that you liked, it turned out not to be as much fun as you would think. Not even when it made you a hero.

“Harry Potter,” said Lyra. “How *dare* he.”

It had been a complete rout.

She had never imagined he would just *say* the Dark Lord’s name like that, out loud, in the snow, as if it were anybody’s.

She had not had one word to say back. She had turned and fled, and the fact of having fled at all was enough, all by itself, to make her want to scream. A Malfoy did not run from anybody.

“He thinks he’s so clever. Going about with a blood traitor, and him nothing but an orphan himself. He’s going to pay for this…”

“But how?” said Goyle doubtfully. “If he says—you know—*His* name again…”

That was the trouble. The name was a thing you did not say. And Lyra, and Crabbe, and Goyle had parents who had been on the Dark Lord’s side, in the old days, before he fell; to the three of them the name was harder still. They had grown up with a fear of it that was nearly a kind of reverence, and when Harry Potter said it as easily as *pass the salt*, it was like a slap.

The Dark Lord was gone; they could not borrow his name to frighten anybody with. But they could not say it themselves, either, the way Potter did. Something in them pulled back from it, every time.

It was a curse you could not block. A thing only a child who knew nothing could do.

“He only dares because Dumbledore’s at his back,” said Lyra. “If the Dark Lord were really here, he wouldn’t say a—oh.”

She stopped. A slow smile came over her face.

“There’s a way.”

“A way?”

“It’s simple. Potter’s the only one stupid enough to say the name. So we leave him alone, and go for everybody round him.”

It was not only Lyra who was afraid of the name. Every child from a wizarding family was, and most of them a good deal more than she was.

And there was nothing much in Gryffindor apart from Potter. A half-wit like Longbottom would burst into tears if she so much as looked at him.

“Poor little blood-traitor Weasley. Know-it-all Granger. And Longbottom, who can’t remember his own name. There’s plenty to go round. We’ll show them what happens when they make us angry.”

“Oh, yes!” Goyle bounced on her toes. “They’re all useless! That’s brilliant, Malfoy!”

Lyra’s nose went up a little. She waited for more applause, and found, at the moment it ought to have come, that Crabbe was standing there with a blank look on her face.

“Crabbe? What are you mooning about?”

“What? Oh—um.”

Crabbe, who had always been the first to agree with anything Lyra said, had her mouth shut.

“Have you got a better idea?” said Lyra, with a slight edge.

“No. No, it’s not that.”

“What, then? Say it.”

Crabbe went pink. She was round-faced still, that year, with her hair in the two plaits her mother made her wear, and when she went pink it went all the way up into her parting.

“It’s only… his parents are dead. Isn’t it a bit sad?”

“Who?”

“H-Harry Potter.”

Lyra screwed up her face. So that was it. Crabbe was feeling sorry for the enemy.

*Defending* Harry Potter, to Lyra’s face?

She had heard somewhere that the most dangerous person on any side was the one who had begun to feel for the other. She ought to put a stop to it at once. But it was Crabbe; so she decided to try reasoning with her first.

“Don’t be absurd, Crabbe. What has that got to do with us?”

“But he said it as if it was nothing. Both of them, gone. He just said it. Isn’t that sad?”

“Oh, don’t be ridiculous. Not having parents. As if that were such a great matter.”

She had managed perfectly well, after all. She had been without them herself.

It had been when she was eight. Some business had come up, something urgent that nobody explained to her, and both her parents had had to go away at once and could not say when they would be back.

She had come down in the morning to the dining room, and there had been nobody at the long table but her, and the breakfast Dobby had made, laid out in front of one chair. The table had seemed very long that day. The chair was colder than usual. Even the food did not taste of much.

She had eaten a little of it, crossly, and gone to the library, and read, and gone out and flown about the grounds on her broom by herself. She had called Dobby and ordered him about for something to do. She had poked at the fire. The family portraits never said anything but dull, old-fashioned things, but that afternoon she had sat and talked to them for a whole hour, and had rather enjoyed it.

Draco had been too small to count. He had cried for their mother at bedtime and been put to bed by Dobby, and Lyra, who was the eldest, had not cried, because somebody had to be the eldest.

She had played chess against herself. She had had her bath, and gone to bed earlier than usual, and lain there with her eyes wide open in the dark.

Nobody knew this. Nobody would ever know it. She had thought, *What if they don’t come back tomorrow either*, and she had very nearly cried after all. Like a Malfoy, she had held it in.

They had come home just before dawn, as it turned out, with a box of sugared violets from her mother’s confectioner, all for her. Her mother had not taken off her travelling cloak, and her father’s wand hand was bandaged, and nobody mentioned either. Lyra let the violets carry off the whole of that bad day, and had not thought about it since.

*But what if*—

What if that day came every day?

It went through her like cold water. Just imagining it made her skin creep, and Lyra shook her head hard.

*Rubbish. If you hadn’t got parents, you’d have elves, and relations. What a fuss about nothing.*

She shook the whole picture out of her head. What mattered now was making Harry Potter sorry.

Until then, no weakness of any kind. None whatever.

“After Christmas, Potter,” said Lyra. “You’ll see.”

The arrow was already off the string.
