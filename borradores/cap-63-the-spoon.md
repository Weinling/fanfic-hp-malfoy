# Chapter 63: The Spoon

There were two rooms now that one did not go into, and a third that one knocked at.

Lyra learnt this in the first ten minutes. She came up the drive on the last Saturday of March in a thin cold rain. The peacocks had gone from the front lawn. Pobble took her cloak without looking at her, and told the floor, in a whisper, that Miss was to use the back stairs after dark. There was a new smell in the house. She could not have said what it was. It was not an unpleasant smell. It was dry and rather sweet, like the inside of an old trunk, and it was on the curtains, and it had got into the bread.

Her father kissed her forehead. It took him some time to cross the hall. Her mother came down in grey, with a small flat thing on a chain under her collar, and looked at her, and held her face for a moment in one hand, and told her that she was thin. Nobody mentioned Draco, who had gone straight up from the carriage to the room beside her mother’s and shut the door.

At dinner there was somebody in her mother’s chair.

“So this is the one,” said Bellatrix Lestrange.

She had her elbows on the table. She was eating a chop with her fingers, very fast, with her eyes going about the room. Azkaban had taken a good deal off her. The bones of her face stood out as they did on her sister’s, with a horrible likeness, as though somebody had taken a beautiful drawing and gone over it, hard, in black. She licked her thumb. She looked Lyra up and down, from the hair to the shoes, and her heavy lids came down, and she smiled. She had very few teeth left at the back.

“He told me. I made him tell it twice. You got there on the second try, and you did it to *Lucius*.” She laughed. It was a high, happy, breathless noise. At the side of the table her father went on cutting up his meat. “Oh, Cissy, why have you been hiding her? I thought she’d be all ribbons. Come here. Show me your hand.”

It was in the long gallery, the next morning, with the rain on the glass.

She had thought it would be the elf. She had lain awake on that. It was a peacock; somebody had brought it up from the terrace, in a sack, and it stood on the boards with its train dragging and one wing held out from its side, looking about it with its flat stupid eye. It was the white one. She had known it since it was an egg.

“You’ve got it all in the shoulder,” said Aunt Bella. She walked round Lyra, trailing one long dirty finger across her back. She smelt of cold stone and something burnt. “Everybody does. They think it’s like throwing something. It’s more like *pouring*. You have to mean it, and you did, I’ll give you that; but you were meaning it at the whole of him, all at once, like a child in a tantrum. Nobody’s taught you the wrist. It’s all in where you put it. Now. Find the place, and go in there, and stay.”

He was at the end of the gallery, in the window. She had not seen him come. He had a chair, and he was sitting with one thin leg across the other and something long and heavy lying over his feet. He was not looking at her. He had a book. He turned a page.

*Do it the first time.*

She found the place. It was quite easy to find. She had thought it would have gone, and she only had to reach for it, down the back stairs, and it was there: small, and hot, and exactly the size of her hand. She raised her wand.

It took no longer than a moment. It made very little noise. It was only that its feathers stood out, all of them, all at once, and stayed like that. She watched it. She kept her wrist where she had been told. She had always wondered what they thought about.

“*There!*” cried Aunt Bella. She clapped her hands. There were tears of pure delight in her eyes. She seized Lyra’s face and kissed her on the temple, with cracked dry lips. “There she is! Oh, you little *beauty!* You’ve got Mother’s hands. Do you hear me, Cissy’s child? It’s in the *wrist!*”

At the end of the gallery, a page turned.

“Thank you, Bella,” said the Dark Lord. He had not lifted his eyes. “Miss Malfoy, a word.”

She went down the length of the boards. It was a long way. She put the room in order as she walked. It was getting harder to find things for it; it had got rather bare. She needed something that would stand a good deal of handling, and she took down from the wall of herself the best of what was left: the summer she was six, and the lawn in the early morning, and her father’s hands holding the broom steady, and his voice saying *Look up, my treasure. Don’t look at the ground, look up.* She carried it in and set it in the sun.

He looked. He was in no hurry. He had his finger in the book, to keep the place. He came in, and wiped his boots, and walked about, and she stood aside. He picked the morning up, and looked under it.

“He sleeps badly, I understand,” said the Dark Lord. “Your friend.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“And it was moths.”

“He says so, my lord. I have thought it may be something else. I am trying to find out.”

“Do,” he said. “You keep such a very tidy house.” His red eyes rested on her, with mild enjoyment, and they moved a little to the left, and came back. “You will tell your father that I was pleased about the man in Battersea. It’s so seldom that anybody brings me what I ask for.”

That night she tried it. She had known she would. She lay in the dark and she went to the place, and there was the lawn. There was the dew on it. It had been early. His hands had been on the broom, she could have told anybody that; she knew what he had said. She looked at it. She stayed looking for rather a long time.

That was two.

Her grandfather had been moved up to the house.

They had put him in the old nursery at the top of the east stairs, because of the bars. It was the one that she knocked at. She went on the Wednesday, with a tray, because her mother’s hands were full, and because nobody had told her not to.

She would not have known him. He was in a high-backed chair by the window. They had tied his wrists to the arms of it, with strips of soft linen, quite neatly; it was the only way of stopping him from hurting himself, Pobble said. All the same, they went on going. There was a fine dry rustling from the sleeves. The marks had come back on his face, and joined up, and something had gone from the long chin. He had the chessboard in front of him on a stool. His eyes were as they had always been. They came round to her, pale and clear and very like her own, and looked at the tray.

“Ah,” said Abraxas Malfoy. It came out in pieces. “The c-clever one. Knight to … b5.”

She moved it for him. There was no one else to do it. She looked at the board and she took his bishop with her queen, and for a while they went on in that way, with the rain on the bars and the black pieces muttering, and it occurred to her that he was going to beat her in about nine moves.

She took up the bowl. It was soup. It seemed to be all that they gave him. She sat down on the stool, and put the napkin under his chin, and she dipped the spoon, and held it out. It was very odd to see that mouth open. A certain amount went down his neck.

She did not do it for love. She wanted that noted. She had seen what was lying on the table by his bed, with the lock hanging open, and she knew very well who had the keeping of it now, and that it was not going to be an eleven-year-old who would not come up the stairs.

“You’ve got g-good hands,” said her grandfather, when she wiped his chin. His eyes had not left her face. “Steady. I had those.”

“Yes, Grandfather.”

“Bode,” he said. “That was y-you.” The corner of his mouth went in, a quarter of an inch, and it shook. “Well done. Never … never go and look.”

She took the tray away.

The Malfoy Fund gave Easter eggs to the fourth floor of St Mungo’s every year. It had been her grandmother’s idea. Her father had asked particularly that it should go on as usual. He thought it would be as well for them to be seen.

So on Easter Monday, in a good grey coat, she followed her mother along a corridor that smelt of cabbage and antiseptic, with a hamper between herself and a junior Healer. A bright, plump witch with a bunch of paper daffodils pinned to her front walked ahead of them and talked without stopping. It was the Janus Thickey Ward. They didn’t get many visitors. It was so *good* of Mrs Malfoy. They had a lovely lot this year, and everybody was doing so well.

It was a long light room, with flowered curtains. There were pictures on the walls that somebody’s children had done. At the far end an old wizard in a dressing gown was explaining something to a door. A thin white-haired woman with a huge, vague, wandering pair of eyes came up the ward in her slippers. She stopped in front of Lyra, and studied her, with great attention. Then she took Lyra’s hand and turned it palm upwards, and she put something into it, and closed the fingers, and patted them.

“Oh, that’s Alice,” said the Healer. “Isn’t that nice? She’s given you a present. She does that, she only gives them to people she likes. Say thank you, dear.”

It was a sweet wrapper. It was a pink one, from a Droobles, smoothed out very flat. Lyra looked at it. She looked at the woman, who had already forgotten her and was going away, and she looked, because she could not stop herself, at her mother. Narcissa Malfoy was standing quite straight beside the hamper. She had lost a certain amount of colour. She had shared a nursery with the person who had done that, and she had passed her the salt on Saturday.

“Thank you,” said Lyra. She put the wrapper in her pocket. There was already a piece of paper in there.

“And this is our new gentleman,” said the Healer. She had stopped at the bed by the window. “We’ve only had him since the middle of March. We *are* getting on nicely, aren’t we? Yes, we are. He’s got his sister with him.”

There was a small grey woman on a hard chair, with some knitting in her lap. She was not knitting. She looked up, and got to her feet, and she made a funny little bob to Mrs Malfoy, and stood with her hands folded.

In the bed there was a man.

He had been propped up, and somebody had combed his hair. He was about her father’s height, she thought. He had a mild, square, ordinary face, a clerk’s face, with a bit of sticking plaster on the chin where they had shaved him. It was a very clean face. He was looking at the window. She waited for him to do something. His eyes were open. They were a sort of hazel; she had not thought about that. There was a little light on them, from the rain. They did not go to anything. He breathed in, and his chest went up. He breathed out. There was nothing behind his eyes at all, nothing whatever, not fear, not even tiredness. It was like looking through the front windows of a house, and seeing straight out at the back.

She had not known. She had heard the word all her life. She had thought it would look like something.

“It’s his dinnertime,” said the Healer. “We’re a bit behind, with the eggs. There now.” She had got a bowl from somewhere, with something pale in it, and a spoon. She looked round, beaming, with her daffodils; and she looked at the tall girl with the good grey coat, standing at the end of the bed with her hands in her pockets. Her plump kind face lit up. “Would you like to, dear? Miss Bode’s got her poor hands, and we’re so short today. It’s ever so easy. He takes it very nicely from the young ladies.”

She held it out.

There was a good deal of time. She could hear the old man down the ward. She could feel her mother, at her shoulder, not moving. It seemed to her there were a number of things a person might say. She had always been so good at it. She had a very nice voice for this kind of thing. She opened her mouth, to use it, and she found that it had nothing in it.

She took her right hand out of her pocket. She took the spoon.

It was only an ordinary spoon. It was warm. She went round the side of the bed. Somebody had moved the chair for her. She sat down. She dipped it in the bowl, and it was some sort of semolina, with a very little sugar, and she lifted it. Her hand was perfectly steady. *Good hands*, she thought. *I had those.*

She touched it to his lower lip.

His mouth opened.

It opened by itself, wide and soft, like a bird’s. His eyes went on looking at the window. She put the spoon in. His lips came together round it. She drew it out. She saw his throat move. He swallowed, and the mouth stayed shut for about as long as it would take to say a name; and then, without his turning his head, it came open again, and waited.

“There!” said the Healer. “Isn’t that good? What a good boy.”

“He was always fond of his food,” said the small grey woman.

She had a soft, flat, apologetic voice, with a little of south London in it. She had come up on the other side of the bed. She was looking at the spoon as one looks at a fire, and her eyes had gone red a long time ago, and stayed that way. She reached out, with her swollen knuckles, and she tucked in a corner of the sheet.

“I used to leave him a pie,” she said. “Tuesdays and Thursdays. I had my Circle. I keep thinking, if I hadn’t—” She stopped, and she looked up at Lyra, and she tried to smile. “It’s ever so good of you, miss. There’s not many would. He’d have liked you. He was a very polite man.”

Lyra dipped the spoon.

She did it eleven times. She kept the count. It was very important, for some reason, to have the number right. He ate it all. When the bowl was empty he went on opening his mouth for a little while, and shutting it, and then he stopped. She wiped his chin. She laid the spoon in the bowl, and she gave the bowl to the Healer. She said, in a perfectly pleasant voice, that it had been no trouble at all.

The lavatories on the fourth floor had green tiles.

There was a window at the end of them, with bars, and a brown stain on the ceiling in the shape of Ireland. She got the door shut. She got as far as the second cubicle. She went down on her knees on the tiles in her good grey coat and held on to the seat with both hands; and it all came up, the breakfast, and the toast, and the egg, and went on coming when there was nothing left. It seemed to be coming from the soles of her feet. She could hear the noise she was making. She had heard a hare make that noise. It went on, in long dry heaves, with her forehead against the cold enamel, until her ribs ached and the water ran out of her eyes and off the end of her nose.

After some time it stopped.

She stayed as she was. There was a tap dripping. She looked at the tiles. They were very clean, and there was a small crack going across one of them, like a river on a map.

She got up.

She pulled the chain. She went to the basin, and ran the cold water, and rinsed her mouth, twice, and spat. She washed her hands. She washed her right hand again. She dried it on the roller towel, and looked in the glass. It was her own face. There was a little colour wanting, and she pinched her cheeks, the left and then the right, as her mother did before the Ministry dinner. She took two pins out of her hair, and put them back. She looked at it once more, from the front. There was nothing on it anywhere.

Then she unlocked the door, and went back along the corridor to the ward, and helped them to finish handing out the eggs.

Nobody spoke, in the carriage. It had come on to rain harder. Her mother sat across from her, very upright, with her gloves in her lap, looking out at the wet brown streets. They had gone about a mile before she moved. She did not look round. She put out one hand across the space between the seats, and she took Lyra’s right one, and held it, tight, for about as long as it took to pass the end of a road. Her fingers were quite cold.

She let go. Lyra looked at the rain.

“Shut your eyes,” said Potter.

“I shall do nothing of the kind.”

“Go on. I’ve been working on it all week. Shut your eyes and hold out your hands.”

It was the Sunday after they came back, the fourteenth. It had stopped raining at last. There was a thin clean April sun going through the small square window for the first time that year; it lay across the bald rug in a long stripe, and it made the room look rather shabby, and rather pleased with itself. He was standing in the stripe, in his shirtsleeves, with a dishcloth over his shoulder. He had got ink on his ear. He looked as though somebody had plugged him in. He had been on the stairs, she thought, when she came up; he had been sitting at the top, listening for her.

She shut her eyes. She held out her hands.

Something was put into them. It was round, and heavy, and rather too hot. She looked. It was a cup of tea. It had been made with a great many leaves. There was a thing floating in it. It had the colour and very much the consistency of a pond in August.

“I learnt how,” said Potter. “Hermione showed me. I got the elves to give me a pot. You warm it first. A fortnight I’ve been doing that, I’ve been doing it in the dormitory, Ron thinks I’m going mad. I thought you must have had a filthy time. Fifteen days,” he added, with some feeling. “I counted. Don’t ever do that again.”

She looked at the cup. She looked at him. There was a very long way to go. She found that she could do it quite easily; she went up the stairs, and along, and she got to where he was, and she took hold of the corners of her mouth. It went up. It was remarkable how well it worked. Everything was exactly where she had left it.

“It’s the worst thing I have ever seen,” said Lyra.

He was so happy that it hurt to look at him. He laughed out loud, and came and dropped into the chair, and it took the two of them, with its long sigh of horsehair. He wanted to hear all of it. Had it been foul? Had she seen Draco? Was she eating? She told him about the rain. She told him how many times her aunt had laughed at dinner, and she did the voice. She drank some of the tea. It was perfectly true, what they said; one could get used to almost anything. He had got her feet up on the fender; he was telling her something about Weasley and a broom, with his hands going. The sun had reached the timetable. It had got warm. There was something in the pocket of her robes, two things, one rather stiff and the other very thin. She laughed in the right place.

He stopped.

He had turned his head and was looking at her, from a few inches off, with his eyes screwed up against the light. She held quite still. It was a thing she was good at. He looked at her for a long moment, with his crooked, tender, idiotic smile; and he lifted his hand, and she watched it come.

“You’ve got something on your chin,” said Potter.

He wiped it off with his thumb.

“There,” he said.

She smiled at him.
