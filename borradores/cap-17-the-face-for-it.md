# Chapter 17: The Face for It

Her father was pouring port when she knocked.

“Come,” he said, and then, seeing who it was, he set the decanter down and smiled the smile he never wore outside the house. “Lyra. My treasure. I thought you’d gone up.”

The study was the darkest room in the Manor and, in winter, the warmest. Pobble had built the fire up until the four generations of Malfoys in the panelling looked faintly flushed beneath their disapproval, and the curtains were drawn against the snow on the lawns. Lyra sat down across the desk from him with her back very straight and folded her hands in her lap.

She had been home six days. She had spent most of them working out how to begin.

Put in order, it was simple enough. The thing in the drawer had never been a diary. It had been a piece of a man, one of seven; the Dark Lord was only waiting, and when he came back for it he would learn that Father had dropped it into a little girl’s cauldron to spite Arthur Weasley. If Father knew, he might take fright and go to Dumbledore of his own accord, and then at least one Malfoy would be on the side that meant to win. Potter had made it very clear that it was not going to be her. She had been ready to help him—not help: extend him credit, against the diary—and he had declined to open the account.

“You’re very solemn,” said her father. “Is it a boy?”

“Father.”

“I only ask. Your mother says you’ve been sitting on the window seat a great deal.” He pushed the dish of sugared almonds across the desk to her, the one he kept there for her and pretended was for guests. “There. Don’t tell her.”

She took one and did not eat it.

“May I ask you something? It’s silly.”

“Those are my favourite.”

“If he came back.” She looked at the almond in her fingers, not at him. “The Dark Lord. If he came back and asked you what had become of the thing he left with you, what would you tell him?”

Up in the panelling a great-uncle cleared his throat, as portraits will when a silence goes on too long.

Her father laughed. It was a fine laugh, warm and easy, and a fraction too loud for the room, and while he was laughing he picked up the decanter again and poured himself a second glass he had not meant to have. The neck of the decanter chattered once against the rim. He set it down at once, and smiled at her, and laid his hand flat on the desk beside it. In October, in the rain, she had told Potter that her father was perfectly calm.

“He is not coming back, my treasure,” he said. “Whatever they’re telling you at that school. Dumbledore likes his ghost stories. They keep the children in at night.”

“Of course,” said Lyra. “I said it was silly.”

In July she had asked him very nearly the same thing, and he had laughed then too, and meant it, and had not needed a second glass. So that was that. Father had been given the most precious thing the Dark Lord owned and had thrown it away on a whim to annoy a man he despised; this autumn he had written *us* to her as though it were a family crest; and the one thing in the world that frightened him was the one thing that should. You did not give a secret like hers to a man like that. One day somebody would look at him in the right way and it would fall out. And if the Malfoys, who had sneered at Dumbledore for twelve years, were suddenly seen in his corner, everybody would want to know why; and people who changed sides were always the first to be dealt with.

She put the almond down.

“Only,” she said, as though it had just come to her, “if anybody ever did ask—anybody at all—I suppose the simplest thing would be the raids.”

Her father’s eyebrows went up.

“The summer before last,” said Lyra. “When Mr Weasley’s people were going through half the cellars in Wiltshire. They took all sorts of things from all sorts of houses, didn’t they, and never gave anybody a list. If something had gone missing that year, nobody could ever prove it hadn’t gone with them.” She examined a fingernail. “I’m only saying. It would be so tiresome for you to have to think of something on the spot.”

Her father looked at her as if she were a letter that had come in a hand he did not recognise. Then he sat back in his chair, and his face did the slow, delighted thing it did when an investment came good.

“The Ministry,” he said. “Arthur Weasley’s little crusade.” He turned his glass by the stem. “Do you know, that is very nearly elegant.”

It had been Potter’s idea, by the lake in June. She had only changed whose people did the breaking in, and she did not see why he should have the credit.

“It isn’t anything. It was only a silly question.”

“Silly questions,” said her father, “are my favourite,” and he raised his glass to her. She watched him take it and tuck it away for later, as he tucked away everything that might be useful one day, without the faintest idea that he needed it.

On the train in September Potter had asked whether she had talked to her father, and she had said, *Extensively*. Well, she had now. It had taken four minutes. He had not heard a word of it that mattered, and at the end of it he kissed her forehead and told her to go to bed, and she went.

Draco came in on Christmas morning without knocking, which was the first thing wrong with it, and carrying a broom, which was the second.

“Lyra! *Lyra!* Look!” He held it over his head like a trophy. He was still in his pyjamas, and so pleased with himself he was practically giving off light. “It’s a Cleansweep Ten. It’s not a *Nimbus*, I said that, but Father says I can have a proper one when I go to Hogwarts, and this one’s for practice till then. I’m going to practise *all the time*.”

Lyra put a hand over her eyes.

“Draco. Out. Knock. Come in again.”

“I did knock,” said Draco, who had not. “Anyway, it’s nearly eleven. Mother says you’ve been asleep so long you’ll have grown roots.”

She sat up, slowly, and pushed her hair back off her face. Draco took a step back towards the door.

“You horrible little creature,” said Lyra pleasantly. “Come here and say that.”

“You can’t do anything. You haven’t got your wand out. And you’re not *allowed*, not at home, it’s—”

“Who said anything about a wand?” She swung her feet out of bed.

He fought like a cornered cat. It made no difference: she had four years and most of a head on him, and she knew exactly where he was ticklish, because she had been finding out since he was in a cot. She got him down on the rug, and he shrieked and kicked and laughed until he could not breathe, and the Cleansweep rolled away under the bed with nobody left to defend it.

When she let him go he lay on his back on the rug, scarlet and streaming.

“When I’m at Hogwarts,” he panted, “I’m going to find out all your secrets. And then I’ll *tell* everybody.”

“That’s two years away. I shall have much better ones by then.”

“I’ll find those out too.”

“I should like to see you try.”

Draco stuck out his tongue. Then he sat up, put a hand in his pyjama pocket, took out a pack of cards and shuffled them.

He did not shuffle them as a boy of nine shuffles cards. The pack ran from one hand to the other in a long rippling bridge and closed with a soft, final clack; he cut it one-handed, fanned it, and squared it again, and never once looked down at his hands while he did it.

“He says I’ve got the hands for it now,” said Draco, very pleased. “As well as the face.”

Lyra sat down on the end of the bed.

“The gentleman in the east cottage?”

“He’s *ever* so good. He can make the queen of hearts come out of your ear. Well, out of the pack, but you’d swear it was your ear.” Draco squared the cards. “Somebody sent me a letter in the autumn. It said not to play cards with him. It didn’t have a name on it or anything.” He looked up at her, scornful. “I think it was Pobble. Pobble thinks cards are wicked.”

Lyra, who had disguised her handwriting for that letter with enormous care, said nothing.

“I didn’t stop, anyway,” said Draco. “He’s *nice*.”

He had not written to her since October, not once, not even to boast. Lyra did not ask him why. She was afraid he would tell her.

Draco bounced to his feet. “Mother says you’re to come down and do presents. She’s got you writing paper again, I saw.” At the door he turned round, to make sure of the last word. “And don’t do your hair. It takes *hours* and it never looks any different.”

“Draco Lucius Malfoy—”

He was gone, shrieking with laughter, along the corridor towards the stairs.

Lyra was smiling. She sat on the end of the bed and listened to him go, all the way to the landing, and she went on smiling until the very last of it. Then the house was quiet again, and there was nothing left to smile at.

Writing paper. *Write on Sundays*, her mother had said in September, and Lyra had, about nothing whatsoever, and had still not answered the postscript.

Her brother was nine, and he did not know anything. He did not know what had been in Father’s drawer, or what Potter had said about him in the Shrieking Shack. He ought not to know. That was what nine was for. Whatever else happened, she was going to see to it that nobody took that smile off him; and Potter had told her, as plainly as anyone could, how he wanted her to go about it.

“Fine,” said Lyra, to the empty room. “Then I shan’t interfere.”

Let him do it alone, if he was so clever. Let him and Granger and Dumbledore save the world between them, and see how far they got.

And if they failed—if the Dark Lord came back after all—she would not wait to be sent for. She knew how that went: the ones who hung back were the ones who had to explain themselves afterwards. The Malfoys would be at his feet before the others had found their masks, and she would be at the front of them, on her knees, and she would make herself so useful, for so long, that one lost diary would be a thing he could afford to overlook. Whatever that took. Even if it meant turning on Potter.

She sat there a little longer, making quite sure she meant it. Then she got up and went down to do presents.

The first double Potions of term was on the Thursday, and until the very end of it Lyra did not look at Potter once.

It was not difficult. He sat with Weasley on the Gryffindor side, near the door, and she sat on the Slytherin side by the shelves, with Crabbe on her left and an empty stool on her right, and she kept her eyes on her own cauldron and her own neat pile of ginger root, and she knew exactly where he was the whole time. You could not stop knowing where somebody was by not looking at them. You only knew it harder.

“Potter.”

Snape had stopped at the Gryffindor benches. The room went quiet in the particular way it went quiet whenever Snape stopped anywhere.

“I asked,” said Snape, “for a Wit-Sharpening Potion.”

“Yes, sir.”

“This is not a Wit-Sharpening Potion. This”—he did not touch the cauldron; he only looked at it—“is bright yellow, smells of peppermint, and appears designed to make the drinker cheerful. What, precisely, have you made?”

“It sharpened yours, sir,” said Potter. “You knew what it was just by looking.”

Most of the Gryffindors laughed outright. A good few of the Slytherins could not help themselves either. All of them stopped the instant Snape turned his head. Lyra did not laugh. It was a nearer thing than she would have liked, and she put it down to the peppermint.

When Snape spoke again his voice had gone very soft, which in Snape was the same as shouting.

“Ten points from Gryffindor for the potion, Potter, and ten for the wit. You have until the bell to make what I asked for. If you have not, you may come back and make it every evening until you can.”

“Shame,” said Potter cheerfully, ladling the yellow stuff into a beaker and holding it up to the room. “It’s for Monday mornings. Two sips and you stop minding what anyone thinks of you. Anybody?”

On Lyra’s left, Crabbe’s hand went up, with a sidelong look at Lyra that made it quite plain who she wanted the beaker for.

Goyle pulled it down again by the sleeve without a word, and gave her a look, and then gave Lyra a quick look as well, to see whether she had noticed. Lyra had not noticed. She weighed her ginger root against the book a second time, to the grain, and did not hear a thing.

When Snape came round to her bench, he looked into her cauldron for a long time.

“Faultless, Miss Malfoy,” he said.

He went on looking at her afterwards, though, as he had since November: as though she had borrowed something of his and he was waiting, with great patience, to see what she brought back. Lyra had nothing to bring back. Whatever Snape hoped she would find out about Professor Lupin, she had found out in November, and kept, and was keeping still.

She asked to go early, bottled a sample and went. Potter was at the end of the bench by the door, and she had to pass him to get out. She had made up her mind not to look at him, and she looked anyway.

He was in the middle of a knot of Gryffindors: Weasley, and Thomas, and Finnigan, and Lavender Brown leaning over from the next bench. They were all talking at once and passing the beaker round under the bench, and Finnigan was doing Snape’s voice. Potter sat in the middle of it, stirring a cauldron that was slowly going from yellow to the correct dull brown, and he was the only one of them who was not smiling.

He looked up as she came level with him, and for a second she thought he was going to say something more than her name.

“Malfoy.”

“Potter.”

She had told him not to smile in front of her. She had said nothing about everybody else.

She went out into the corridor.

“He was watching you,” said Crabbe, falling into step at her elbow. “The whole lesson.”

“He was watching the clock.”

Crabbe said nothing more about it. She never did, and it was the most irritating thing about her.

In Charms that afternoon they did Cheering Charms. Crabbe, who was good at charms when she forgot to be nervous of them, put one on Goyle at the first attempt, and Goyle went round the room for ten minutes telling people she loved them, the window included. Then Crabbe turned to Lyra, with the face of somebody who had been waiting all day for exactly this, and did it again.

Lyra felt a faint fizz behind her ears, like the first sip of something with bubbles in it. That was all.

Crabbe went pink and put her hand up. Professor Flitwick came bustling over, climbed on to a chair and cast it himself, with a little flourish at the end. Lyra felt the fizz again, slightly stronger, and then nothing.

“Well!” squeaked Professor Flitwick, rather higher than usual. “Well, well! That does happen, now and then—a witch with very strong natural defences of the mind—nothing at all to worry about, Miss Malfoy!”

Lyra thought of Snape’s office, and the small cold tick at the back of her head, like a fingernail against a wineglass, and Snape saying *You have a natural—* and stopping.

After that, of course, half the class had to have a go, and Lyra bore it with great dignity until Parkinson came back for seconds.

“Panther,” she said, “if you point that thing at me once more, I shall snap it in two and send you the pieces by owl.”

Parkinson went away.

Crabbe and Goyle tried for the rest of the afternoon without wands: Goyle with a Chocoball she had been saving since Christmas, Crabbe with the news that Millicent had fallen asleep in History of Magic and been marked present by a ghost. Neither of them was any good at it, which helped more than either of them could have known.

Dinner was already going badly at the Gryffindor end by the time Lyra sat down.

“—*ate* him, Hermione! That great ginger *thing* ate him!”

“Ron—”

“You saw the sheets, Harry! Blood all over them, and ginger hairs, and he’s *gone*. That cat’s been after him since September—” Weasley made a horrible chomping gesture.

“He’s not dead, Ron,” said Potter.

“You don’t *know* that!”

Lyra had put the whole width of the Slytherin table between herself and the Gryffindors, and the back of her head besides. It made no difference whatever. Weasley had a voice that carried through stone.

Down the Slytherin table, heads were turning.

“Weasley’s rat’s gone.”

“Gone? It was about a hundred. It probably just lay down.”

Lyra could not find it in her to mind. Rats at the Manor did not get names. They got Pobble, with a broom.

“Go on, then, stick up for the cat.”

“I’m not sticking up for anybody. It’s a rat, Ron. It was old when Percy had it.”

“He was *family*!”

“Say that a bit louder,” said Potter. “I don’t think Fred and George heard.”

“Oh, that’s *funny*, is it? Fine. Go off with Hermione, then, the pair of you, seeing as you’re getting nowhere with Malfoy—”

Lyra’s fork stopped halfway to her mouth, and stayed there.

For a moment the Slytherin end was quiet too.

“Potter and Granger had a table to themselves in the library this afternoon,” somebody said. “Very cosy.”

“Since when?”

“Since whatever it was with—”

“Lyra, was there a—”

Lyra looked down the table. Daphne Greengrass, who had been speaking, found something of enormous interest in her mashed potato and stayed there.

Lyra cut her chicken. *Getting nowhere with Malfoy.* And now it was Granger, was it? Granger, who wasn’t counting. Well. The three of them had lived in one another’s pockets since first year; it was hardly surprising if two of them had finally noticed. It was nothing to do with her. Potter had made quite sure of that.

“He won’t even *look* at the evidence,” Granger was saying at the Gryffindor end, in the voice of somebody who has said it several times already. “There isn’t a scrap of it. Harry at least is being sensible—”

“Hermione,” said Potter. “I’m not on your side because I think Crookshanks is innocent.”

It meant something. Granger understood what, and Lyra did not; and on either side of her Crabbe and Goyle went very quiet, and stole a glance at her, and then another at her plate.

She had cut her chicken into small, exact squares, as though somebody were going to mark them. She went on cutting.

Lyra put down her fork. It made rather more noise than a fork should. Then she got up and walked out of the Hall, not quickly, and her way to the doors took her behind the Gryffindor bench.

“Hermione,” Potter was saying, low, “I need to borrow it again.”

“Harry, it isn’t a toy—”

“We need the time.”

She did not slow down.

Behind her, as she went, she heard somebody say, “Well, *go* on, then,” and Goyle answer, with feeling, “We went last time. *And* the time before,” and Crabbe say, “Your turn, Panther,” and Parkinson, for once in his life, say nothing.

She was halfway across the Entrance Hall when she saw them.

Potter and Granger, at the top of the first flight of the marble staircase, going up. Potter had half of Granger’s books under his arm, as he had carried them the day term ended, and the two of them turned at the landing and went on up together and out of sight.

Lyra stopped.

Then she ran: up the stairs two at a time, round the turn and up the second flight. The corridor at the top was empty in both directions.

She stood there getting her breath back.

Thirty seconds ago they had been behind her, at the Gryffindor table, with their plates still full. Nobody could have got from there to here without passing her, and she had been in the way the whole time.

Then she remembered. *It isn’t a toy, Harry.* Granger slipping into Arithmancy on the bell, out of breath, a minute after Lyra had heard her footsteps going off the other way. A timetable that had come tumbling out of a split bag in December, with Arithmancy and Muggle Studies both at nine o’clock on a Monday morning, in Granger’s neat hand, as if that were a perfectly reasonable thing for a timetable to say.

Lyra looked at that for a moment. Then she put it away, where she had promised Granger it would stay. There was an obvious explanation for all of it: she was going mad, and it was Potter’s fault.

She went back down.

On the last landing she stopped in the shadow of a pillar and looked down into the Hall; and out of the Great Hall came Potter and Granger, exactly as if they had just got up from dinner, which by every law Lyra knew, they had. Potter was still chewing. They were walking side by side towards the east corridor and talking in low voices.

They passed almost underneath her and never looked up.

“—it’s meant for *lessons*, Harry, that’s the whole—”

“Says the girl who used it to finish a Runes essay.”

They went on. She had actually turned towards the dungeon stairs when she saw where they were going.

The east corridor went one way only, past two disused classrooms, to the girls’ lavatory at the end—not the haunted one upstairs; the other one, down here—that nobody had used since a troll smashed it to pieces in first year.

She followed them.

She stopped at the corner. They had gone on into the dead end, and she could not go any further without being seen; and the thought of Potter turning round and finding Lyra Malfoy creeping after him like a jealous scullery maid was so mortifying that she nearly went back to the dungeons there and then. But if she went back now she would spend the night inventing what had been behind that door, and everything she invented would be worse.

She looked about for a reason, and found one lying ready, as reasons generally were. It was a girls’ lavatory. She was a girl. There was no rule in the castle, and no law in the country, that could stop a girl walking into one whenever she liked, and if Potter happened to be in it with a Mudblood that was his lookout, not hers.

She walked to the end of the corridor without hurrying, arranging her face for whatever was behind the door, and threw it open.

There was nobody there.

Every cubicle door stood open. A tap dripped into a cracked basin. The troll had left a long dent in the far wall that nobody had ever mended, and the mirror above the basins gave her back a girl with her mouth open, standing in an empty lavatory.
