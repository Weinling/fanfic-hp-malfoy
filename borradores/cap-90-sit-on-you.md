# Chapter 96: Sit on You

He told Ron at ten to seven on the Friday morning, in the dormitory, because Professor McGonagall had said before breakfast and he couldn’t think of a way to make that any later.

He had been down to the dungeons first. At six o’clock, with the castle dark and the snow still coming, he had let himself into the Potions store and gone to the third shelf on the left, and taken one small brown shrivelled thing out of a jar, and not written it in the book. It had come out of the stomach of a goat. He had got one down somebody on a hearthrug once, in another life, with his thumb, and had not thought about it since; and on Sunday afternoon there had not been one within four floors of a girl with flour on her hands. He had it in his left pocket now, and kept his fingers round it.

Ron was sitting on the edge of his bed, lacing a boot. He’d got a practice. He looked up, and saw who it was, and his face did what it had been doing for a fortnight, which was to wait and see.

“I’m off the team,” said Harry.

“You’re what?”

“For good. McGonagall. Last night.”

“It’s Ravenclaw in *February!* You can’t be off the— I’ve got Demelza on the left and a Beater who can’t count, I’ve built the whole—” The boot had stopped. He had gone from pink to red in the time it took to say it, and now, as he looked at Harry properly, he began to go the other way. “What did you do?”

He had lain awake working out how you said this. Madam Pomfrey’s way turned out to be the only one there was.

“I cursed Malfoy. Yesterday. She’s in the hospital wing.” He put two fingers under his own left ear, and drew them down, and out along the arm. He had done that for a boy of twelve the night before, and it had not got any shorter. “From there to there.”

Ron did not say anything at all. He sat with the lace wound round his fist. His eyes had gone to Harry’s cuffs; they were clean ones, and it made no difference, people seemed to know where to look.

“Like Lestrange,” he said at last.

“Yeah.”

“On *her*. On— She’s your—” He couldn’t find the word. Nobody had ever been able to find it. “Did she go for you first?”

“She’d stopped.”

In the silence Seamus turned over, behind his curtains, and said something about a goalkeeper.

Harry watched it arrive. He had known this face for five years and three months, in two lives, and he had seen most things happen to it. He’d seen it across a chessboard, and under a Sorting Hat, and at a hospital bedside. He had never seen it look at him as it was looking now, and he found that he had been expecting to for some time.

“Right,” said Ron. His voice had gone rather high. He bent down and finished the boot, and pulled the knot tight, and tighter than that. “Right. So I’ve got no Seeker.”

“Ginny.”

“Don’t you tell me who I’ve—” He stopped. He stood up. He was a good four inches the taller, these days, and he looked down from the whole of it. “She’ll be better than you,” said Ron. “She’s wanted it since she was nine. She used to break into the broom shed.”

“I know.”

“No, you don’t. You never asked.”

He took his broom from the end of the bed and went out. But at lunch, when Harry came in late and sat down at the very end of the table, by the doors, there was somebody already there, a foot away, eating a pie as if he had a grudge against it.

“It’s Friday,” said Ron, to the wall. “I’m on the list. Don’t read anything into it.”

There was no potato.

The mustard waistcoat was still on the back of its chair.

Nobody had moved it since August. It had gathered a fine grey bloom along the shoulders, and the loose button had at some point come off and been put in a saucer on the dresser; and the dog with the torn ear, which had got the use of its leg back and none of its sense, still lay down underneath it every night at seven and sighed. They had come down to Grimmauld Place on the Saturday afternoon by the Knight Bus. Lupin had carried his own bag. He had not said one word between Hogsmeade and Islington that was not about the weather.

He cooked. That was as before. What had changed was that he no longer did it alone.

“The half-breed is burning the onions of the House of Black,” said Kreacher.

He was standing on a stool at the end of the range, in a clean pillowcase, with his arms folded. He had come out of the boiler cupboard some time in October, Lupin said, at the smell of a white sauce, and stood in the doorway for an hour wringing his hands; and he had been at that stove ever since, on a footstool of his own, in a condition of unbroken horror. He never touched the spoon. He would not have dreamt of it. He only watched.

“They’re meant to be brown, Kreacher.”

“They are *black*. Kreacher’s Mistress had onions like amber. Kreacher’s Mistress would have had the skin off a servant for that pan.” The bloodshot eyes swivelled. “And the brat sits at Master Sirius’s table in his outdoor shoes, saying nothing, and it is Kreacher who will be scouring it.”

“Thank you, Kreacher,” said Lupin, “that’s very helpful,” and put in a good deal too much salt.

So it went for four days. They ate what there was. The dog had the rest, and in the evenings Lupin sat by the range with a pile of third-year essays on Grindylows, in the same jumper, and Harry sat across from him with a book that he wasn’t reading and a hand in his left pocket. He changed it over in the mornings; on Tuesdays and Thursdays it went in the right. He could not have told you what that was for.

It was Christmas Eve when Lupin put down his pen.

“Minerva wrote to me,” he said.

Harry had known that she would have. He had been waiting since the bus.

“I’m not going to ask you about it. She has, and Albus has, and from what I gather you told them the truth, which I’ll admit I hadn’t expected.” The tired, kind, intelligent eyes rested on him for a moment, and moved to the waistcoat. “I’m going to tell you about your father. I ought to have done it years ago. Sirius would never have it; he said you’d had enough taken off you already.”

The range ticked. On his stool, Kreacher had gone very still, as elves do who wish it to be understood that they are not listening.

“You know about the lake, I expect. Somebody will have told you.”

“Yeah.”

“They always do tell that one. He was fifteen, and he turned a boy upside down in front of half the school because he was bored and the boy was there. And then people say that he grew out of it, and that’s true as well; by our last year he’d stopped hexing anybody for the fun of the thing. It’s what he grew into that nobody mentions.” He had picked up the pen again, and was looking at the nib. “He became a person who knew what was best for you. He was extraordinarily good at it. He decided when I was thirteen that I oughtn’t to be alone at the full moon, and three boys broke the law every month for three years because James Potter had decided. It was the kindest thing that anyone has ever done for me. Somebody might very easily have been killed. Both of those are so.”

He said it without any heat, as he might have set out the properties of a Kappa.

“He decided who should keep a secret. At the end he decided that your mother should take you and run, and that he’d hold the stairs, with his wand on a sofa in the other room. He didn’t ask her. There wasn’t time; and I don’t suppose it would have entered his head if there had been. He was very nearly always right, you see. That was what made it so hard to say anything.”

“Did you?”

“No,” said Lupin. “That’s the part I wanted you to have. They made me a prefect in my fifth year. I’ve always thought it was in the hope that I would say something. I sat under a beech tree with a book, and when it got loud I turned the page.”

He laid the pen down, quite straight.

“I have sat at this table since August and watched you become somebody who knows what’s best for people, and I’ve stirred the gravy. There was a man put on a hillside in Scotland with forty-one sheep. He told me about it going down the stairs at the Ministry, at a run. He thought it was the funniest thing that had ever happened to him; he would have forgiven you a great deal worse. I notice that the people who love you generally do. I shouldn’t lean on it.” He looked up. “So I’m saying something. It is twenty years late, and it’s to the wrong Potter. I’m sorry for that.”

Harry had his hand shut on a small hard thing in his pocket.

He looked for something to say back, and there was nothing. He had been told a number of true things since Thursday, by people who had been keeping them for him, and he was beginning to know the feeling: as though a door had been opened at the far end of a room that he’d thought was a cupboard.

“That wasn’t what Thursday was,” he said. “I wasn’t deciding what was good for her.”

“No. I know what Thursday was. You’ve got Albus for that, God help you.” Lupin rubbed his eyes. “I’m not talking about your arm. I’m talking about the rest of you.”

The dog sighed, under the chair. After a while Harry found that he had started to speak, and that he could not have said when he’d made up his mind to.

“Can I ask you something? It’s a stupid question.”

“They’re the only ones worth marking.”

“If I had to go and die for something.” He kept his eyes on the saucer with the button in it. “Not in a fight. If it was the only way a thing could be done, and I had to walk somewhere and let it happen. And you knew. Would you come with me? As far as you could. Would you walk along?”

Lupin did not answer at once. He had gone rather grey.

“No,” he said.

“You did.”

He had said it before he could stop, and heard it land on the table between them, among the Grindylows.

“I mean— I had a dream. A while ago. You were all there. You and Sirius, and my mum and dad. I’d got something in my hand, and you came out of the trees and walked next to me, the four of you, the whole way. Nobody tried to stop me. My dad said I was nearly there. You said—” He had to wait for a moment. “You said it was quicker than falling asleep. And you were all so proud.”

He had not told that to anybody. He had not known that it was a thing one could mind. That had been the best quarter of an hour of the other life; he had kept it, and gone back to it; and it was only lately, and for no reason that he could find, that he’d started to turn it over and look at the underside.

Lupin sat with his patched elbows on the table. He studied Harry for a long time, and whatever he saw there, he didn’t ask about it. He had been a teacher for too many years.

“Then I was dead,” he said.

“What?”

“In your dream. I must have been. That’s a thing the dead say, Harry. They’ve nothing to lose by it. They’re on the other side of the door already, holding it open; of course they’re proud of you, you’re coming to them.” His voice was quite gentle and quite rusty. “I’m not dead. I’m in a kitchen. And if you were to get up from that chair tonight and tell me that you had to go and let something happen to you, I shouldn’t walk you anywhere. I’d sit on you.”

Harry stared at him.

“I mean it literally. I’m not a heavy man, but I’d do my best. I’d get Kreacher to hold your feet.” On the stool, a pair of bat’s ears twitched. “I’d be wrong, possibly. I daresay it would turn out to have been necessary, and half of England would have something to say to me afterwards. I’d still sit on you. That’s the whole difference between us and them.”

Something had come up into Harry’s throat. It felt rather like a laugh and rather like the other thing, and he did not seem to be able to get it either up or down. *Somebody ought not to be here*, something said, a long way off, in a voice he very nearly knew; there was a smell of leaves; and it was gone before he could turn his head.

“Kreacher would not touch the brat’s feet,” said Kreacher, to the range, “for a gold Galleon.”

The laugh won, as it turned out, and came out through his nose.

“How is she?” said Lupin, later.

They had got as far as the washing-up. Kreacher did it. They were permitted to stand by with cloths.

“She’s got a scar that’s never coming off. She’s gone home with it.” He dried a fork. “She won’t let me— I tried to give her something. A promise. She read it through and told me what was wrong with it. She wants me to teach her the counter-curse instead.”

“Which is what?”

“You sing it.”

Lupin took the fork from him and looked at it, and put it in the wrong drawer.

“Can you sing?”

“No.”

“No. I’ve heard you in the bath.” He stood for a moment with the cloth over his shoulder; and then, with the air of a man who has been in debt to a particular young woman for three years and has just seen a way of paying a shilling in the pound, he went across the kitchen and took down from the top of the dresser a flat black case with a handle. It had a gramophone in it. It had belonged, by the dust, to the bad master, and it was full of records in paper sleeves with motorbikes on them. “It isn’t your ear. I’ve heard worse ears. It’s your breath. You sing like a person who expects to be interrupted. Stand up straight. Feet apart. No, take your hand out of your pocket, whatever you’ve got in there will keep.”

“You can’t stop to breathe. That’s the whole—”

“Then you take enough in to begin with. My mother had me in a chapel choir in Aberystwyth till I was eleven. Down here, not up there. Give me your note.”

Harry gave him his note.

It was a quarter to midnight on Christmas Eve, in the basement kitchen of the Noble and Most Ancient House of Black. The dog got up from under the waistcoat. It put its head back, with a look of the purest fellow-feeling, and joined in.

“*Master is making the noise of the bad master’s motorbike*,” said Kreacher, in tones of despair, with both hands over his ears; and Lupin leant against the dresser, with his grey face gone a good ten years younger, and wept with laughter into a tea towel.

They did it every night after that. By the time they went north again he could hold three notes and a fourth, in one breath, for the length of the kitchen table.

She came by the fire, as she had in August, at twenty past eleven on the twenty-eighth.

He was alone. Lupin had gone up. She stepped out over the fender in the Muggle anorak with her hair tied back, and put a hand down for the dog without looking, and stood there with the green going out behind her.

“Mum thinks I’m in bed,” said Ginny. “I’m your Seeker. Ron told me on the train. I’ve wanted that since I was nine, and I’ve got it because you cut somebody open. I thought I’d come and say thanks.”

She did not sit down.

“I know who it was. My hour.” She took a folded sheet out of her pocket, and did not unfold it. “I didn’t burn that one. I’ve stopped burning them. I wrote down everything I had, the same night, before I’d slept, and I’ve been through it with a pencil every week since. There’s the back of a head with its hair coming down. There’s a voice telling me I’ve dropped my glove. It says *Weasley*. And somebody’s put my collar straight. I didn’t have that at first; it came back on its own, about a fortnight ago, in the bath.” Her eyes were very direct, and there were shadows under them the colour of slate. “Nobody does my collar. Mum does. And there’s one other person in that school who can’t see a crooked collar without putting her hands on it. I’ve watched her do it to you in the Entrance Hall since I was thirteen.”

The range ticked. He could hear the dog’s tail going on the flags.

“Hermione said there were four people who could do a Memory Charm like that. She wouldn’t say who. She didn’t have to.” The paper went back into the pocket. “I’m only asking you one thing this time. Did you know?”

He had had a great many answers ready for a great many people, over the years. He appeared to have run through them.

“Yes,” said Harry.

“Since when?”

“The cupboard. When you said about her hair.”

“You stood in that cupboard and told my brother it wasn’t back.”

“It wasn’t.”

“No. It was your girlfriend.” It came out flat and quite quiet. “And you went off and had a row with her about something else, I expect, and I went back to my dormitory and slept with the light on for five weeks. Then that’s two of you. You for the book, and her for the hour. I shall have to start a second column.”

He took it, and it went in and stayed where it was. He could not have got out from under one word of it if he had wanted to; and he noticed, with something like surprise, that he did not much want to.

“Do you know why she did it?”

“Yes.”

“Are you going to tell me?”

Here it was. He could feel the old thing get up and stretch. He could have told her. He had it all: an arm, and a grey line, and a thing that heard. She was owed it, if anybody had ever been owed anything. He’d only have to open his mouth, and decide, over the top of a girl in Wiltshire with her arm in a sling, that it was best. It would have been the kind thing. He was very nearly always right.

*Nothing moves without asking.* He had thought that she meant chess pieces.

“No,” said Harry. “It’s not mine. It’s hers to tell.”

“Can’t, or won’t?”

“Won’t.”

Ginny considered him. He had the feeling that she was reading it twice, once for the sense and once for what had been left out.

“Then I’ll get it from her.”

“She can’t tell you either.” He stopped. He picked the next three words one at a time, and laid them down, and knew while he did it that he was deciding again. “Not out loud.”

Nothing moved in her face. But she had been a year in a bathroom on the second floor, with a friend whom nobody else could hear; and he watched it reach her, and go in, and be put somewhere safe, the way that a Chaser takes a ball out of the air without appearing to have looked at it.

“Not out loud,” said Ginny.

She turned back to the fire. She took a pinch of powder from the pot on the shelf, and at the last moment she glanced over her shoulder, at his left side, where he had a hand in his pocket.

“I’ve got a pencil,” she said. “Tell her I shall be bringing it.”

There were nine of them.

He counted from the Gryffindor table, on the evening of the fifth, with the candles lit and the snow still on the sills. The Slytherin second-years sat about two-thirds of the way down their bench, in a row, by height. There had been ten of them in December. He went along it again from the other end, in case; and there was a place between a boy called Baddock and a girl with plaits, about as wide as a clean grey suit, that nobody had closed up.

He had known. He had stood by the hourglasses and said so, in three words, to a child.

Neville had the night. He was beside him, with the pot, cutting up a chop into pieces of equal size, and he had seen Harry counting and had counted too. He put his knife down. He didn’t say anything. His jaw had begun to work.

She came in at a quarter past six.

He had been watching the dungeon stair. She came by the great doors instead, from the direction of Professor Snape’s study, with her cloak over her arm; and the noise in the Hall did something that he had only heard it do twice before, once for a troll and once for a dead owl. It went out from the doors in a ring.

She had on a grey dress that he had never seen, cut square at the neck and low on the shoulder, as a woman wears a thing to show her pearls. She hadn’t any pearls. She had her hair up, every pin of it, well away from the left side; and it ran down from under her ear, in the candlelight, white and raised and as thick as a bootlace, over the collarbone and out of sight along the arm. The arm lay in a sling of black silk, and she had made no attempt to hide that either. She walked the whole length of the Great Hall with her chin at the angle where it had been since she was eleven, in front of six hundred people, and she did not hurry, and she looked at nobody.

*I shall wear what I like. People will look at it, and you’ll be one of them.*

He looked. It was the arrangement. He made himself do it properly, from the top to as far as the dress went, and he did not let himself off any of it; and Professor McGonagall, at the High Table, with her fork in the air, was looking not at the scar but at him.

She did not go to her place. She stopped halfway, level with the Gryffindors, and turned her head by an inch.

“Longbottom,” said Lyra.

Neville dropped his knife.

“I’ve a message for you from my brother. He’s not coming back this term; my family has other arrangements for him.” It carried, in the pleasant voice, to the ends of both tables. “He says that you were going to let him count the Mandrakes again in the spring, and that he expects there will still be forty. Unless one has died. He says you’re to look behind the pots.”

Nobody at either table was eating. Neville had gone a slow dull red, and his eyes were bright, and he looked at her as he had looked along a table in November, at somebody buttering toast.

“I will,” said Neville. His voice cracked. “Tell him— I’ll look. Tell him they’re all right.”

“I don’t expect to be writing,” said Lyra.

She inclined her head, as far as it would go. She walked on. And as she drew level with the end of the bench, something small and pale slid from the cloak on her arm and dropped on to the flags, a yard from Harry’s foot.

“You’ve dropped your glove, Malfoy,” said somebody.

Ginny was three places up, with a bit of treacle tart on her fork, and she had not raised her voice; and for the space of one step the straight grey back seemed to check.

Harry picked it up. Kid, for the right hand, with something folded very small inside the thumb.

*He never heard the room,* it said, in the best hand in Slytherin. *Not once. The rat was never in. He had only what was written down and thrown away. Go through your pockets. D. is kept at home to pour the tea.*

He read it under the Cloak, on the seventh floor, by the end of his wand. He read it four times. Then he burnt it, and he stood in the dark by the tapestry of the trolls with his heart going, and looked at a blank stretch of wall.

He had not walked past that wall and asked for anything since September. He had gone in because it was a Monday, or a Thursday, and the door had been there, and it had given him what was true. It had given him two chairs.

He walked past it now. He did it three times, as you were supposed to, with his hands out of his pockets, and he thought of a bishop, and of a girl lying on her stomach with her feet in the air, and a spoilt sheet of paper coming at his head.

The door was where it had always been. He opened it.

There was the bald rug, and the fender, and a good fire. *Some Lesser Houses of Wessex* was on the bookshelf, between a Potions primer and something about the Goblin Wars, where he supposed it must once have lived. And in front of the hearth, standing level on all four of its feet, with its stuffing put back, was one armchair. Somebody had mended the leg. Whoever did it had used a different wood, a shade too light, and made no attempt to hide the join.

He looked for the other. He looked behind the door.

She came at ten. He heard the wall open. She stood on the threshold, in the square-necked grey, with her book of rounds in the crook of her good arm; she looked at the chair, and at the place nine feet away from it where there wasn’t anything; and then she looked at him. He had read that face for five years, and he had been shut out of it since June. He was not let in now. It was only that somebody had come to an upstairs window, and stood there, with the lamp behind her.

It lay along her arm with its eye open. Neither of them could say one word.

“You’ve let the fire get low, Potter,” said Lyra, for whoever might be listening. “I suppose it was too much to hope that you’d learn.”

She crossed the rug and sat down in the chair. She did it as one sits in a thing that has been returned from the menders: carefully, in the middle, and then all at once. He heard her breath go out. He had been going to stay where he was, on the far side of the hearth, with his hands where she could see them. He found that he was on the floor by the arm of it, with his back against the horsehair, and that nobody had told him to move.

She opened the book. She began to read, in the nice clear carrying voice, something about the Persians and a bridge of boats.

About a page in, she put down her right hand, and took hold of his. She turned it over. She had the right one, with the white letters across the back, and she held it palm upward on the arm of the chair; and with the first finger of the other, the one that came out of the sling, slowly, in capitals, so that he had to spell it out, she began to write.

*B.* *I.* *S.*

He had it at the *H*.

It came up through him like something in a pipe. He got his teeth shut on it; he bent over with his forehead against the chair, and his shoulders went, and a noise got out of his nose that would not have deceived a first-year.

“Ten points from Gryffindor,” said Lyra pleasantly, “for breathing,” and went on with the Hellespont.

When he could, he took her hand in his turn. Cold and narrow, it lay perfectly still. He wrote on it with his finger, as she had. He got as far as *S*, and *O*, and *R*.

Her hand closed. It shut on his finger at the third letter, quite hard, and stayed shut; and she kept it there, in her lap, with the grey line running down to it, and read him the whole of the crossing into Europe without once looking up.
