# Year One, Part 4: Two Words

A few evenings after Harry Potter went up the Headmaster’s stairs to complain about him, Severus Snape went up them himself, and did not sit down.

The Headmaster had pointed him to a chair with the air of a man who had been expecting him for an hour and had kept the tea warm, and Snape walked past the chair and the tea and stopped in front of the desk.

“What,” he said, “did you tell the boy?”

“That you owe James and Lily Potter a debt you can never repay,” said Dumbledore, “and that until you have paid it, you will keep their son alive.” He folded his hands. “I thought it was time somebody told him something true, Severus. He came all the way up here on his own, you know, to complain about you. He did not know the password. He stood at the bottom and guessed sweets until the gargoyle gave in. It took him eleven.”

“He is eleven. He complains about everything.”

“He looked me in the eye and told me that you are unfair. Not very elegantly. But I could not find anything in it to correct.”

Snape did not trouble to deny it.

“And the rest?” he said at last. “Did you tell him the rest?”

“No.” Dumbledore regarded him over his spectacles. “Whatever else Harry knows, Severus, he did not have it from me.”

“He knows it,” said Snape. “He said as much to me. In a corridor, after dinner.”

There was a silence. One of the silver instruments on the shelf behind the desk let out a thin puff of smoke and was still.

“You may be as disagreeable to the children as you please,” said Dumbledore. “That is your affair, and I have long since stopped hoping otherwise. But it is not to cost us the Stone. Watch it as you have been watching it.”

“I have not been careless.”

“The boy hates you, Severus. He will get under your feet at every turn, and he is a wizard, however small. I should not like you to be looking at him when you ought to be looking somewhere else.” He paused. “And I know whom you suspect. Keep it to yourself until you can prove it. To anybody.”

He ought to have bowed and gone. He did not.

“The boy has changed,” he said. “His manner. All of it. In September he was merely insolent. Now he says the Dark Lord’s name across my classroom as though he were reading it off a list, and he looks at me—” He stopped. “When I look at him, Headmaster, I think of the Dark Lord. I do not know how else to put it.”

He had expected the Headmaster to be troubled. Instead the old man looked interested, and then, unforgivably, amused.

“You need not worry about Harry,” said Dumbledore. “He has something I lack, and that you, once, had a great deal of.”

“Don’t.”

“Love,” said Dumbledore, quite unrepentant. “It sent him up my stairs this week.”

Snape had heard this sermon before, and would hear it again. He went out without a word.

“It is *Potter*,” said Lyra Malfoy. “It is not a dragon. It is not even a *large* boy.”

Nobody answered. Crabbe and Goyle, on the bench beside her in the corridor outside the library, studied their shoes, which was an answer of a kind.

It had been three weeks since the Potions lesson. In three weeks, Slytherin House had learned to leave Harry Potter the whole width of a corridor.

It had not been one thing. It had been a great many small ones, and the trouble with each of them was that nobody could prove it. Two third-years had cornered him by the greenhouses to teach him some manners, and had both spent the night in the hospital wing, and neither of them would say what had happened; Potter, who had a split lip, said he had walked into a door. A fourth-year who had tripped Weasley on the stairs was found the next morning with his head jammed in a lavatory in the dungeons, and Potter, asked about it in front of the whole Great Hall, looked so honestly puzzled that a Hufflepuff laughed out loud. Whatever you did to Potter, or to anybody near him, came back. It came back worse, and it came back without a single mark on it that a teacher could pick up and look at.

And through all of it he went about the castle with half the school at his elbow, laughing, as though it were all enormous fun.

So the Slytherins had stopped. They went round him. They found things to look at in corridors when he passed. And since the whole of Slytherin had gone quiet, nobody was much frightened of Lyra any more either. She could take out her wand in a corridor now and people simply went on walking. Second-years had stopped stepping aside for her. And the worst of it, the part she would never forgive anybody for, was the game.

“*Look out—Potter!*” somebody called from the far end of the corridor, a Ravenclaw by the sound of it, and three or four voices laughed.

Lyra did not turn round. Goyle swung all the way round on the bench, and so, if Lyra was honest, did Lyra’s eyes.

There was nothing behind them but a suit of armour.

“You jumped,” said Goyle, under her breath.

“I did not jump.”

“Your shoulders did.”

“My shoulders were *checking the corridor*. One checks a corridor. It is called being sensible.”

“It looked like jumping.”

“Well, it *wasn’t!*”

Because the name had started doing it to her too. In Slytherin they said *Potter* now very much the way grown-ups at her father’s dinners said the other name: quietly, and only when they had to.

“Malfoy,” said Crabbe, in the careful voice she kept for things she thought might be unwelcome, “couldn’t we just keep out of his way? Like everybody else?” She twisted the end of one plait round her finger. “My mum says you don’t argue with a wasp. You just go into another room.”

“Crabbe! Whose side are you *on?*”

“Yours! I only meant—”

“He is a boy with a *scar*,” said Lyra. “Somebody tried to kill him when he was too small to notice, and failed, and everybody’s been clapping ever since. I do not go into other rooms for people who are famous for *not being dead*.”

She believed it, too. She very nearly believed it.

At the start of the year it had been different. At the flying lesson she had come off her broom at the top of an oak, which was not a thing she cared to think about, and Potter had thrown himself after her, and she had not minded nearly as much as she ought to have. She had been brought up to be looked at, and she knew the difference between being looked at and being *watched*, and she knew what his face had been doing that day, coming down out of the sky after her with his hand out and his whole face open, as though she were the only thing up there worth catching. She had been falling off a broom in front of the entire year, and she had still been the whole point of the afternoon.

Now, on the rare occasions he looked at her, his eyes went through her like something thin and cold. Then he breathed out once through his nose, short, the way her father did over a wine that had turned out to be a disappointment, and looked away.

It was not a way she was used to being looked at. It was not, she was quite sure, a way anybody had the *right* to look at her.

“I am not frightened of him,” said Lyra.

Goyle said nothing. Crabbe said nothing, more loudly.

“I am *not*—”

And then Neville Longbottom came out of the library with an armful of books and went past them towards the stairs, walking in the anxious, hurried way he always walked, as if he expected the floor to give way.

Lyra watched him go. It had been her plan since before Christmas, after all, and she had simply not got round to it: leave Potter alone, the one person stupid enough to say the name, and go for the people round him. Potter might be awkward. Longbottom was what Longbottom had always been.

She took out her wand.

“Professor Flitwick says practice makes perfect,” she said. “Who remembers the Leg-Locker Curse?”

Neville came into the Gryffindor common room through the portrait hole with his legs stuck together, and fell flat on his face on the hearthrug.

He had hopped. He had evidently hopped all the way from the library, up seven floors, with his books clutched to his chest and his knees locked, and half the common room was on its feet laughing before anybody had worked out it was not a joke. Hermione was the first to stop. She went across the room at a run, knelt beside him, and said the counter-curse, and his legs sprang apart, and he got up slowly, shaking.

“Blimey, Neville,” said Ron. “There’s easier ways up the stairs.”

Harry did not say anything.

“Malfoy,” said Neville miserably. He sat down on the arm of the nearest chair; his knees did not trust themselves yet. “Outside the library. She said she wanted to see if she could still do it.”

“Go to Professor McGonagall!” said Hermione. “Tell her!”

Neville shook his head. “It’ll only make it worse.”

“You can’t just let her, Neville!” said Ron. “You’ve got to hit back sometimes, or they never stop. She walks all over people, and you lie down so she doesn’t have to step so high—”

Harry got up.

He did not say anything at first. He took out his wand, and turned it over once in his fingers, and something in the way he did it made the Gryffindors nearest to him go quiet, and look at each other, and then look back at him with a sort of nervous, hopeful interest, as at a firework somebody has just lit.

“Outside the library,” said Harry. “That’s what you said, Neville?”

“Yes… why?”

“Well, it’s no good practising a jinx on somebody who can’t jinx you back.” Harry closed his hand on the wand. “She won’t learn anything. Somebody ought to give her a proper go.”

“But there’s three of them,” said Neville. “There’s always three of them.”

“Then she’ll have help.”

“Back in a bit,” said Harry to the room, and went out through the portrait hole.

He had got as far as the first staircase when he heard somebody scrambling after him.

“Harry, *don’t*.” Hermione had caught up with him, out of breath, her hair coming down. “You can’t do magic in the corridors. It’s against the rules.”

Harry tipped his head back towards the portrait hole, behind which Neville was presumably still sitting on the arm of a chair, trying to get his legs to stop shaking. Hermione followed the look, and pressed her lips together, and changed her ground.

“She’ll tell. She’s clever, Harry, she’ll go straight to a teacher and make it look like it was all you.”

“Let her.”

“She’ll go to *Snape!* You don’t know what he’ll do!”

“Snape.” Harry’s face went cold, as though somebody had opened a window behind it. “I’d quite like a detention with Snape. Just him and me, in the dungeons, late, with nobody else about. I’d like to see what he does.”

“*Harry!*”

Hermione’s mouth fell open. She looked up and down the empty staircase, in case somebody had heard, and then took hold of his shoulder.

“You’re being *strange*. You’ve been strange for weeks.”

“Am I?” Harry spread his arms. “Everybody jumps out of their skin if you say Voldemort. Nobody jumps at the people who used to work for him. One of them marks our homework. Tell me which bit of that *isn’t* strange.”

“But everything you *do* is dangerous! You know somebody wants to—Harry, somebody might really try to kill you!”

“I know,” said Harry. “I keep hoping they’ll get on with it.” He smiled at her as he said it, and did not seem to know what his face was doing. “Then I’d know who’s who.”

Hermione let go of his shoulder.

She looked at him the way she had looked at the troll in the girls’ lavatory at Hallowe’en, which was not a way she had ever looked at Harry before. He pulled away from her and went on down the stairs.

“Oi! Harry! *Wait!*”

Ron came clattering down after them both, red in the face and grinning all over it, having climbed out of the portrait hole behind Hermione and come down two steps at a time.

“You’re not having all the fun,” he said. “I want to see Crabbe hop.”

He fell in beside Harry, still grinning, and started describing at great length what Crabbe would look like going up seven flights with her ankles stuck together, and Harry laughed. It was his ordinary laugh. The face he had worn a minute ago on the stairs might never have been there.

Behind them, after a moment, Harry heard Hermione make a small, furious noise on the landing, and come after them.

They were still outside the library, the three of them, on the bench under the window, and they had seen him coming from the end of the corridor.

“Looking for me, Malfoy?”

Harry smiled as he said it. It was a good smile, and he knew it was; he had been practising since the summer, on the instructions of somebody who had told him in a robe shop to smile like he meant it. He meant this one. He meant it the way you mean a punch.

“Leg-Locker,” he said. “On *Neville*. Honestly? My cousin used to do that sort of thing when he was seven, and he didn’t even have a wand. I thought you’d come up with something a bit better than that.”

He watched it land. In a few words he had turned her into a girl who jinxed people’s legs in corridors because she could not think of anything cleverer, and she knew it, and so did the people beginning to drift over from the library doors to see what was happening.

Malfoy’s mouth went thin.

“Potter,” she said.

“That’s me.” He said it loudly, for the corridor. “Was there something you wanted to tell me? In front of everybody? Only you’ve gone a bit pink.”

“In your *dreams*, Potter.”

She put up a hand and swept her hair back over her shoulder.

It was a very small thing to do. Harry, who had spent five months watching her across classrooms when he was supposed to be doing something else, knew exactly what it was for. The hair went back, pale and bright, and for a moment every eye in the corridor went with it, and he saw her feel them go, and stand up straighter because of it. Chin up. Shoulders back. She had done the same thing to him once, with her own two hands.

“You want to be careful, Potter,” said Malfoy.

“Of what?”

“Of *everything*.” There were twenty people round them now, and more coming, and she raised her voice to reach the back of them. “You fight with older students. You answer back in Potions. You haven’t any respect for anybody. If you carry on like this, things are going to start happening. To you, and to everybody round you.” Her hand had gone to her wand. “Longbottom was the polite version.”

Harry took a step towards her.

All three of them flinched, as neatly as if they had rehearsed it. Malfoy caught herself at once and turned it into something else, a toss of the head, but he had seen it, and so had the people at the front.

“Hang on,” he said, and he did look confused, honestly and openly, like a boy asking for help with his homework. “*I* fight with older students? You mean the two third-years? Two of them, behind the greenhouses, waiting for a first-year? That’s the story you want to tell everybody? If I were them I’d be having my breakfast in the kitchens.”

Somebody at the back laughed. Then several people did.

“And respect.” Harry turned so that he was talking to all of them. “Go on, then. Which bit wasn’t true? That Snape used to be one of *his*? Ask anybody’s dad. That he takes points off you for breathing, unless you’re in Slytherin? Ask anybody.”

This time the whole corridor laughed: the Gryffindors, and the Hufflepuffs who had been on their way to the library, and a knot of Ravenclaws by the window with their books held against their chests. Everybody but the Slytherins.

It had taken him less than a minute. Crabbe and Goyle had both gone quiet, and Malfoy was biting her lip.

“Stop it! Both of you!” Hermione had pushed her way to the front. She looked at the crowd and then at Harry, and pulled at his sleeve. “Harry, that isn’t why we came. We came about *Neville!*”

“Speak for yourself,” muttered Ron, and stopped when Hermione looked at him.

“You’re going too far as well, Harry, you know you are. But *you*—” she rounded on Malfoy, “—you can’t just go round jinxing people because you feel like it! You owe Neville an apology!”

Harry knew it was a mistake a second before it happened. Malfoy had just been laughed at by half the school. She was not going to apologise to anybody for anything, and least of all because a girl with bushy hair had stepped out of the crowd and told her to.

“Who asked *you?*” Malfoy’s voice went high and shrill. “Go back to your Muggles, you filthy little *Mudblood*!”

The corridor changed.

Harry felt it before he understood it. He did not know the word; nobody at Privet Drive had ever needed one like it. But he could feel what it was from every face round him. The laughter stopped. Hermione went white. Ron, who five minutes earlier had been grinning about Crabbe’s legs, stepped in front of her with his fists clenched and his ears scarlet, and even some of the Ravenclaws by the window had gone pale with anger.

So it was that sort of word.

Harry moved first.

“*Locomotor Mortis!*”

Crabbe’s legs snapped together and she went down sideways, and her wand skittered away across the flagstones. Goyle had hers out a moment later. Something hit Harry across the knuckles like a ruler, and his wand went spinning out of his fingers and away under the bench.

Ron went for Goyle. There was a great deal of flailing; Goyle shoved him so hard he went rolling across the floor, but he had got hold of her wand on the way, and by the time the two of them were on their feet again both wands had been flung down the corridor in opposite directions, and Ron and Goyle were glaring at each other with nothing in their hands.

Which left Malfoy.

Harry walked towards her. She had her wand up, and her hand was shaking.

“*Flipendo!*”

It hit him on the side of the head. The corridor tipped. He took one step to the side to stop himself falling and kept going, and was on her before she could say anything else. He got her wand out of her fingers and threw it as far as he could, and took hold of her wrist, and put her back against the wall.

She hit the stone with a thump. “*Ow!*”

“Take that back,” said Harry.

“Let—go of me!”

“You’re frightened of his *name*,” said Harry, “and you’ll say *that*? You want something to be scared of, be scared of that!”

“I’ll say what I *like!*” She was frightened; he could see it all over her. She said it anyway, straight into his face. “*Mudblood!*”

“*Voldemort!*”

People flinched all round them, as if he had thrown something. Something was running down into Harry’s left eye, warm and wet. The jinx had split his eyebrow open. Half of the corridor had gone red, and he did not care; he could still see her.

“He came for me when I was one,” said Harry, “and he couldn’t do it. A *baby*, Malfoy. He lost to a baby, and then he ran off and hid. And you can’t even say his name?” He leaned closer. “That isn’t scared. That’s dressing up. You want to know what scared actually is—”

Her face crumpled.

He watched it go. She had been holding it all together the whole time, the hair and the voice and the chin, and now it went, and she burst into tears: not neatly, not like a girl in a book, but like a child, with her mouth open and her eyes screwed shut and great hiccupping sobs that rang off the stone.

It went straight through him. The anger ran out of him, and the pounding in his head went with it.

He looked down at his own hand. It was still round her wrist, and there were red marks coming up on her skin where his fingers had been. That would hurt. He had not thought of that. He let go of her as fast as if she had burnt him, and took a step back.

“What—what are you *crying* for?”

“Stop it,” sobbed Malfoy. She did not seem to know she was talking. “I hate it, I hate both of them—Vol-Voldemort, and that—that word—I hate all of it—”

Harry stared at her. She had said the name. He did not think she knew.

“—and *you*, I hate *you*—”

It was the most babyish thing he had ever heard anybody say, and it knocked the breath out of him in a way the jinx had not. He actually stepped back from it, as though she had pushed him.

Behind him, Crabbe got her legs apart. It had only been a first-year’s jinx, and it had given out. She came across the flagstones like a small round cannonball and hit Harry square in the chest with both hands.

“*Get off her!*”

He went over backwards. He was not expecting it, and he had nothing left to stop himself with; he sat down hard on the floor with his head ringing. By the time he had sorted out which way was up, Crabbe had Malfoy by one arm and Goyle had her by the other, and the three of them were going away down the corridor as fast as they could go, with Malfoy still sobbing between them.

Afterwards, the corridor went very quiet.

Harry lay on the flagstones and looked at the ceiling. By rights the corridor ought to have been cheering. The Slytherins had run; that was how these things were scored. Nobody cheered. It turned out that nobody, from any House, could watch a girl cry like that and feel they had been on the winning side, and somehow, without anybody saying a word, the whole corridor had decided that the person in the wrong was the one lying on the floor. People looked at him with uncomfortable faces, and then looked away, and drifted off in twos and threes towards their own common rooms.

Ron came and crouched beside him. “You all right? Sorry, mate. Crabbe was mine. I lost her.”

“Doesn’t matter.” Harry waved a hand at him. It was better this way, really. If she had stood there crying any longer he did not know what he would have done.

“*What* is going on here?”

Professor McGonagall came round the corner at a speed that did not seem possible in a woman her age, and stopped dead at the sight of Harry on the floor with blood all down one side of his face.

“Potter! You’re *bleeding!*”

“It’s nothing, Professor,” said Harry. He found he did not feel like being funny.

McGonagall’s nostrils went thin and white.

“Fighting. *Again*. I warned you, Potter, in so many words. Five months, and I have had more trouble out of you than out of the Weasley twins in their first year, which I should not have thought possible. Twenty points, and a detention, and you may thank your stars it is not worse—”

“Why’s it only m—”

“Twenty points from every student involved in this, Mr Weasley and Miss Granger *included*, and detentions all round! Hospital wing, Potter, this minute.” She turned on a tall Hufflepuff who had been edging away towards the stairs. “Mr Diggory. Who was Potter fighting?”

So Malfoy would get a detention too. Harry was glad, and ashamed of how glad.

He got himself up off the floor. His head had gone cold and clear, now that all the heat had gone out of it, and things were coming back to him in order. He could not quite remember, now, how he had got from Neville on the hearthrug to Malfoy against the wall. He remembered the word, and the corridor going quiet, and the name in his own mouth. Mostly, though, he remembered her face.

He had decided, in October, that he was going to change her. He had put his chin up on a broom and told himself so.

“I was going to change her,” Harry said, to nobody. “I made her cry.”

Madam Pomfrey mended his eyebrow in about a minute, with a great deal of tutting, and in the morning there was nothing to show where it had been. That was the last anybody saw of it. The Gryffindors did not tell the story, because it was not the sort of story that made Harry look good. The Slytherins did not tell it, because it was not the sort that made Malfoy look good either. Nobody repeated what he had said about Snape. Snape himself, the next time Harry came into the dungeon, did not so much as raise his eyes, and Slytherin took its lead from him. By Easter nobody spoke of any of it.

Crabbe came up to the dormitory after lights out, when the others were asleep, and sat down on the end of Lyra’s bed in the dark.

Lyra had the hangings drawn. She did not open them.

“I didn’t cry,” she said.

There was a pause on the other side of the curtain. Crabbe, who was the worst liar in Slytherin, and possibly in the castle, took a long time over it.

“No,” she said at last. “You didn’t.”

She went on sitting there. After a while Lyra put her hand out between the hangings, without meaning anything by it, and Crabbe took hold of it and held on, and neither of them said another word until Lyra was asleep.

She did not say the word again that year. The next time was the following autumn, on a Quidditch pitch, about some brooms, and that time it was Weasley who went for his wand. She went on flinching at the other one, the name, as everybody she had grown up with flinched at it. But there was a particular face that she flinched from more: a boy’s face, white and set, with blood running into one eye and the Dark Lord’s name in its mouth.

She was very good, by the summer, at not thinking about it, or about the cold weeks before it. She was so good at it that, a year and a half later, when she watched that cold face turn on her father at the foot of the marble staircase, she thought she was seeing it for the first time; and when, that same September, a mild man in patched robes sat in a shabby armchair and asked her whether Harry Potter had ever given her a really bad fright, it did not so much as occur to her that there might be an answer.
