# Chapter 56: He Will Look

He came on foot, up the drive, at seven o’clock, and he wiped his boots.

They had been standing in the hall since half past six. Her father was at the front, in the dark green, with the cane under his arm. Her mother was in grey. Draco stood between them in his bow tie with his hair wetted flat, and he kept looking at Lyra, and she kept not looking back, because she had found that if she looked at him she began to go over the room again. Her grandfather had been brought up from the cottage and given a chair by the stairs. Nobody had told any of them to stand. Nobody had needed to.

The great doors opened by themselves. It had started to snow again, and it came in across the marble in a long thin sigh, and there was somebody on the step.

She had thought she was ready. She had been told about the face, and she had the room behind her eyes all swept and lit. She had not expected him to be so tall. He stood on her father’s doormat with his back to the snow, very thin, in a plain black travelling cloak with a little white on the shoulders, and he looked down at his boots, and he scraped them, first the left, then the right, with care. He handed his cloak to Pobble.

“Thank you, Pobble,” said the Dark Lord.

It was a light, clear, courteous voice. She had not expected that either. Pobble made a very small sound, and folded up on the marble with the cloak on top of her, and nobody looked down.

“Narcissa. You will forgive the boots. I have walked from the gates; I had forgotten what a pleasant avenue it is.” He had taken her mother’s hand, and bent over it. His own was long and white and seemed to have too many joints. “Lucius, you look well. You mustn’t stand, Abraxas, at your age. And this will be Draco.”

He stopped in front of her brother. There was the face, then, under the lamp: the flat white place where a nose should have been, and the two slits, and the red eyes looking down at a boy of eleven with an expression of kind and genuine interest.

“I hear you had five points from Professor Snape,” he said, “for a potion that did not explode.”

Draco’s mouth opened. A small dry noise came out of it, and then, because he had been very well brought up, “Yes, sir. My lord.”

“Well done. It’s harder than it looks.”

He straightened, and he turned his head, and it was her turn.

“Miss Malfoy,” said the Dark Lord. “I have looked forward to this.”

There were eight places laid and six people to sit at them. He took her father’s chair. He did not ask, and it was not done with any emphasis; he walked up the long room, talking to her mother about the frost, and he drew it out and sat down in it, as one sits down at home. Her father stood for a moment with his hand on the back of nothing. Then he went round and sat at the side, at his own table, in the place where one put a cousin.

There was soup. It came, and it was set down in front of each of them, and in front of the Dark Lord nothing was set down at all.

“Please,” he said. “I don’t, any longer. But I do so enjoy watching a family at table.”

They ate. She had not known that one could hear so many spoons. Across from her Draco lifted his with both hands and got it to his mouth, and swallowed, and looked to see if that had been right. Beyond him, at the foot of the table, her grandfather took his soup in slow steady spoonfuls, not spilling any, with his long chin lifted.

Something touched her ankle.

It was heavy, and dry, and quite cool, and it moved along the outside of her foot with no hurry whatever, and went on moving for some time. It was as thick as her arm. She felt the whole length of it go by, under the cloth, and she saw it reach Draco, because his spoon stopped.

“Eat your soup, darling,” said her mother.

He ate his soup.

The Dark Lord had put his hand inside his robes. He took out a piece of old velvet curtain, and unfolded it, and set what was in it down on the white cloth, between the candlesticks, with a small click.

Nobody at the table looked at it.

“I want to tell you a story,” he said. “It’s rather a dull one, and I hope you will all stop me if I go wrong. I have had a most instructive week. Your grandfather, Draco, came to me on Wednesday, in a brave and proper manner, to show me this, which I had left at your school when I was young. You found it. I’m obliged to you. Look at it, Miss Malfoy, if you would. I am told you do well in Arithmancy. What do you see?”

She put down her spoon. She looked. She had the face ready, the one for Snape’s office, and she made it a little slower and a little stupider than it was.

“It’s been broken, my lord.”

“It has. What with?”

“I don’t know, my lord.”

“No,” he agreed. “Why should you? There’s only one thing that burns like that, and nobody has had the use of it for a thousand years. I had some myself once, at your age, but I left it where it lay.” He laid one long finger on the black place in the silver, and stroked it, as one strokes a cat. “It was venom. From a basilisk, under the school, which is dead. And in the whole of the wide world, as I understand it, only one boy has come up from there with a fang in his hand.”

The candles burned straight up. Under the table something turned over.

“So I asked myself about this boy, in the snow, on my way here. He killed my basilisk. He has gone up to the seventh floor, and he’s found the one thing that nobody could have found, and he has walked out and said nothing to a soul. How did he know it was there?” He seemed to find this a pleasant thing to wonder about. “And then I thought about a book. I left that with a friend, for safekeeping. The friend came to see me this summer, with his hair so nicely brushed, and he told me it was very sad, but the Ministry took it. It went in the raids, the summer that Weasley’s people went through the cellars. I remember how sorry he was. I remember thinking he told it beautifully.”

He lifted his eyes from the silver. He did not look at anybody in particular.

“Did they go through the cellars, Lucius?”

She had sat opposite her father in his study at Christmas, two years ago, and handed that to him like a present. *I suppose the simplest thing would be the raids.* She had watched his face light up. She could see his hands now, flat on either side of the plate. She saw him look at them, and she saw him think.

He looked at her.

It was the smallest thing. It was not as long as a breath. He looked at her as he had looked at her across all the breakfasts of her life, to see if she had got the joke; and then he pushed back his chair, and stood up, and came round it, and went down on both knees on the carpet.

“There were no raids, my lord,” said Lucius Malfoy. His voice was quite clear. “That was my invention, and mine alone. I put the book in the Weasley child’s cauldron, in Flourish and Blotts, in the summer of 1992, to make a scandal for her father. I did not know what it was. It was destroyed. I was afraid to tell you. Nobody else in this house has ever known anything about it.”

She had not known that he could do that. She sat with her hands in her lap. She found she had stopped breathing, and that she must not, and she began again.

The Dark Lord looked down at the fair bent head, with his chin on his hand.

“You do lie beautifully, Lucius,” he said. “It runs in the family, I think.”

And he turned his head, and looked at her, and came in.

It was not like Snape.

She had been ready for a cold hand in a dark drawer. There was no hand. There was no pressure at all, and no sound; only that she found there was somebody in the room with her, standing just inside the door, who had wiped his boots. *You will feel him come in*, Snape had said, *and you will shut the door, beautifully, in his face.* Everything in her wanted to. It went up her spine like a shout. She stood aside, and held the door, and let him pass.

He looked round. He was in no hurry.

There was the breakfast table, with the light coming in sideways. He paused at it. He picked up a dress, and turned back the hem, and laid it down. A boy on a train, and her own voice saying something mean, polished up to shine. She had given that a great deal of thought. He looked at it. She kept herself very small and very proud in the corner, a clever spoilt frightened girl who loved her father, and who was so flattered to be noticed that she could hardly stand; and it was true, every word, and she held it up in front of her with both hands and did not look at any of the walls.

He came to the stool.

He stopped there. She felt him stop. He bent down, and put out one long white finger, and touched it. She felt the Hat lift. She felt him pick up the whole of it, the little feet, and the thirty seconds, and the table going up in a roar; he lifted it out of the place where she had set it, and turned it to the light, and looked underneath. He handled it thoroughly. It was what she had brought it for. She stood in her corner and watched him do it.

He put it down, a little to the left of where it had been.

The candles came back. She was sitting at a table with soup in front of her. Nothing had moved; her father had not lifted his head; a skin had not yet formed on the top of her plate. There was sweat running down between her shoulder blades, and she kept her hands exactly where they were.

“You keep a very tidy house, Miss Malfoy,” said the Dark Lord.

She did not know what that meant. She thought she would go on not knowing for some time.

He had got up. He walked down the length of the table, behind Draco’s chair, trailing his fingers along the backs, and he stopped at the end of it, by her grandfather.

“Abraxas,” he said. “You have been so well.”

The old man laid his spoon in his plate, and looked up. She had seen that face go white on Christmas night. It did not do so now. It looked at what was standing over it with the pale steady interest with which it had looked at her across a teacup; he had come back up that path on the Friday knowing exactly what this was going to be, and he had had himself carried here to meet it, and he did not lift his hands from the cloth.

“I had you on credit for fifty-two years, Tom,” said Abraxas Malfoy.

Her father made a sound. Nobody else in the room seemed to be breathing.

“You did,” said the Dark Lord gently. “It’s a very old joke, isn’t it? And I paid you. I paid you in June, in front of everybody, and I saw you understand it. Do you know, I have missed being in your debt. I find it so much more comfortable for us both when one of us owes.”

He bent down, and he laid his palm against the pale pitted cheek, as one might with a child who has a fever.

He took it away.

It was not very much to see. For a moment the old man sat as he had been sitting. Then the spoon began to chatter in the plate. It was a small bright noise, like teeth, and it went on, and it went on getting faster; his hands had started to go on the cloth, both of them, and he looked down at them, and he tried to take hold of the one with the other, and that went too. The soup had got on to his cuff. The marks on his cheeks had darkened, one after another, as if something had been poured in under the skin. He got his right hand as far as the handle of the spoon. It knocked over the salt.

“Put that down in your book,” said the Dark Lord, and he took a napkin and wiped his palm.

“Grandfather?” said Draco.

It was hardly a voice at all. Nobody answered. Her mother had laid her hand on the top of his head. It lay there quite flat, and it did not move.

“Well,” said the Dark Lord. “We were talking about your father.”

He had come back up the room. He stood looking down, with his hands behind him, at the man on the carpet, who had not stirred. She could see the parting in her father’s hair.

“Somebody has told me a lie at a family table, and I’m afraid it will have to be felt. I don’t care to do it myself. I have had so many of you.” His head turned. “I am told you’re a prefect.”

Her wand left her sleeve.

She felt it go, a small tug at the inside of her wrist. It went across the table end over end and into his hand, and he glanced at it, as one looks at the label on somebody else’s coat. Then he came round and held it out to her, handle first, with a slight bow.

“Stand up, my dear. It’s quite a short word. I expect you know it. Your friend Moody will have shown you what it looks like, on a spider.”

She stood. She did not recall deciding to. The wand was warm from his fingers.

*Whatever he asks you to do, do it the first time.*

There were four yards of carpet. Her father had raised his head. He looked at her, and he smiled: not the stuck one, the real one, a little crooked, that had come round a door to her every morning of her childhood. He gave her the smallest nod. *Go on, my treasure. It’s quite all right.*

She raised her wand. It seemed to be somebody else’s arm.

“*Crucio*,” said Lyra.

A few red sparks came out of the end of it, and fell on the carpet, and went out.

Nobody laughed. It would have been easier if anybody had. Her father had flinched, and opened his eyes again, and that was all. In the silence the spoon went on chattering at the end of the table.

“No,” said the Dark Lord, with mild regret. “It isn’t a thing one can do by saying it. You have to mean it.”

“Perhaps it’s too much to ask.” He had turned his head a little, and he was looking down the table, at the fair wet parting of a boy in a crooked bow tie. “Draco has done so well already. Shall we see?”

Her mother’s fingers closed.

It was the only thing Lyra saw them do. She had gone quite cold. She looked at her father, on his knees, on the carpet that had come from Isfahan with her great-grandmother, and she went down into herself to look for it, because it had to be found, now, in the next four seconds, and it had to be real. She looked for hatred. She loved him. He had taught her chess. He had stood up from his plate this very minute, and knelt down, and lied for her with his whole heart; he had looked at her first, and she would remember that when she was old. There was nothing there, nothing, room after swept and lighted room, all full of him. She went further down. She went past the furniture. She went down the back stairs of herself to the place where she never went, where it was cold, and the light was the colour of a Christmas morning, and there was one place laid at a very long table.

*Please come.*

She had been eleven. She had written it in her best hand. It had taken her four tries to get the *P* right. The answer had come on the Friday, a whole page in his beautiful careful hand, about the weather abroad and the Board of Governors, and she was not to be fed on pudding.

He had read it. That was the thing she had never once looked at. He had read it, and he had turned over, and he had dipped his pen.

*Who came?* said a voice in the straw. *Go on. You’re so sure.*

There it was. It was very small, and very hot, and it had been there for a long time. It fitted her hand exactly.

“*Crucio*,” said Lyra.

It went down her arm like a stair going out from under her.

She had thought it would feel like something being done. It did not feel like doing anything. It was the easiest thing she had ever done in her life. It simply ran out of her and across the four yards of carpet and into him, and it went on running, and her father’s back arched, and his head went back, and a noise came out of him. She had heard that noise once, from a hare, in the long grass behind the stables. She had not known that it could come out of a person who had read to her. His hands had come up, and they were opening and closing. His hair had fallen over his face. A chair went over.

She could not look at that. There was only one other thing to look at. Past the end of her wand, on the far side of the table, there was a white face above a bow tie.

Draco was not looking at their father.

He was looking at her. His mouth was a little open, with the gap in it. She had seen him look at her in a great many ways: on a back stair, on a lawn, on a hearthrug with a rosette on. He looked at her now as one looks at a door in one’s own house that one has never seen before; and it was not fear, not yet. He only wanted very much for somebody to tell him what it was.

“That will do,” said the Dark Lord.

She went on. She did not mean to. It did not seem to have anywhere to stop. It took some effort, a sort of closing of the hand, and nobody had mentioned that. Then it was gone, and her arm was her own arm, and it was cold; and in the great silence of the dining room she could hear somebody on the floor breathing in small careful sips.

He had come to stand beside her. He took the wand out of her fingers, for which she was grateful, because she did not think she could have opened them. He looked down at her from a long way up with his red and interested eyes, and he slid it back into her sleeve himself, with his cold dry fingers against her wrist, and he patted it.

“Well done,” said the Dark Lord, “my treasure.”

He complimented her mother on the soup.

He said that he found the north rather cold, at this time of year, and it had all become so public; he had been so comfortable here in the old days, and he hoped they would not think it an imposition. He understood the south rooms got the morning. Something long and dark came out from under the end of the table and poured itself across the carpet after him, past her father’s hand, and the door shut behind it, and they heard him going up the stairs, not fast, asking Pobble something about the chimneys.

Nobody moved.

Then her mother got up. She crossed the room, and knelt down in her grey silk, and she turned Lucius’s face towards her and wiped his mouth. She did not look at Lyra. Her grandfather was still sitting at the foot of the table, with his hands in his lap and his sleeves wet, looking in front of him. It was Draco who got down from his chair. He did it carefully. He walked along the wall, right round the room, so as not to go near her. He went out.

She went up at eleven. It seemed to be a thing that one could do. They had moved his bed to the room next to her mother’s, and there was a line of light under the door.

“Draco,” she said.

The light went out.

Then she heard, on the other side of the wood, quite quietly, a small hand turning the key. He had never locked a door in his life.

Her father was in the big bed, propped up on three pillows, with a glass on the table and his hair brushed. Somebody had brushed it. He looked clean and old. He put out his hand when she came in, and it took him two attempts to get it as far as the edge of the sheet.

She took it. She did not know what else there was to do. She had thought of what she would say, on the stairs, and it had all gone.

“My treasure,” said Lucius Malfoy. It came out as a thread, and he stopped to get his breath, and he smiled at her, with his cracked lips, and his eyes were shining. “You were *magnificent*.”

He meant it. She looked to see, and he did. He had been frightened all week that she would fail, and that it would be the end of her, and she had not failed; his clever girl had stood up in front of the Dark Lord and done it the second time. He pressed her fingers. He was so proud. He could hardly speak.

She kissed his forehead, as he did every night. She said what she was supposed to say, and she went along the gallery to her own room, past her great-great-grandmother asleep with her mouth open, and she shut the door, and got as far as the window seat.

Her hands had begun. She looked at them with some interest. They had started very small, at the fingertips; they were going like her grandfather’s spoon.

She wanted Draco. She did not want him as he was now, behind a door; she wanted the other one. She shut her eyes. There was one thing that had always worked, on the worst nights of this whole bad year, in the dark, under the lake. She went to the middle of the room, and there it was, a little to the left: a small boy on a stool with the Hat over his eyes and his feet not touching the floor. There was the Hall. There was the table going up. She looked at it.

She looked at it for some time.

It was all there. She could have told anybody how long the Hat had taken. She knew perfectly well that she had been so frightened, and then so glad, that her hands had gone white under the table. She knew that as one knows the date of a treaty. She stood in front of it and waited, and it did not do anything. It was a good likeness. It was a thing that had been put in a room.

He had looked underneath it. He had set it down a little to the left.

She opened her eyes. Somewhere over towards the village, thin and small in the snow, they had started to ring the old year out.

She got up, and crossed the floor, and opened the drawer. It took her some time to undo the handkerchief, because of her fingers. The ring fell out into her palm, and it lay there on her bare skin, plain and silver and cold; and then it was not cold.

She had made a good many promises about this. She had made them in an Owlery, and they had been extremely well expressed.

She closed her hand, and sat down on the floor with her back against the bed, and held on. When she opened it again the ring had gone the colour of a fresh cut, and it was so hot that it hurt.
