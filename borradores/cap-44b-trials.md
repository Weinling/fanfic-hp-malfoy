# Chapter 45: Trials

On the first morning of term Harry changed his seat.

It was only a matter of going round the end of the Gryffindor table instead of along it, and sitting down on the far side, between Neville and the marmalade, with his back to the Slytherins. He had sat facing them for four years. He had had reasons: you could see who came in. Ron arrived with his badge on and his hair wet, looked at the place where Harry usually was, looked at Harry, and sat down opposite him with the air of somebody who has been asked to think about too many things before breakfast.

“You all right?”

“Draught,” said Harry.

He had thought it out in the night. He had told a person on a train that she ought to have been crying, and she had put his collar straight, and he had taken her by the wrist and told her she couldn’t keep doing that. It seemed to him that there was only one thing left that he could do to her that he hadn’t done already, and that was to stop. People gave things up. Uncle Vernon had given up pudding for nearly a week. You didn’t have to feel any better about it. You just arranged matters so that the thing was behind you, and you ate your toast.

It lasted very well. He did not look at her at breakfast. He did not look at her at lunch. He discovered, in the course of that first Saturday, how much of his day had been built round a point about forty feet behind him; it was like coming downstairs in the dark and finding that somebody had taken away the bottom step. He knew when she had come in. That was the annoying part. He had his back to her and his mouth full, and he knew, from the way that Parkinson’s voice went up, and because Neville had glanced over his shoulder at nothing, and for no reason at all.

“What’s that?” said Harry.

Neville looked down. He had a clay pot on the table, between the toast rack and his elbow, and in the pot there was a small grey lumpy thing with boils on it.

“It’s from my great-uncle,” said Neville. “It’s very rare. It can’t have been easy to get. It doesn’t do much, yet. I’ve got to talk to it.”

“What about?”

Neville went pink. “I’ve just been telling it what’s for breakfast,” he said.

Harry looked at the plant for a while. It was the first thing he had looked at all day that did not know anything about him. He had forgotten about the plant. He had got a list of a great many things that were going to happen to a great many people, and there was no earthly reason why a small grey lump belonging to Neville Longbottom should have been on it, and it was very good to see it.

“Kippers,” Harry told it.

Neville gave him a look of such shy and overwhelming gratitude that he had to go back to his toast.

There was a table in the corner of the Gryffindor common room, between the fire and the window, that nobody had ever chosen. It had one short leg, and it had been propped since before Fred and George came to the school on the first volume of *Unfogging the Future*, which was the only use that anybody had found for it. Some long-dead Gryffindor had carved a rude word in the top and somebody else had made it into a dragon. It was too small for four people, and there were generally seven.

By the Sunday night, Hermione had the end of it, with a stack of parchment and her prefect’s rota, and was drawing lines with a ruler. Ron had the chessboard. He was playing Dean and Seamus at once, with his chin on his hand, and he was reading the back of a Quidditch magazine while he did it. Every so often he reached out without looking and moved something, and Seamus swore. Ginny was sitting on the arm of the sofa eating an apple and telling Ron what was wrong with him.

“You’ve got your elbows out.”

“I’m playing chess.”

“On a broom. You’ve got them out on a broom, and it’s the same when you sit down. You look like a jug.”

“Angelina hasn’t said anything about my elbows.”

“Angelina hasn’t seen them. Trials are on Saturday.” She bit her apple. “I’ve seen them. I’ve been throwing things at them since I was six, and I’m having Angelina’s place the day she leaves, and I’m not going out on that pitch with a jug.”

Ron’s ears had started. He moved a bishop, took Dean’s queen, and put the magazine down. There was a page of it he seemed to have been reading for some time. It was a piece about the Cannons’ new Keeper, and he had got his thumb on the photograph, as you would hold something down.

“There’ll be about twenty people going,” he said. “Everybody knows it’s open, with Wood gone. There’s that big seventh-year, Bunce. There’s some third-year who’s supposed to be good. I don’t know why I said I’d do it. I’ve got to be a prefect. I’ve got to go round telling people to stop doing things, and then I’m going to have to go out there, in front of them—”

“You’ll get it,” said Harry.

He was on the floor with his back against the sofa, not doing his Potions. He had said it without looking up. He heard the table go quiet, and Ron turned round in his chair, and looked at him, and there was a good deal in that look. There was a boy who had seen Harry catch his first Snitch in his mouth, and had thought for four years, without ever once saying so, that it must be nice.

“You reckon?”

“Yeah.”

“You’re not being funny? Like with Head Boy?”

He was not being funny. That was the awkward thing. He had been funny on a pavement in Diagon Alley, and Ron had gone red and looked at his brown paper, and neither of them had mentioned it since. He had missed this, the first time. There had been a trial and a detention at the same hour, and he had come back up to the tower with his hand on fire and had the whole of it from Ron in about nine words. A year later he had stood on the ground and watched him do it again, in front of a big red-faced boy called something else, and fly away grinning like a lamp. He knew how it went after that, too. He knew how the first match went. He knew what a Slytherin stand could do to a person, in a good loud wind, with a tune that everybody could join in. He had thought about that, on and off, since the summer, and he had thought of six or seven things you might say to somebody to help them through it. And all of them began with knowing about it.

It might be different. Everything else was.

“You’ve got a new broom,” said Harry. “And you’ve been stopping Ginny’s for eight years. I’m only saying.”

“Oh,” said Ron. He turned back, slowly. He had gone a dull, surprised, pleased colour, and he looked at his chessmen as if they had been saying something nice about him behind his back. “Well. Yeah. I suppose.”

“Check,” said Ginny, with her mouth full.

Seamus looked down at the board. “Who said that? He’s not even looking!”

“He’s got you in four,” said Ginny. “I’m saving time.”

Hermione had raised her head. She looked at Harry, over the top of her ruler, for about as long as it takes to read a line, and then she looked at the rug, and at which side of it he had taken to sitting on.

“You’ve moved,” said Hermione.

“Draught.”

“In here?” She waited, and he went on not doing his Potions, and after a moment she looked down. “I’ve put Ron and me on the same nights, by the way. All of them. It’s the most efficient arrangement.”

“’Course it is,” said Ginny.

Hermione went pink to the parting, made a small neat mark on her rota, and blew on it.

Defence Against the Dark Arts came round on the Wednesday. He had been looking forward to it in the way you look forward to the dentist, with a certain interest in seeing whether it would be as bad as you remembered.

It was exactly as bad. It had the same doilies. It had the same kittens, on the same plates, and the same small round girlish voice; and he sat in the third row with a book called *Defensive Magical Theory* open in front of him, and listened to Hermione’s hand going up. There was a good deal of pale hair in the front row, neatly pinned. It was not behind him now. It was very difficult to arrange that. He looked at the blackboard.

He had shouted, the last time. He had been fifteen, and it had all been new; he had stood up in front of everybody with his fists shut and shouted at a toad. He wasn’t going to do that. He knew what it got you. He knew what was on the other end of it, on a little table by a window, in a long thin box. He had been going over what he knew about that quill, in the night, and he thought there was a fair chance that he could get through the whole year with his mouth shut, and the use of his hand. It was what any sensible person would do. Hermione was saying something about using spells. Umbridge laughed, and said that nobody was going to attack them in class. Harry looked at the back of Dean’s head.

Twenty-seven people were sitting in that room with their wands in their bags. Eleven of them, by his count, would be dead or in St Mungo’s before they were twenty. Nobody had told them. Somebody at the back of the room was yawning.

“Not in class,” said Harry. “Outside it.”

So that was that. He said the rest of it quite politely. He heard himself being polite. He said the name, and heard twenty-seven people breathe in, and he saw, in front of him, a very straight back not move at all; and the toad smiled at him, with her head on one side, and said *five o’clock*.

He was putting his book away when the bell went. He did not look round. He had got his bag on his shoulder, and Ron was saying something furious at his elbow, and they were nearly at the door when he noticed that the room had emptied in front of him and that one person had not got up. She was sitting in the front row, with her back to him, closing her bag very slowly, as you’d close something that might wake.

He knew what she was going to do. He could have told you what she would say, and in what voice. *I told you which side*, she had said, in the straw.

Ron said, “What?”

“Nothing,” said Harry, and went down to lunch.

The quill was as he remembered it.

He had thought he might be better at this, the second time. He had thought a good deal about what you did with your mind. It turned out that the back of your hand had not been told. It opened up along the same line, and he sat at the little table under the window, with the kittens frisking, and wrote, and he looked at the thing happening to him with a sort of tired recognition, as you’d look at a town you had hoped not to have to change trains in.

He got back to the tower at ten past nine. He had a story ready, and his right hand in his pocket. The common room had thinned out. There was a napkin on the short-legged table, with a plate on top of it to keep off Neville’s plant, and Ron was sitting beside it in his socks, doing nothing, with an expression of great cunning.

“What’s this?”

“Nothing. Sausage roll. Two. There were three.” He pushed it across with one finger. “You missed dinner. You’re going to be missing it all week, Hermione says; she’s looked it up. I’ve had a word with the elves. Well, Fred has. What did she make you do?”

“Lines.”

“Lines,” said Ron, with contempt. “That’s not so bad. Percy got lines off her once, at the Ministry, for something to do with a stapler. Are you going to eat that?”

Harry took out his left hand, and picked up the sausage roll. It was cold, and it had a thumbprint in it. He had eaten at a good many tables, in the course of his two lives. He had been fed in the Great Hall and at the Burrow, and out of a tin in a tent, and he sat down on the bad end of the bench and ate this one, right down to the crumbs, with the fire going out, and Ron yawning at him across a rude dragon.

There were things you could tell people. He could feel it, running up the bones of his wrist to the elbow. He thought about how it would be: Ron’s face, and the noise he would make. He’d go to McGonagall. He’d stand up in that room with his badge on. He had a trial on Saturday and a list of things to tell people to stop, and he had been so pleased about the sausage roll.

“You look awful,” said Ron.

“Thanks.”

“Any time.”

He went to see Diggory on the Thursday. He had known what would happen, and he went anyway, as you press on a tooth.

It had got about by then, in the way things did. There was a room behind the kitchens that had once been used for duelling, with a long strip of worn boards and a smell of cold dust; and the Head Boy had decided that if the Ministry of Magic was not going to teach anybody to defend themselves, then he would, on Tuesdays and Thursdays, and that he did not propose to ask. It was sixth- and seventh-years, somebody said. Cho Chang was in it. Fred and George were in it, on a promise. Harry found him at the end of lunch, in the Entrance Hall, by the doors, with four or five people round him and a list in his hand. He had his hand on the list and his badge on his chest. There was a fine white line in his hair.

“I heard you’re starting something,” said Harry.

Diggory looked up. It took him no time at all. It was a very pleasant face. Harry had seen it on its side in a hedge, with a lump coming up on its forehead, and he had done that.

“Sixth years and seventh,” said Diggory.

“I can do a Patronus. A proper one. I could—”

“I know what you could do, Potter.” He did not raise his voice. He said it with perfect courtesy, in front of Cho Chang and two Hufflepuffs and Lee Jordan, as you would explain to somebody that the shop was shut. “I’ve seen it. It’s very effective, from behind. I don’t think we’ll need that.”

Lee Jordan looked at his shoes. Nobody else moved.

There was nothing to say to that. He looked for it, to be fair. He had been going to tell Diggory that it had been for his own good: that he could not have let him touch that Cup; that in another life he had seen him lying on the grass with his eyes open. He looked at the white line. It was all perfectly true, and it would not have done anybody any good at all.

“Right,” said Harry.

He turned round. Ron and Hermione were on the bottom stair, with her books and his Quidditch magazine; she was explaining something about the rota. Ron had his arm out, for some reason, and was holding it at a very careful angle, with the elbow in. He looked up, and said something, and she laughed. They would be sixth-years next September, he thought. It would be a thing they went to together. He had thought of getting into that room, as he’d thought of three tickets on the Holloway Road. He supposed he ought to have seen this one coming.

He went up to them, and he asked about the elbow.

Saturday was bright and blowing, with big white clouds going over very fast. There were twenty-three people on the pitch, and about half of Gryffindor in the stand to watch them. Harry had taken his seat in the front row, as Seeker, with the rest of the team and a bag of toffees, and he held it in his left hand.

Angelina had got a whistle. She had got a voice to go with it, and she had been waiting for that voice since her first year. She had them up in fives. She sent the Chasers in on each of them, one after another, and Harry sat and watched the left-hand hoop. Bunce was large, and let in two. A very small third-year shut his eyes every time that the Quaffle came near him, and saved three, and was led away. There was a sixth-year called Vane who stopped everything for ten minutes, and then started shouting at Katie Bell about her grip.

Ron was nineteenth. He came out with the new broom, very tall and very pale. He had a look on his face that Harry had seen on him in a chess game, once, with a white queen coming at him. He rose up to the hoops. Halfway there he looked round for the stand. He found Harry; Harry could see him do it, across the whole of the pitch; and his elbows stuck out on either side of him like the handles of a jug.

“*Elbows!*” bawled Ginny, from somewhere behind Harry’s ear.

Ron let in the first one. He let in the second. It went through the right-hand hoop as he was looking at the left, and a sound went up from the stand, not unkindly, the sort of sound you’d make at a dropped plate. There was somebody at the Hufflepuff end of the pitch who had stopped to watch. Harry had got hold of the bag of toffees rather hard.

“He’s going to be sick,” said Fred, with interest.

“He’s got a sensitive nature,” George agreed.

The third shot came in low, from Alicia. Ron went for it. He had his mouth open and his eyes on the ball; he had stopped looking at the stand. Harry saw his shoulders come down. He had seen that in a tent, in the rain. He saw the long arm go out. It was a horrible sight, and it was all elbow. The Quaffle hit him on the wrist, and bounced, and went away over the top of the hoops into the sky.

Harry was on his feet.

He did not remember getting up. He seemed to have got toffees all over the seat. There was somebody shouting “*YES!*” in a very loud cracked voice, and people were looking at him; he had not made a noise like that about anything since he had got here; and it was the most extraordinary feeling. He knew how this came out. He had been told. He had never once seen it. He stood on the bench with his bad hand in his pocket, and yelled at Ron Weasley as if the world depended on it.

Ron stopped the fourth with his foot. It was not clear that he had meant to. Katie took the fifth herself, hard and high, straight at the top of the middle hoop. He was going the other way. He flung himself back along the broom, nearly came off it, and got the Quaffle full in the side of the head. There was a sound like somebody dropping a cushion. It went wide. Ron hung there for a moment, with one leg over the handle, looking mildly surprised, and one of his ears gradually turning the colour of a boiled beetroot.

There was a short and thoughtful silence.

“He saved that with his *head*,” said Ginny, in a voice of awe.

Ginny was the first to reach him. She flew out without a broom of her own, on the back of George’s, and hit him so hard between the shoulder blades that he nearly went off the other side. He was saying “Meant to, I meant to,” to anybody who would listen. Then Harry had got there, on foot, running, and Ron had landed on him rather than near him. He smelt of new varnish and fright. He had got Harry by the front of his robes with both hands, and he was shaking him, and he didn’t seem to know that he was doing that.

“Did you *see?*”

“I saw.”

“You said! You *said* I’d— I got three! With my *head!*”

He was laughing, with his whole big raw red face, and his ear was coming up like a loaf. Hermione had arrived and was doing something to it, and crying a bit, and telling him it was concussion. There wasn’t anything else that needed doing in the whole of Scotland. Harry had his left arm round somebody’s neck. It was a thing that had already happened. He had missed it, and he had thought that you didn’t get those back. And he looked at Ron’s ear, and his own face hurt, and he noticed that he was not thinking about what came next.

Angelina came in at one o’clock with mud on her whistle, read out one name, looked at his ear, and said, “Tuesday. Elbows in.” They had the party at the short-legged table, because it spread out from there. Fred and George went away and came back with a sack. There were pumpkin pasties and a great many Chocolate Frogs and something fizzy in a bucket; and Lee Jordan did the save, with sound effects, using Neville’s head, with his permission. Somebody started to sing. Ginny had got on to a chair. Neville’s plant had been given a very small paper hat.

“I told it about the save,” Neville said, in Harry’s ear, over the noise. “I thought it ought to know.”

“Did you tell it about the head?”

Neville looked at the plant, and considered this with some care. “I thought I’d leave that till it’s older,” he said.

Ron sat in the middle of it, with a cold flannel on his ear and a Chocolate Frog in each hand, being told what he had done by people who had been there; and every so often he looked across the rude dragon at Harry, and started to grin again, and winced. Nobody asked Harry anything. He ate four pasties, left-handed. He stayed till the end.

The rota went up on the Monday.

It was on the noticeboard by the portrait hole, among the usual things: a notice about Hogsmeade, somebody’s lost cat. Harry had come down early, because of his hand, which had been written on three times now and hurt in a dull, reasonable way, like somebody explaining something. He had the common room to himself. Pinned at the bottom, in Hermione’s small square writing, was the prefects’ list for the term, ruled in three colours.

He had not been going to look at that.

He only read it because it was there. There were eight columns. There were the Gryffindors, and the Ravenclaws. *Slytherin: Malfoy and Zabini, sixth and seventh, alternate nights.* That was how it had been drawn, with a ruler.

Somebody had come along since and changed it. It would have come through the prefects’ room, and Hermione would have copied it, because she copied everything. She had put a line through a name, very straight. *Zabini* had gone from the top floor. *Seventh: Malfoy*, it said, in fresh ink. *Every night, by arrangement.*

Harry stood and looked at it.

There wasn’t anything on the seventh floor, not at that hour. There was the Arithmancy door, which he had stood outside every morning for five months, and which would be locked. There was a bare wall, and a bust in a wig, and a tapestry of some trolls. She had no reason in the world to want it. She hated the cold. There was nothing up there at all, at midnight, except a room that he knew about and nobody else did; and a very long corridor, with a window at the end of it, a deep one, that you could sit in.

He was not going to do anything. He wanted that understood. He had given this up. He just wanted to know where she was. It wasn’t the same as looking.

Feet were coming down the boys’ stairs, large ones, and somebody was complaining about his ear. Harry took his hand out of his pocket to see what it was doing, and he put it back, and he went to breakfast. He sat between Neville and the marmalade.
