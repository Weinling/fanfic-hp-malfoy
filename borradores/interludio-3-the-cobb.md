# Interlude: The Cobb

I take the soup up on Thursdays.

I have done so for fourteen years and five months, and until this spring I took it to the east cottage, through the study, by a Portkey that I have never been thanked for. Now it goes to the old nursery. There are forty-one stairs. It is barley, because he can manage barley. I carry the tray myself, since Pobble’s hands have started to shake when she comes to that door, and I set it on the stool, and I tie the napkin, and then I give it to my daughter, who has taken to being there.

I have a list. One should. The linen was counted this morning. One of the double sheets in the second-floor cupboard has a hole bitten in the crest. It was at the bottom of the pile, folded with the hole inside. I have not spoken to anyone about that. I put it back as I found it. The limes want cutting. My husband is allowed one blanket and has written to ask for a second. My son has oiled his key. There are sixteen people dining tomorrow, and the Dark Lord does not eat, which means that I shall have to think of something to do with his plate.

I do not sleep a great deal, and I have found that it’s better to go through things in order. I shall go through this one, since nobody else was there.

It was the eighteenth of June. I took the Portkey to Charmouth at ten o’clock, and I told Madame Levasseur that I would come back for the fitting, and I walked. I had not walked anywhere in the open air, by myself, since Easter. It is three miles to Lyme along the cliff road. I wore flat shoes, which I had bought for the purpose. It was the hottest day of the year. There were Muggles on the beach in very little clothing, eating things. I had a thin gold chain round my neck, and I had not taken it off for six months except to bathe.

He was at the far end of the Cobb, where the wall curves round, sitting on the edge with his boots hanging over. I had not seen him since the summer of 1977. I had seen his face on a poster. I knew him by the way that he sat. He sat as he had at nine, when we were brought there by the governess, as though he had paid for the sea himself and thought it rather overpriced.

He didn’t get up.

“Cissy,” he said. “You’ve got old. Who’s dead?”

He was thirty-six. I hadn’t been ready for it. His hair had gone grey over the ears and he had burnt his nose, and he had on a Muggle waistcoat of a colour that I shall not attempt, with the buttons fastened wrongly. It was buttoned wrongly in such a way that I had an impulse to put out my hand. I did not put out my hand. There had been a time when I did that for all three of them, in the hall at Grimmauld Place, before our aunt came down.

I said what I had come to say. He looked at my shoes. He said that if this was one of Lucius’s, there were crabs just below him who had been waiting for me for some time. I undid the clasp. It took me a little while; I found that I could not feel the ends of my fingers, in that heat. I held it out to him, the paper with its small brass case, and he looked at it, and then at me, and for the first time he stopped smiling.

He knew the hand before he had unfolded it. I saw him know. Regulus made his capital letters as his father did, with the little stroke at the foot, and he had been told about it.

I shall not put down what it said. There are three lines and some initials. He read it. He read it with his head bent, in the sun, with the gulls going over, and I stood at the end of a stone wall in Dorset and looked out at the English Channel and I did not watch. I am able to do that. I have sat at table for six months with what I have sat at table with. It went on for some time. When I looked round, he had his forearm across his eyes, and his shoulders were not moving, and his mouth was open a little, like that of a person who has been running.

Then he read it a second time. And he laughed. It was a dreadful noise; it went off along the wall and put up all the birds. He wiped his face on the waistcoat. His eyes had swollen, and they were very bright. “The little *idiot*,” said Sirius. “By himself. In a *cave*. He couldn’t cross the road by himself. Who brought you this?”

I said that it had been brought. He looked at me. He has our family’s eyes. He looked at the shoes again, and then, in quite a different way, at my mouth.

“There’s a girl,” he said slowly. “Isn’t there? It’s not you. You’d never have thought of it. There’s a girl who gets things carried. There’s a very thin girl with a nose on her, who once gave a stray dog a chicken leg in a hedge, and told him exactly what he owed her.” He had begun to grin. It came up on one side. “There’s no one in Slough, is there? Oh, you’re joking. *Lucius’s*— Does she hit him with a ruler? He says she hits him with a ruler.”

I did not reply. I have not been asked that question before.

He laughed for some time. He seemed to find it the best thing that had happened to him since Azkaban. He beat on the stone with his hand. He made me sit down, and I sat; I sat on the end of the Cobb in a dress from Madame Levasseur, with my feet above the water. A Muggle had a cart at the landward end, and he went and bought two ices, with money that I gave him, because he had not got any, and he brought one back to me, in a sort of biscuit. Nobody has ever handed me such a thing in my life. I held it. It started to run down my wrist.

At some point he went down the steps at the end of the wall and stood in the sea to his knees, in his boots, and came back with weed on his cuff. He said that he had wanted to see if it was still wet.

He told me about the sheep.

I have gone over this a good deal. I do not believe that he had spoken to anyone for twelve days, apart from the bird. He had been on a hillside in the north of Scotland, in the rain, in a hut with one good corner, and he told me what he’d been told: that a watch had to be kept, and it was to do with the seven, and it couldn’t be anybody from the Order, and he mustn’t breathe a word to Dumbledore. He did the voice. He did the boy’s voice being offhand. “There’s forty-one of them, Cissy. There’s nothing up there. There has never been anything up there. Nobody has come near that hill since the Jacobites. I knew it by about the second day. He’s moved me. He’s found out something, or he’s frightened of something, and he’s picked me up and put me on a moor, like a chess piece, the horrible boy; and he sends me an owl every morning to see if I’m bored. He’s got a *knot*. Have you ever heard anything so outrageous? He thinks he’s lying to me.”

He licked the ice from the side of his hand.

“He’s fifteen. He does it with his whole face. He looks you in the eye and tells you he’s fine, and he’s got his hands in his pockets, and you’d believe him, you’d give him your watch. James couldn’t lie to save his life. He went red. Lily could do it and she’d look sorry. That’s not out of either of them. I don’t know where he got that. That’s just *his*.”

I said that he seemed very pleased about it.

“I’m so proud I could burst,” said Sirius Black.

He wasn’t laughing. He was looking out to sea, with his eyes screwed up and the tin-coloured light all over his face. He said that nobody had thought he was worth the trouble of deceiving since 1981. He said he’d let the boy think that he had got away with it for a bit longer, if it was all the same to me. It seemed to be doing him good.

I had not cried for Regulus. I hadn’t, you understand, when we were told. I don’t cry; it marks the skin; I had thought that this was a question of having been properly brought up. I sat beside my cousin on a wall with something sweet running down my arm. I do not know how long that was.

He didn’t look at me. It was the only courtesy that I have ever known him to show. After a while he held out a very dirty handkerchief, in my general direction, without turning his head, and said that I’d been sitting on his side of the sea since 1968, and that I could give that back when I had finished.

I still have it. Bella has not found it. It’s under the third drawer.

I had meant to stay for a quarter of an hour. The tide had come in round the foot of the wall and gone a good way out again. The clock of St Michael’s struck the half-hour after four, up the hill behind us, and I had not sat anywhere for four hours together since my wedding.

He had the paper folded small by then, and he had put it away in the pocket of that waistcoat. He patted it. I had thought that he would give it back; I had meant to ask. I found that I didn’t.

“You can tell your daughter that the chicken’s on my account,” said Sirius. “The chocolate as well. Tell her she’ll get it when I’m a disgraceful old man. Tell her—”

It came across the water.

I saw it before he did. It came very fast and very low, from the direction of the land. It was the colour of the light on a knife. It ran on the air as a dog runs on a beach, a long lean grey-silver thing with its head down. It went by the Muggle with the cart and he did not turn round. I have seen a great many things that frightened me this year, but I had never seen that, and I stood up. It stopped in front of him, a yard from his boots, with the sea showing through it. It opened its jaws. It had a tired, educated, rather gentle voice, and it was out of breath.

*They’ve got Harry. The Ministry. Come now.*

I should like to be able to say what his face did. I had my eyes on it. I have thought that if I could only see that clearly, I might be able to put it somewhere. He had a bit of biscuit in his hand. He looked at it, and he dropped it to the gulls. And he got up, all in one movement, as he used to get up from table, at nine, when someone had told him that there was a fight; and all those years went out of him, like water going out of a basin, and he had started to laugh.

“Forty-one sheep,” said Sirius. “You little *liar*.”

I said his name. I believe that I said *Don’t*. I do not think that I have ever said it to anyone before, and it’s not a word that I know how to use.

He looked round. He looked very pleased. I think that he had forgotten who I was.

“Cissy,” he said, “you’ve got ice cream on your chin.”

There was a small flat crack, and I was by myself at the end of the Cobb.

I walked back to Charmouth. I had the fitting. I am told that it was a success.

This Thursday, when I had finished with the soup, Severus Snape came up to look at Abraxas’s hands.

He comes every week. He has no treatment to suggest. He stands and looks, and writes something down, and the old man watches him do it with considerable enjoyment. My daughter had gone. She has things to do, in the afternoons, in my husband’s study, with a good nib, and I have seen her hands. I shut the door. I told Severus what she had asked for, at my table, in front of eight people, and what she had been given, and I told him what would happen to her if the thing was not done by the twentieth of December. It was not news to him. He stood with his back to the window. I do not know how to describe his face. There has never been anything on it that I could use.

I have known him since he was eleven. He has eaten at my table for fifteen years. I have never known what he wants, or whether he has ever wanted anything except what he can’t have. I know that he stood in my drawing room when she was born and looked at her as one looks at a bad hand of cards. I know that he has had her on Thursday evenings for a year, and that she has come out from them better at something.

I said that I wished him to swear.

He asked me what, as one asks the price of something in a shop where one has no intention of buying. I told him. I have had some time to get the wording right. I wrote it out, and I learnt it, and I burnt the paper. I said that there were three things. He would keep her in his sight. He would put himself between her and anything that came, so far as he was able. And should her life be asked for the thing undone, he would do that thing in her place.

Nobody spoke. The hands were going, under the linen, with that noise.

“It’s a b-bad contract,” said Abraxas Malfoy, from his chair. His eyes had come alive. “No t-term to it. No consideration. Who’s the third party? I’ve seen men hang for less. I’d be d-delighted.”

It wants a third to bind it. There is no one else. I undid the strip from his right wrist; he can’t hold a wand now, so I laid it across his fingers and I tied it there, with the same strip of linen, in the same knot that I use for the soup napkin. I took Severus’s hand. It was dry and rather cold, and it was the first time that I had touched him in all those years. He hadn’t yet said anything. He was looking at me, with his flat black eyes, down that long yellow nose.

“I wonder, Severus,” I said, “if you understand what I am asking.” My voice was quite as usual. “You will have heard from a dozen people what a Malfoy is. I daresay that it’s all true. I have nothing to pay you with. I have looked. There isn’t anything in this house that you can’t get for yourself, or that isn’t already his. I shall owe you, and I can’t pay, and I shall go on owing you for as long as I live, and you’ll never be able to collect. I can think of no good reason at all why you should stand here.”

He looked at the bars of the window. It was a fine afternoon. You could see a very long way, from up there. It’s a thing I had forgotten about nurseries.

“You needn’t look at me,” said Severus Snape, “as though I had anywhere else to be.”

He said the words. He has a very clear voice when he cares to use it. *I will*, and *I will*; and I saw him stop, before the third, for no longer than it takes to turn over a card. *I will.* The linen drew tight. It went round both our hands and it went in, cold, like a wire being pulled through butter. It was there, and then there wasn’t any linen. There was the old man’s wand, rolling on the rug, and a thin red line across my wrist, which faded as I looked at it.

“I sh-should like that in the book,” said Abraxas. He was breathing fast, and he looked twenty years younger. “For form’s sake. The p-parties, and the date. It’s the b-best thing to go in there since the Greengrass affair.”

Severus had picked up his notes. He stopped with his hand on the door. He looked at the long thing lying on the table by the bed, with its lock hanging open; and then, and it was only for an instant, he pushed back his left cuff with one finger, as you might check the time.

“I have signed one book,” he said. “I don’t care for the bindings.”

I heard him go down. There are forty-one stairs.

Bella came in as I was doing the flowers.

She likes to sit with me now. She took a rose out of the vase, and ate some of it. She’d had a good morning; her cut had opened, and she was pleased about that. She told me about the Ministry, and she told me again about the Atrium, and how he’d gone backwards with his eyebrows up. She has told me nineteen times. I counted. She looks at my neck when she tells it.

“I’ve run out of wax, Cissy,” said my sister. “May I have yours?”

She held out her long dirty hand, and smiled, and the corner of her mouth came slowly open.

I went to the desk. I took out the stick of green from the second pigeonhole, with my initials on it, and I put it in her palm, and I folded her fingers over.

“Do keep it,” I said.

I have another. It is in with the seed catalogues.
