# Chapter 24: Replay

The ring went gold at a quarter past two on a Tuesday morning, and Lyra, who had not been asleep, sat up to look at it.

She drew the hangings round her bed at nine o’clock every night that summer and put it on in the dark, where nobody could see. It was certainly not because somebody had asked her to, under a cloister, in May. The slip of instructions from Zonko’s, which she had long ago learned by heart, listed more colours than any reasonable person could want, and in July she had found out that most of them were real. Purple, *on guard*. Gold, *success; something accomplished*. Green, *safe*. Red she had not seen since February, and did not intend to see again.

It had been gold for about a minute. Then it went green, and stayed green, and Lyra lay back down on her pillow with the satisfied feeling of somebody who has read the last page of a book first.

She had no idea what Potter did with his holidays. But a boy whose ring went purple at four in the morning and gold at a quarter past two was plainly not spending the summer weeding his aunt’s garden, and it was very rude of him not to say what he *was* doing.

He wrote, sometimes. Not often; an owl coming too often to the Manor would be noticed, and anything she sent by the family owl went by way of her father’s study window first. Potter’s letters came by birds she had never seen before, a different one every time, and they were signed *D. Dursley*, which was so ugly a name that it could only belong to a real person. They said very little. They told her it was hot. They told her to look after herself. Once, at the bottom, there was a line she read twice and then put inside the cover of a book she did not intend to lend to anybody. She answered twice, by owls hired at the post office in Diagon Alley on the days her mother took her shopping, which was more trouble than any letter had ever been worth.

“He’s telling *me* to be careful,” said Lyra, to the canopy of her bed. The ring, at that moment, was purple.

There was one more thing on the slip, and it was the thing she minded most. The rings worked both ways. Whatever hers told her about Potter, his was telling Potter about her, at that very moment, in colours, wherever he was. So when she lay down at night she was careful to think about nothing in particular: her Arithmancy, or the new broom polish, or the Quidditch World Cup, which was in a fortnight and which her father had got them into the top box for. She was not going to lie there thinking about anything that might come out blue.

She turned the ring round on her finger, and turned it back.

He would be at the World Cup. Everybody would be at the World Cup.

Lyra fell asleep looking at it.

“Lyra! *Lyra*, look! It’s Viktor Krum!”

Draco came into the morning room at a run, without knocking, which he had been told about, and unrolled a poster across her Arithmancy before she could stop him. On it, a young man with heavy black eyebrows and a nose like a hawk’s was hurtling straight down at the ground on a broom, faster and faster, until it seemed impossible that he would not go into it head first; then at the last instant he pulled up, the player behind him ploughed into the turf, and the picture swung round to show Krum rising, unhurried, with a Snitch in his fist. Underneath, the words *WRONSKI FEINT* blazed up in red and faded, and the whole thing began again.

“I thought you supported Ireland,” said Lyra.

“Ireland’s Seeker is *boring*. Krum’s *eighteen*, Lyra. He’s still at school and he’s Seeker for the whole of Bulgaria.” Draco was ten that summer, and he said *the whole of Bulgaria* as though he had personally been consulted about it. “Look, he does it again in a minute—”

Lyra leaned in to look. Draco snatched the poster back against his chest.

“No. You can’t see. It’s mine.”

There was a time, not very long ago, when Lyra would simply have taken it off him. But he was ten now, and had found out about stubbornness, and on the subject of stubbornness Lyra knew she was up against the only person in the Manor with more of it than she had. So instead she reached under the table, into the bag she had been meaning to show him anyway, and brought out a pair of golden binoculars covered in little brass knobs and dials.

Draco’s eyes went perfectly round.

“What are *those*?”

“Omnioculars,” said Lyra. “Made specially for the Irish team, for their training. Slow motion. Replay. They’ll follow any player you like for the whole match, and keep it, so you can watch it again afterwards. There are about twelve pairs in the world.”

“Whose are they?”

“Mine. The way the poster’s yours.”

“Give me a go!”

“I don’t think I shall, you know,” said Lyra, and turned them gently from side to side so that the light ran up and down the brass. Draco pretended not to watch them and failed. “Unless you’d like to swap.”

“*No*.”

“Never mind. You can look at Krum doing the same dive over and over again. I’ll watch him doing it live.”

“That’s *cheating*.”

It took him nearly a minute, a long time for Draco, to fling the poster at her. He took the omnioculars with both hands, as if they might fly away, and went to the window at once to look at a peacock through them.

Lyra unrolled the poster and watched Krum come out of the dive again. He really was extraordinary. She had flown against Potter for two seasons and she had never seen anybody do that with a broom, or with that little visible effort, as though the ground had been a suggestion. There was something not quite right about the picture, all the same. It was the face, she decided: heavy and sullen, and rather a waste of a dive like that. If it had been a different face, somebody’s with untidy hair and glasses, say, it would have been a good deal more—

“You *fancy* him!” Draco had the omnioculars trained on her from the window, and was delighted. “You do! Girls are all the same. They see somebody good at Quidditch and they go all stupid.”

“I do not fancy Viktor Krum,” said Lyra, with great clarity. “Not even slightly.”

“Then you don’t want the poster.”

She considered. “No. I’ve seen it. You can have it back.”

“*Yes!*” Draco snatched it off the table before she could change her mind, and clutched both his treasures to his chest, and bolted. She heard him shouting from the hall that he was going to show the gentleman.

Lyra sat for a moment after he had gone. The gentleman in the east cottage still lived in the east cottage, then. She had not asked, all summer, for the same reason she had not asked at Christmas, and nobody had said.

Then she reached into the bag again and took out the other pair of omnioculars: the real ones, Irish team issue, with the gold leaf on the dials. The pair she had given Draco had cost ten Galleons in Diagon Alley, and he would never know the difference, and he was perfectly happy, and so was she.

“When,” she said to the empty room, “is that child going to grow up?”

The Malfoys did not queue.

The Ministry had connected a handful of private fireplaces to the stadium for the use of important persons, and the Minister for Magic had invited Lucius Malfoy to use one, so they stepped out of the Manor’s drawing-room grate and into a panelled anteroom somewhere under the stands without so much as a smut on their robes. Lyra had heard that other people had walked for miles over wet fields at dawn, with a Portkey that looked like an old boot. She was sorry for them, in a general way.

Cornelius Fudge was waiting at the top of the stairs, in a bowler hat and a state of great excitement. He shook her father’s hand for a long time, and kissed her mother’s, and then turned his beaming face on Lyra and Draco.

“And these are the children! At Hogwarts, aren’t you? Both of you?”

“Lyra is,” said her mother. “Draco goes next year.”

“Well, well.” Fudge winked at Lyra, enormously. “Lucky things. You’re in for something rather special this year, you know. Very special indeed. But I mustn’t say.”

Draco, who had decided that this was a deliberate insult to anybody who would not be at Hogwarts until next year, glared at the Minister for Magic with such concentrated loathing that Fudge spent the next three flights of stairs trying to win him round with talk of Bulgarian Chasers.

Lyra hardly heard any of it.

*Especially next year. If students come from other schools.* Potter had said it to her in May, out of breath, with a ring in his hand, and she had been too angry at the time to ask what on earth he meant by it. She had put it away to be wondered about later. Now the Minister for Magic was winking at her about something special at Hogwarts that he mustn’t say, and he was the Minister, and it was his business to know. Potter was fourteen, and it was not.

She had no time to go on with that, because they had reached the top box.

It was a small, beautifully placed box at the very top of the stands, exactly halfway between the goals, with two rows of purple and gilt chairs; and the front row was full of Weasleys.

Lyra stopped in the doorway.

There were a great many of them, in a row, red-headed and shabby, and Granger at the end in a jumper, and in the middle of them all, with his elbows on the rail and a pair of ordinary brass omnioculars round his neck, was Potter.

She had brought the good omnioculars, the gold ones, for the express purpose of finding him somewhere in the cheap seats on the far side of the stadium. He was sitting one row in front of her.

“Arthur,” said Fudge, with great heartiness. “You’ll know Lucius, of course. He’s made a most generous donation to St Mungo’s. I asked him along myself.”

Mr Weasley and her father greeted each other with the expressions of two men who had each been handed something unpleasant to hold. Her father led his family past the Weasleys to their seats, and as Lyra went by, Potter looked round.

He nodded to her. It was a perfectly civil nod, the kind one gave to somebody from school whose name one could just about remember, and then he turned back to the pitch.

That was what they had agreed. Not in front of people. Not anywhere anybody could see. Lyra had told him at the time exactly what she thought of the arrangement, and then walked off with his ring on her finger, which came to the same thing as agreeing. It was quite sensible, and she would have been extremely annoyed if he had done anything else.

She turned her head away sharply and went and sat down.

It was still a perfectly horrible nod.

Beside her, her mother arranged her robes, and looked at Lyra, and then, for a little longer, at the back of the row in front.

The Bulgarian mascots came out first, and they were veela.

Lyra had read about veela. She had not been prepared for a hundred of them at once. They came gliding out onto the pitch in a long line, women who were not women, with skin that shone as if the moon were somewhere behind it and hair that moved silver-gold about their shoulders with no wind to move it, and then the music started and they began to dance. Within a minute every male in the stadium had lost his mind. In the box below, a Ministry wizard was taking off his hat and putting it on again, very quickly, as though he could not decide which suited him better. In the row in front, Ron Weasley had one leg over the rail and appeared to be getting ready to dive.

Lyra looked at all of it with deep and untroubled contempt.

Then she thought, *wait*, and looked at Potter.

He had his omnioculars up to his eyes. He was leaning forward with both elbows on the rail, perfectly still, quite absorbed, and the corner of his mouth had gone up, and stayed up. If the music was doing anything to him, it did not show.

Lyra’s mouth went thin.

“Lyra,” said Draco dreamily, from her other side, “when I grow up I’m going to marry a veela.”

She cuffed him round the back of the head. It was not for any reason. It was simply that somebody in the family had to keep their wits about them, and the music had stopped.

In the row in front, Potter made a small snorting noise into his omnioculars.

The Irish mascots were leprechauns, a great cloud of them, glittering green and gold, and they rained gold coins down on the crowd until the stands rang with it. Lyra thought them every bit as vulgar as the veela. Weasley caught an armful of coins and pressed a fistful into Potter’s hand with a face of shining joy.

“There you go, Harry! For the omnioculars! *Ten Galleons*—”

Potter nodded without looking round and put the gold in his pocket. He still had the omnioculars up. He was still smiling. Watching the veela again, Lyra supposed, on the replay.

Then the match began, and for a while even Lyra forgot about Potter.

She had never seen flying like it. The Irish Chasers moved like one person in three bodies, and the ball went between them so fast she lost it twice in the first minute. They were ten goals up before the Bulgarians had worked out where they were, and twenty before long, and it went on like that, pass after pass, all afternoon. Krum alone was worth the price of the box. He went after the Irish Seeker over and over, dragging him about the sky, and once he did the Wronski Feint for real, and the Irishman went into the ground, and the whole stadium stood up.

It was not enough. Ireland were a hundred and sixty points ahead when Krum caught the Snitch, and everybody in the stadium could count. He had caught it knowing that it would lose them the match by ten points. He had caught it anyway, to finish it himself, rather than go on being beaten slowly.

Lyra, who had once let go of her broom in a thunderstorm rather than win a match the wrong way, found that she quite understood him, and clenched her fist on the rail.

“Oh, *no*, I missed it!” Weasley swung round on Potter. “Harry, did you get that? Krum catching it? Give us a look—”

He took the omnioculars off Potter’s neck before Potter could stop him, put them to his eyes and pressed the replay. Then he went quite still.

After a moment he took them away from his face and handed them back, with an extraordinary expression.

“Mate,” he said. “The *whole* match?”

“Krum got the Snitch?” said Potter. “So who won?”

“You really didn’t see any of it,” said Weasley, and shook his head slowly at him, as at somebody past help. “You absolute nutter.”

He went off down the row to Granger. Potter scratched the back of his neck and said nothing at all.

Lyra had no idea what any of that was about. She put it down to Weasley.

Her father did not come back to the tent with them.

He saw the three of them inside the tent, which had four rooms and a chandelier and Pobble’s supper laid out on a table that had certainly never been near a field before, and then he put on his travelling cloak, which was not the cloak he had worn to the match.

“Stay in the campsite,” he said. “All of you. I have some old friends to see.” He kissed Narcissa’s cheek, and Lyra’s forehead, and ruffled Draco’s hair, and went out, and the tent flap fell shut behind him.

Her mother looked at the flap for a moment after it had stopped moving. Then she turned back to the table and asked Draco whether he wanted the chicken or the salmon.

Draco wanted to talk. He wanted to talk about Krum, and the Wronski Feint, and the Irish Chasers, and Krum again, and he was so full of the match that it seemed to be leaking out of him at the edges; and since Lyra was the only other person in the tent who had ever been on a broom, it was mostly leaking at her. She answered the first twenty questions. She answered the next twenty in fewer words. By the fiftieth she was answering in single syllables, and Draco, who had found some inexhaustible new supply of breath, did not appear to have noticed.

“He sounds,” said her mother, “like a mandrake that has just been pulled up. Go out and get some air, darling. I’ll see to him.”

“Thank you, Mother. I was beginning to think somebody ought to put a sock in him.”

“Don’t go far. We may be leaving early.”

Outside, this end of the campsite was keeping up appearances. It was where the Ministry put people who mattered, and while there were fireworks going up over the far fields and somebody a long way off was singing the Irish anthem very badly, here the tents had striped awnings and the celebrating was being done with dignity. An old wizard in a velvet hat was explaining the match to two people who had also seen it. Some witches in a great deal of jewellery were drinking mead and shrieking, but witches like that shrieked everywhere.

Lyra walked a little way along the path between the tents, out of the light from her own, and took the ring out of her pocket, and put it on.

It went pink.

*In the company of their sweetheart.*

Lyra looked at it. It went on being pink. She looked up at the fireworks, and then back down at the ring, and it was still pink, and her mood, which had been improving, stopped improving.

So that was where Potter had got to. With his sweetheart. Somewhere in this campsite, while Lyra stood here in the dark holding a ring, Potter was with somebody, and the ring had the effrontery to tell her so. One of the veela, presumably. They had been very much his sort of thing, to judge by the omnioculars.

It had done this once before, she remembered. In February, on the night of the full moon, it had gone red and then pink and then red again, back and forth, as if it could not decide, and she had not understood why until afterwards: that there had been two of Potter that night, one lying on the shore, and one in the castle, in the same moment, under a cloak, standing beside—

Lyra stopped walking.

Pink. *In the company of.*

“Harry?” she said, to the air.

“Long time,” said the air, very close to her ear, and something cool and silvery fell over her head and shoulders, and the world went on exactly as it had been, except that now there was Potter standing in it, an inch away, grinning.

It was very peculiar. A minute ago she had had a great deal to say to him, all of it sharp. Now that he was actually there she found that the sharp things had gone somewhere and what was left was a ridiculous urge to walk forward into him, forehead first, and stay there. Her pride caught it just in time. Lyra Malfoy did not do that sort of thing, and she was certainly not going to be the first to do it. She folded her arms.

“Idiot. You took your time.”

“Sorry. Did I keep you waiting?”

“How did you even find my tent?”

“Followed you under this. I couldn’t exactly come in, so I’ve been out here.” He considered. “For a bit.”

It occurred to Lyra that the one who had been kept waiting was not, strictly speaking, her.

“Draco,” she said, “is a great deal of work. It took some time to get away.”

“Your brother? He’s loud, isn’t he.”

“He hasn’t stopped since the Snitch.”

Potter’s arm came round her shoulders. She knocked it away with the back of her hand.

She was very glad to see him. There was, however, a matter to be settled first. She had not forgotten a boy at a rail with his omnioculars glued to his face, smiling.

“You couldn’t take your eyes off the veela,” she said. “Were they really so very wonderful?”

Potter looked honestly baffled. “I don’t know much about magical creatures,” he said. “I know one with platinum hair who makes men stupid, though. She’s standing right here.”

“Do you really imagine you can get out of it like that?”

And then she saw them, hanging from his wrist by their strap: the omnioculars. With replay.

She had them off him before he could move. She held them up and found the dial and turned it back, and back, to the beginning of the match.

“I will not be lied to,” she informed him, with her eye to the lenses. “If you think you can talk your way round me with a lot of pretty—”

She stopped.

Only one setting had been on all afternoon. It was the one that followed a single player round the pitch, whatever happened, and kept them in the middle of the picture.

It had not been following a player.

There was Lyra Malfoy with her arms folded, glaring at the back of somebody’s head. There was Lyra Malfoy cuffing her little brother round the ear, and sighing, and smoothing her robes. There was Lyra Malfoy leaning forward when the match began, with her lips parted and her eyes very bright, and Lyra Malfoy half out of her seat when Krum did the Feint, and Lyra Malfoy with her fist clenched on the rail as Krum caught the Snitch. Two hours of it. Every second.

They had been made for watching Quidditch at the World Cup, the greatest match of the age, with half the wizarding world in the stands. He had watched her.

Lyra lowered the omnioculars, very slowly. Potter, who had obviously been waiting for exactly this, grinned.

“Accusing people without any evidence,” he said. “That’s not very fair, Malfoy. There are laws about that, you know.”

She looked at the omnioculars in her hands. Then she hung them round her own neck.

“These,” said Lyra, “are evidence. I’m keeping them.”

“Ron paid me ten Galleons for those.”

“Leprechaun gold. It’ll be gone by morning. I saw.” She folded her arms over them. “And as for the false accusation, you may put it on my account.”

“I’d rather settle now,” said Potter.

His arm came round her shoulders again, and this time she let it stay. He had sat through the World Cup final and not watched a minute of it. He had watched her instead, for two hours, and she was so embarrassed about that and so pleased about it that she could not tell the two apart, and she had not been alone with him since May. His hand came up, slowly, to her face, and she found that she had lifted her face to meet it, and shut her eyes.

“Lyra?” Her mother’s voice, from the door of the tent. “Your father’s sent word, we— Now where has she got to?”

Lyra’s eyes flew open.

She was out of his arms in an instant, pushing his omnioculars down inside her robes, putting her hair back. Under a cloak, of course, but even so. *Here*, of all places, a few yards from her mother.

“Too many people about,” murmured Potter, in the voice of somebody who had been given a reasonable answer and did not care for it. “Shame.”

It was then, with her mother’s voice still in the air, that the rest of the evening came back to her: a travelling cloak that was not the one he had worn to the match, and the tent flap falling shut, and *old friends*. Potter had once told her, in an empty classroom, exactly what sort of old friends her father had.

“Harry,” she said. “My father went out. He changed his cloak first. Where are they all going?”

He did not make a joke of it. That was how she knew it was a real question. His face changed, under the cloak, in the dark, into the one she did not like, and then he put it away again, and what was left was only tired.

“Go back to your mum,” he said. “Stay with her tonight. Don’t go wandering about.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No,” said Potter. “It isn’t.” He put his hand on her hair, very briefly, and leaned in, and kissed her forehead.

Then the cloak slid off her shoulders and he was gone inside it, a ripple in the dark between two tents.

“See you at school,” said the ripple.

Lyra stood on the path, perfectly visible, with her face burning and the ring on her finger shining pink.

She put her hand in her pocket just as her mother came round the corner of the tent.

Her mother stopped. She looked at Lyra’s face for some time in the light from the awning. Then her eyes went down, to the pocket, and stayed there a moment longer, and came back up.

“There you are,” said Narcissa Malfoy. “We’re going home. Your father will follow.”

They went back by the Ministry fireplace, the three of them, with Draco protesting all the way that it was not fair and nobody else was going to bed.

Lyra was the last into the grate. As she stepped in, with the Floo powder in her hand, she heard the noise from the far fields change. There was still shouting, but it was not singing any more, and it was not cheering, and somewhere under it a great many people had begun to run. In her pocket, under her fingers, the ring was growing warm.

Her mother did not look round.
