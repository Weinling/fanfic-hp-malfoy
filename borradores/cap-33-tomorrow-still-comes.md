# Chapter 33: The Corner Table

Potter could not dance.

Lyra had suspected it, and she found out in the first eight bars. He did not tread on her. He was so determined not to tread on her that he did very little else; he moved his feet about underneath them both like a man crossing a stream on stones he did not trust, and steered her round the floor by a series of small apologetic shoves, and looked down, now and then, to see where everything had got to.

“Left,” said Lyra, smiling for the Hall.

“I am going left.”

“Your other left.”

She had been taught to waltz at five years old, in the long room at the Manor, by a little man with a fiddle who rapped her ankles with his bow when she looked at her feet. She did not look at her feet. She took the lead from underneath, without appearing to, so that from the tables it would look as if Harry Potter had been dancing all his life, and after a while he noticed what she was doing and stopped fighting it, and they went round rather better.

It was the middle of the Great Hall on Christmas night, with the frost glittering on the walls and every eye in the room on her, and it was what she had pictured on the night of the dragons, nearly to the last pin; and she was not thinking about the room.

“Why did you do that?” said Potter, under the music.

“Do what?”

“You know what.”

She turned under his arm, and came back.

“Because I had no intention of being left over in front of Slytherin,” said Lyra, “like the odd glove in a drawer. And because I had three days.”

He did not say anything for a few bars.

“Your father—”

“My father reads what I decide he reads.”

It came out with all the right weight, and she very nearly believed it herself. Over Potter’s shoulder, at the judges’ table, Karkaroff was talking to nobody, with his hand on his left forearm.

“I had it worked out,” said Potter. “You’d have been—”

“Safe. Yes. I saw. It was very well done.” She turned his right hand a little in hers, so that the cuff slid back from the wrist. Under the lanterns the mark was a dull, angry red, raised like a weal, running right round the bone. “What is that?”

“That?” He glanced at it. “Proof of hard work?”

She looked at it, and then at him.

He had been somewhere. For three days, while she sat in a library and stood in a corridor and walked up a gangplank in the snow to be refused, he had been somewhere doing something that left marks like that on a person, and she had not known. She had not been told. Nobody would have told her if he had not come back.

And she could not even be angry with him properly. He had gone off and done something dangerous for everybody, for the whole school, for her, and there was nothing she could say against that which would not make her small. She could not stand in the middle of the Great Hall and complain that he had been brave without asking her first.

“Lyra,” said Potter. His voice had changed. “I’m sor—”

He stopped.

She felt him stop. It went through his hand and his shoulder and his feet, which lost the beat entirely, so that for a step or two she was steering a boy who had forgotten he was in a waltz.

Lyra knew why. *Never apologise*, her father said, *it hands the other party the pen.*

“I should have told you,” he said instead.

“That isn’t an apology.”

“No. You’d only have to forgive me.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“I know,” said Potter, and smiled, properly, for the first time since the doorway.

Lyra stepped on his foot. She did it neatly, with her heel, on the beat, and he made a small noise and did not let go of her, and she felt a good deal better.

The music ended. The Hall clapped, the way halls do for champions, and Potter stood in the middle of it and went on holding her hand for some while after the clapping had stopped, which was very bad for her composure and, she thought, for his reputation.

“Malfoy.”

It was Granger, at her elbow, pink from dancing, with Krum behind her like a bear that had been put into dress robes against its will.

“There’s a table,” said Granger. “In the corner. If you like.”

The table in the corner was half hidden by a Christmas tree hung with live fairies, and it was the only one in the Hall that nobody else had wanted, since it was a long way from the food. Granger sat down with the air of somebody who had chosen it on purpose.

“Butterbeer?” said Potter.

“I will come,” said Krum.

They went off together across the floor, and Lyra watched them go: the youngest champion and the oldest, side by side, neither of them saying a word. They had got about halfway to the drinks when Professor Karkaroff rose from the judges’ table, came down the steps with surprising speed for a man in so much fur, and took Krum by the elbow. Potter, who knew a trap when he saw one, kept walking.

Lyra looked at the judges’ table. Madame Maxime, Bagman, Dumbledore, and Karkaroff’s empty chair; and at the end, in the place where Mr Crouch of the Ministry ought to have been sitting, a tall young man with horn-rimmed glasses and red hair, very upright, explaining something to Madame Maxime that she was not listening to.

“Ron’s brother,” said Granger, following her eyes. “Percy. He works for Mr Crouch. He’s been telling everybody all evening that Mr Crouch is overworked and sends his apologies.”

“How very loyal of him.”

“He thinks Mr Crouch is the most marvellous man in the Ministry.” Granger’s mouth went thin. “He *sacked his elf*. At the World Cup. She hadn’t done anything, she was only frightened, and he gave her clothes in front of everybody, and she’s down in the kitchens now, and she cries all the time. Ron’s brother thinks that’s quite right. That’s why I started—” She stopped, and looked at the badge Lyra was not wearing. “Never mind.”

Lyra thought of Pobble crying for a week in the scullery at the Manor, and for once had nothing ready about elves; and Granger was looking at her as if she might, so she changed the subject.

“And Weasley?”

“Oh.” Granger glanced across the Hall to a table near the band, where Weasley sat on his own in a set of dress robes that had plainly belonged to somebody’s great-aunt, glaring at Krum’s back. She shrugged. “He’s a big boy.”

“Is he.”

“Mostly.”

Then Granger turned back and looked at her, and went on looking, with her chin in her hand and an expression Lyra did not care for in the least. It was the expression of a girl who has been handed a very small, very cross kitten and is trying not to laugh at it.

“What?”

“Nothing,” said Granger, and became very interested in her butterbeer.

Lyra let that go, since she could see exactly what lay at the end of it.

“Did you know?” she said instead. “What he was doing. The plan.”

“Only that I wasn’t to tell anybody who I was going with. He asked me a month ago. I thought he meant because of the *Prophet*.” Granger took her hands down. “I didn’t know you’d— I didn’t know about the rest of it. He didn’t tell us anything. He told us he was off with his godfather for a few days and not to fuss.”

“He moved me about,” said Lyra, “like furniture.”

Granger stopped smiling.

“Not a word. And then I didn’t know for three days whether he was— whether he was anywhere at all.” She picked up her bottle, and put it down again. “I’ve given him a truce. That’s all. A short one.”

Granger thought about that.

“It did help, though,” she said. “A bit. Didn’t it.”

“Not in the least.”

“You didn’t come with anybody else,” said Granger. “Even when you thought he wasn’t coming. You came by yourself.”

“There was nobody else worth the trouble.”

“No,” said Granger. “Of course not.”

Lyra turned and looked at the fairies on the Christmas tree for a while, since they were the only things in the room that were not looking at her as though she were a kitten.

“Why did he say it?” she asked the tree. “Krum.”

Granger did not pretend not to understand. She looked across the Hall to where Karkaroff had Krum backed against a pillar and was talking at him very fast, and Krum was listening with the patient, unmoved face of a man waiting for a storm to finish.

“I don’t know. I didn’t ask him to.” She hesitated. “He said once that you fly like a Bulgarian. I think it’s the nicest thing he knows how to say about anybody.”

Potter came back on his own, with three bottles of butterbeer held in a triangle between his fingers and an air of having escaped from something.

“Karkaroff’s got Krum,” he said, sitting down next to Lyra. “I don’t think he’s getting him back before midnight.”

Lyra took her bottle, and drank from it, and set it down on the table rather hard.

“I’m waiting.”

“For what?”

She looked at him.

“Right,” said Potter. He glanced round. The tables nearest them had been glancing back all evening, and at the next one along a Hufflepuff fifth-year was leaning so far out of her chair to listen that her partner had a hand on her sash. “There are a lot of people about.”

Lyra slid her wand out of her sleeve, where it had lain along the inside of her wrist all evening under the violet silk, and gave it a small flick under the table.

“*Muffliato.*”

There was a faint buzzing in the air round the three of them, like a bee in a jar, and then nothing. The Hufflepuff fifth-year frowned, and shook her head as if she had water in her ear, and gave up.

Potter looked impressed in spite of himself. He leaned out a little and said, quite loudly, “Seamus, your flies are undone,” to a boy at the next table but one, who did not so much as glance down.

“Where did you learn that?”

“From my father,” said Lyra. “Talk.”

He talked.

He did not tell it well, and he did not tell it long. He told them about Moody, and the Imperius he had thought was on him, and a trunk with seven locks, and a place in it the map did not show.

“How did you—” Granger began.

“I just thought it.” He went on before she could ask anything else. Wednesday night; Sirius; the seventh lock; his wand lit over a shaft with nobody at the bottom of it, looking for—he did not say what. *Somebody who wasn’t there*, he said. And Moody behind them in the dark, with the map.

“He thought it was me,” said Potter. “Under the Imperius. A champion who breaks into his office at midnight with Sirius Black and goes through his trunk looking for somebody who isn’t there.” He made a short, unhappy noise that was not quite a laugh. “I’d have thought so too.”

Three days, he said. Dumbledore had got back that afternoon and had them out by six. He turned his wrist over on the table, so that the welt showed, and did not explain it; and Lyra, who had asked once already, did not ask again.

“And Moody?” said Lyra.

“Moody’s Moody.” Potter looked at his bottle. “He’s just Moody.”

On Friday, on the fourth floor, Moody had stood in the grey light and asked her what her ring was showing. *I know where he isn’t.* He had, too. He had known to the inch. Lyra put her hand flat on the tablecloth and kept it there.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

“You told us,” said Granger, very quietly, “that you were going somewhere with Dumbledore.”

“I know. I’m sorry, Hermione.” He rubbed his face. “If I’d found him, I’d have had to take him straight to Dumbledore, wherever Dumbledore was. I didn’t know how long it would be. I didn’t want you two looking for me.”

He said it to her easily, the whole word, and Granger nodded and looked at the tablecloth.

“Where’s Sirius now?” she said.

“Gone. Dumbledore saw to it. Before Moody had finished shouting.”

It was the *Prophet*, then. She could see it now. Something in that article had sent him down the seventh lock in the middle of the night, with Dumbledore away; something about a voice in a tent that nobody had seen. And Moody could see through a cloak. Moody, of all the people in this castle, could have stood outside that tent and seen exactly who was inside it.

She had been carrying the answer for a month. She sat up a little straighter, and let herself enjoy it.

“Moody can see through a cloak, Potter,” said Lyra. “He can’t see into a beetle.”

Potter looked up.

“There was a beetle in the tent. On the canvas, near the roof, the whole time. It went past my ear when I ran out.” She folded her hands on the table. “It was Skeeter. She’s an Animagus. She isn’t on any register, or she couldn’t do it; Black wasn’t, and nor was Pettigrew. She sits on the wall and listens, and then she makes up a charm to explain how.”

“I know,” said Potter.

Granger said, “She’s *what*? She can’t be. There have only been seven registered this century, I looked them up in third year, there’s a list, and she isn’t—” and stopped, because she had heard herself.

Lyra did not look at Granger. She was looking at Potter.

She had worked it out on a wet path with her collar up, from a beetle and a stupid lie and two men who had spent years in fur, and she had told nobody, not even Crabbe, and had been pleased with it every day for a month; and he had known.

“Then you knew it wasn’t Moody,” she said. “Listening. In the tent.”

“Yes.”

“So why did you go after Moody at all?”

Potter turned the butterbeer bottle round on the tablecloth, once, and then again.

“I was sure,” he said.

That was all. He did not say what of, or how. Lyra waited, and he did not say anything else, and after a while she took the two things, *I know* and *I was sure*, and set them down side by side somewhere inside her, next to a question she had worked on all one summer on a window seat at the Manor, about a diary and a number seven and a house up north, and had never asked; and left them there.

The buzzing round the table broke like a bubble. Professor Dumbledore was standing beside the Christmas tree, in his plum-coloured robes, with his hands folded in front of him and the fairies lighting up his beard.

“Forgive me, Miss Malfoy,” he said. “I must borrow your partner. Madam Pomfrey has been very patient, and that arm has been out of her hands for long enough.”

Lyra turned and looked at Potter. Potter found something interesting in the Christmas tree.

“It’s a curse, I’m afraid,” said Dumbledore, quite pleasantly, to the table at large. “The plant in question was confiscated from a gentleman in Lima a long time ago, and it has had a great many years in the dark to think about its grievances. If it is left, the mark will stay.”

“Professor,” said Potter, “I’ve only got the one scar. I was thinking of starting a collection.”

“Hospital wing,” said Lyra. “Now. If you come back with that on your arm for good, Potter, I shan’t look at it, and I shan’t look at you either.”

Potter got up. He did it slowly, like somebody leaving a fire on a cold night, and looked down at her as though there were a great deal more he had meant to say and the evening had run out first.

Dumbledore was looking at her too.

She had met that look once, in a corridor, with Pettigrew trussed beside them. This time it rested on her, mild and in no hurry, then on Potter in exactly the same way, then on her again; and it was asking something. She had no idea what, and she did not think, from his face, that Dumbledore knew the answer either.

Potter was still standing there.

Lyra reached up, took hold of the end of his tie, which had never been straight all evening, and pulled, so that he had to bend down to her.

“You still owe me a tomorrow,” she said, into his ear.

*Go to bed, Malfoy. Tomorrow I’ll tell you everything.* She felt him hear it.

“I hadn’t forgotten,” said Potter.

She let go of the tie. He straightened it, crooked, and went off across the Hall at Dumbledore’s side, looking back twice before the crowd closed over him.

Granger had her chin in her hand again.

“Don’t,” said Lyra.

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were going to.”

“I was going to say,” said Granger, “that I’ve never seen anybody tell Harry to go to the hospital wing and have him actually go.” She picked up her butterbeer. “You should do it more often. It would save Madam Pomfrey a great deal of trouble.”

Lyra drank her own butterbeer, and did not answer, and found she did not mind the corner table quite as much as she had.

Her father’s owl came on the Thursday.

It came down the length of the Great Hall at breakfast, the big eagle owl from the Manor with the white ruff, and it landed beside her plate as though the plate had been laid for it, and held out its leg, and waited. Half the Slytherin table stopped eating to watch her take the letter off. It had been thirty days. She had not counted them; she simply knew.

She read it at the table, because not reading it would have been a statement, and she was not ready to make one.

*My dear Lyra,*

*I have read the article in the Daily Prophet of the twenty-ninth of November. It was vulgar, and inaccurate, and beneath the notice of anyone in this family, and I have taken steps. The woman will not be writing for that paper again. You need give her no further thought.*

*I hear, too, that you were at the Ball. I hear a good deal, even at this distance.*

*I find that I am not displeased. You will have your reasons, and I imagine they are good ones; you have always had a head for where to stand. A boy who confides is a boy worth sitting beside. I shall be interested to hear, in due course, what he confides.*

*Your mother sends her love.*

*Your father.*

Lyra read it twice, and folded it along its creases, and put it in her pocket.

He had not forbidden her anything. She had been braced for a month for the letter that forbade her, and had her answer ready, cold and short and very well spelt; and he had not written it. He had written this instead. *A boy worth sitting beside.* As though she had been clever, and he would like, in due course, to be shown what she had been clever about.

She had used her father that way herself, in November, with a sealed letter and a ship. She knew precisely what it looked like from the other side.

She did not answer it.

Her mother’s letter came that evening, by the grey owl, when Lyra was alone in the dormitory with the hangings drawn. It was short. It was not in the hand with the loops.

*You did not tell me. I would rather have heard it from you.*

There was nothing else on the page: no news, no love, no signature, only that, in the small upright hand that had chosen the violet, and the white space round it.

Lyra sat with it on her knee for a long time.

Then she took down her Arithmancy book, and opened it at the back, behind the tables of values, where the papers were: a scrap with two elevens on it, and a card about a colour, and a strip that had been folded and unfolded so often it had gone soft as cloth. She unfolded it once more.

*P.S. Who is asking? You may tell me anything. I would rather hear it from you.*

Fourteen months. She had carried it through two years and two houses, behind the tables of values and in the inside pocket of her robes, against her ribs, and she had never once answered it, because there had been no answer she could give that did not hand her mother something she could not take back.

She found a quill. She smoothed the strip out flat on the cover of the book, and underneath her mother’s question, in her own hand, as small and as upright as she could make it, she wrote:

*It was Harry Potter who was asking.*

She sent it in the morning, with a school owl, so that it would not go past her father’s window.
