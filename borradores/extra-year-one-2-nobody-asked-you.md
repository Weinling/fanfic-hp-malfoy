# Year One, Part 2: Nobody Asked You for Christmas

“Harry Potter,” said Lyra. “How *dare* he.”

She said it to the snow, mostly, because she was walking very fast across the lawn away from Hagrid’s hut and the snow was what was in front of her. Crabbe and Goyle were having to trot to keep up.

It had been a complete rout. She had not had one word to say back. She had turned and fled, and the fact of having fled at all was enough, all by itself, to make her want to scream. A Malfoy did not run from anybody. A Malfoy, at the very least, walked away at a pace of her own choosing, and she had very nearly picked up her robes and run.

He had just *said* it. The Dark Lord’s name, out loud, in the snow, in front of a gamekeeper and a Weasley, as if it were anybody’s.

“He thinks he’s so clever,” said Lyra. “Going about with a blood traitor, and him nothing but an orphan himself. He’s going to pay for this.”

“But how?” said Goyle doubtfully. “If he says—you know—*His* name again…”

That was the trouble. Lyra and Crabbe and Goyle had grown up in houses where that name was not said, by parents who had stood on the Dark Lord’s side before he fell, and the fear of it had gone into them so early it was nearly reverence. Harry Potter said it the way other people asked for the salt, and there was nothing whatever they could do about it: they could not say it back. Something in them pulled away every time, like a hand from a stove.

It was a curse you could not block.

“Even Weasley jumped,” said Goyle gloomily. “When he said it. So it’s not just us.”

Lyra stopped walking so suddenly that Goyle ran into her.

“Say that again.”

“Weasley jumped?”

A slow smile came over Lyra’s face. “Potter’s the only one stupid enough to say the name. So we leave him alone, and go for everybody round him.”

“Poor little blood-traitor Weasley. Know-it-all Granger. And Longbottom, who can’t remember his own name. There’s plenty to go round. We’ll show them what happens when they make us angry.”

“Oh, *yes!*” Goyle bounced on her toes in the snow. “They’re all useless! That’s brilliant, Malfoy!”

Lyra’s nose went up a little. She waited for more applause, and it did not come. Crabbe was standing there with a blank look on her face.

“Crabbe? What are you mooning about?”

“What? Oh—um.”

“Have you got a better idea?” said Lyra, with a slight edge.

“No. No, it’s not that.”

“What, then? Say it.”

Crabbe went pink. She was round-faced still, that year, with her hair in the two plaits her mother made her wear, and when she went pink it went all the way up into her parting.

“It’s only… his parents are dead. Isn’t it a bit sad?”

“Who?”

“H-Harry Potter.”

Lyra screwed up her face. So that was it. Crabbe was feeling sorry for the enemy.

*Defending* Harry Potter, to Lyra’s face?

Father said there was no such thing as a small disloyalty, only ones that had not grown yet. But this was Crabbe, who had sat next to her at the Sorting and had never once disagreed with her about anything; so Lyra took a breath and was reasonable.

“Don’t be absurd, Crabbe. What has that got to do with us?”

“But he said it as if it was nothing. Both of them, gone. He just *said* it.”

“Oh, don’t be ridiculous. Not having parents. As if that were such a great matter.”

She turned and walked on towards the castle, and did not look to see whether they followed.

⁂

Since October, Harry had been the best at something, and it had gone to his head a bit.

Oliver Wood had shown him the Golden Snitch one Wednesday evening on the empty pitch, with the light going, and it had taken Harry under five minutes to catch it. At Hallowe’en he and Ron, with a lot of luck and a bit of Hermione, had knocked out a mountain troll in a girls’ lavatory. In his first match his broom had gone mad underneath him fifty feet up, and then stopped, and he had caught the Snitch more or less in his mouth, and Gryffindor had beaten Slytherin, and the rest of the school had cheered him all the way back to the castle. In the Slytherin stand there had been one fair head that had had to look up with everybody else.

Malfoy took it personally. She took to being horrible to him and Ron whenever she got the chance, in corridors, at meals, outside lessons, as though she could make the cheering stop.

A week before Christmas, he and Ron were helping Hagrid drag a fir tree up to the castle when a cold, sneering voice came from behind them.

“Trying to earn a bit of extra money, Weasley? Hoping to be gamekeeper yourself when you leave, I suppose? That hut of Hagrid’s must look like a palace to you, after what your family’s used to.”

She stood there in her good winter cloak, with Crabbe and Goyle at her shoulders, looking at Ron as if he were something she had stepped in.

“I expect your family’d be glad of one less mouth to feed. Wouldn’t they?”

Ron went for her. Harry caught him by the back of his robes just in time.

“Leave it. If you go for her, she’ll only enjoy it more.”

“But she—my *family*—”

“That’s what she wants, Ron. Don’t let her have it.”

Malfoy clicked her tongue, and looked past Ron at Harry.

“Well, at least *you’ve* got somewhere to go, Weasley. Some people have nobody who wants them at Christmas, so they have to stay at school.”

It was meant for Harry, and everybody knew it. Ron’s face went from red to purple. Hagrid, at the front end of the tree, had stopped pulling.

Harry patted Ron on the shoulder, and walked straight up to her.

She was looking enormously pleased with herself, because it had worked. It was a bright, childish, superior sort of smile, like a cat’s with a bird under one paw. And Harry, who had had that sort of thing said to him at number four for as long as he could remember and had never once been allowed to answer back, found that he was not angry at all, only, suddenly and enormously, *free*.

He stopped in front of her, and to everybody’s astonishment, he smiled back. It was a perfectly friendly smile. He had been practising it since August.

“Can’t be helped,” he said. “My mum and dad died stopping Voldemort.”

Malfoy flinched as though he had thrown something at her. So did Crabbe and Goyle; so, out of the corner of Harry’s eye, did Ron. All three Slytherins took a step back.

Harry had grown up among Muggles, where a name was just a name, and he had not known until that moment what this one could do. He watched it go through the three of them like a draught under a door, and he took another step.

“And because of them, Voldemort’s gone. Isn’t that what everybody says? So everybody ought to be grateful to my mum and dad. Shouldn’t they? You too, Malfoy.”

Harry kept smiling, and finished it.

“Or do you hate me? For getting rid of Voldemort?”

Malfoy turned on her heel and almost ran. Crabbe and Goyle scurried after her across the snow.

Ron, who seemed to have forgotten he had ever been angry, came bounding up.

“Harry, that was *brilliant!* You shut *Malfoy* up!”

“It was nothing much.”

Harry watched her go, her fair hair bright against all that white.

“You just answer back straight, and smile first. Then you win.”

“That’s what’s brilliant about it! She says all that, and you *smile* at her. How do you even do that?”

“Dunno,” said Harry. “You get used to it?”

Back in the common room, Ron told everybody how Harry had seen Malfoy off. A great many of them had had a bad time from Malfoy, and they all wanted to hear it twice. Neville, who had had the worst time of all, looked at Harry as though he were a hero out of a book.

Harry found he could not enjoy it much.

When everybody hated something that you liked, it turned out not to be as much fun as you would think. Not even when it made you a hero.

Ron was going home on Saturday, with the rest of the Weasleys. Harry had put his name on McGonagall’s list of people staying the day it went up. He had never in his life been so glad to be left somewhere.

The letter came on Friday, at breakfast, by the eagle owl her father had given her in June.

It came down the Great Hall low and slow, as her father had trained it to, and landed between the toast and the marmalade. Lyra opened the letter at the table. A letter from home was a thing one opened in public.

*My treasure,*

*Your mother and I are called away on business over the holidays, and it will keep us abroad until the New Year. It is tiresome, and it cannot be helped. You will be a great deal more comfortable at school than rattling about the Manor with nobody but the elves, and Draco will be looked after.*

*Something will come for you on Christmas morning. I hear Gryffindor had the better of Slytherin last month. Work hard, and do not let it happen again.*

*Your loving father*

She read it twice. Then she folded it in half, and in half again, and once more, until it was a small hard square that would go into the palm of one hand, and she put it in her pocket.

“Is that from home?” said Goyle. “Are they sending the carriage, or are you on the train with us?”

“Neither,” said Lyra. “I’ve decided to stay.”

Goyle’s mouth fell open, with porridge in it.

“Somebody ought to keep an eye on Potter over the holidays.” Lyra buttered a piece of toast with great precision. “You can’t leave a boy like that alone in a castle for a fortnight. He’ll take it apart.”

Goyle closed her mouth and looked at her with something close to worship. Crabbe did not say anything. She looked at Lyra, and at the pocket the letter had gone into, and went back to her porridge.

In the dormitory that night Crabbe sat on the end of her own bed in her nightdress and said, to the floor, “I could stay as well. I could write to my mum.”

“Your mother would have kittens,” said Lyra. “And there’s no need.”

“I know there isn’t,” said Crabbe, and did not write.

On Saturday morning Lyra put on her cloak and went out onto the front steps, as though she had come out for the air.

The carriages were drawn up along the drive in the snow, with nothing in front of them that anybody could see, and the whole school seemed to be climbing into them at once. The Slytherins went in a long green knot. Goyle waved both arms. Crabbe’s round face came to the window of the third carriage and stayed there, looking up at the steps, until the carriage moved and took it away.

At the bottom of the steps Potter was seeing Weasley off. He had his hands in his pockets and no hat, and he was laughing at something Weasley shouted out of the window; and then the carriage was gone and he stood there on his own in the trodden snow, looking after it, and did not seem to mind. He turned round. Lyra went back inside before he could look up.

She was halfway across the Entrance Hall when it came back to her. It came back in her own voice, exactly as she had said it under the fir tree, with Crabbe and Goyle at her shoulders and everybody listening.

*Some people have nobody who wants them at Christmas, so they have to stay at school.*

She had chosen every word of it with great care. She stood in the middle of the empty hall, where anybody might have seen her, and could not walk on until she had stopped hearing it.

⁂

That night the dormitory was dark and very quiet, with four beds in it that nobody was sleeping in, and the lake moving green and black against the window. Lyra lay with her hangings drawn, the way she always did, though there was nobody to draw them against.

She had been without her parents once before.

It had been when she was eight. Some business had come up, something urgent that nobody explained to her, and both her parents had had to go away at once and could not say when they would be back.

She had come down in the morning to the dining room, and there had been nobody at the long table but her, and the breakfast Dobby had made, laid out in front of one chair. The table had seemed very long that day. The chair was colder than usual. Even the food did not taste of much.

She had eaten a little of it, crossly, and read, and flown about the grounds by herself, and ordered Dobby about for something to do. The family portraits never said anything but dull, old-fashioned things, but that afternoon she had sat and talked to them for a whole hour, and had rather enjoyed it.

Draco had been too small to count. He had cried for their mother at bedtime and been put to bed by Dobby, and Lyra, who was the eldest, had not cried, because somebody had to be the eldest.

She had played chess against herself. She had had her bath, and gone to bed earlier than usual, and lain there with her eyes wide open in the dark, very much as she was lying now.

Nobody knew this. Nobody would ever know it. She had thought, *What if they don’t come back tomorrow either*, and she had very nearly cried after all. Like a Malfoy, she had held it in.

They had come home just before dawn, as it turned out, with a box of sugared violets from her mother’s confectioner, all for her. Her mother had not taken off her travelling cloak, and her father’s wand hand was bandaged, and nobody mentioned either. Lyra had let the violets carry off the whole of that bad day, and had not thought about it since.

*But what if*, she had thought then, lying in the dark at eight years old, *what if that day came every day?*

She had decided it was rubbish. If you had not got parents, you had elves, and relations, and a house. What a fuss about nothing.

It had come, then. Not every day: only at Christmas, and by owl, folded very small. She had the square of it under her pillow, and she did not take it out to read it again, because she knew what it said.

Lyra turned over hard and pulled the blankets up to her ears. The thing that mattered was making Harry Potter sorry, and until then she would allow herself no weakness of any kind. None whatever.

“After Christmas, Potter,” said Lyra, to the dark. “You’ll see.”

On Christmas Eve, a little before midnight, Lyra climbed the stairs to the Owlery on her own.

It was the coldest room in the castle, and the emptiest; the school owls had gone home with the school, and the few that were left sat high up in the rafters with their heads under their wings. The eagle owl came down to her the moment she held out her arm, as it had been taught to, and sat there looking at her with its great orange eyes while she took out a quill.

She had brought a whole sheet of parchment. She had meant to write a proper letter, the kind her father liked, with a paragraph about her marks and one about the weather and her love to Mother at the end. She stood at the window with the parchment against the stone and the quill over it, and the wind coming in off the lake, and could not think of a single thing she wanted to say about the weather.

In the end she wrote two words.

*Please come.*

She looked at them. It was the shortest letter she had ever written, and the only one she had ever written that asked for anything. She did not sign it. There was nobody in the world who would not know whose it was.

She tore the rest of the sheet away and folded what was left very small, and tied it to the owl’s leg. Then she carried it to the window and held up her arm, and the owl went out into the dark over the lake, and she stood there watching the place where it had been until the cold had got all the way through her cloak.

⁂

There was a box at the foot of her bed on Christmas morning.

It was wrapped in silver paper, and inside the silver paper was a box she knew: sugared violets, from her mother’s confectioner in Diagon Alley. On top of the violets lay a card, in her mother’s hand, all loops and flourishes, the way she wrote invitations.

*For Christmas morning.*

That was all. It had been written, Lyra saw, a good while ago. It had been packed and sent before anybody could have read a letter that had gone out over the lake at midnight.

She ate one violet, sitting on the end of her bed in her dressing gown. It tasted exactly as it had when she was eight. Then she put the lid back on the box, and got dressed, and did her hair for twenty minutes, because it was Christmas Day.

⁂

They laid the Slytherin table for Christmas dinner as if the whole House were coming.

The elves had sent up enough for a hundred, and by every single plate there was a Christmas cracker in gold and silver paper. Two seventh-years sat at the far end of the Slytherin table, talking in low voices about somebody’s brother at the Ministry. Lyra sat at the other end. Between them were forty empty places and forty crackers.

At the Gryffindor table the crackers were going off like cannon. Somebody had got a hat with an owl on it that hooted. Somebody else had got a set of wizard chessmen out of one, and was trying to make them play on a plate.

Lyra looked down at her own cracker.

It took two. That was the whole point of a cracker: you held one end and somebody else held the other, and you both pulled. There was nobody at this end of the table to hold the other end. She could have pulled it with both hands, like a child, and everybody would have seen her do it.

She left it where it was.

She ate her dinner, all of it, properly, with her back straight and her elbows in, the turkey and the potatoes and a small helping of everything else, and then the Christmas pudding, because a Malfoy did not leave the Great Hall early on Christmas Day for people to talk about. It took a long time. When at last she stood up, the cracker was still lying by her plate in its gold paper, exactly where the elves had put it.

⁂

Harry had woken up that morning to a pile of presents at the foot of his bed, and had sat looking at it for some time before he understood that it was his.

There was a hand-knitted jumper from Ron’s mother, who had never met him; Ron must have written home that he wasn’t expecting anything. There was a fifty-pence piece from the Dursleys, sellotaped to a sheet of paper with nothing written on it. And there was a soft, light parcel with no name on it, and inside it something that poured over his hands like water and made them disappear.

There was a note with it, in narrow, looping writing. It said that the thing in the parcel had belonged to his father, and that it was his now. It did not say who had sent it.

Harry sat on the end of his bed in the empty dormitory with the jumper on and the cloak in his lap, and his throat closed up and would not open. At number four, on Christmas morning, he had lain in the cupboard under the stairs and listened to Dudley opening his presents through the door: the paper going, one parcel after another, and Dudley counting them out loud to make sure there were more than last year. He had made a game of guessing what was in each one from the sound it made, so that it would be something he was doing instead of something that was happening to him. He had got quite good at it.

He had presents, and one of them had been his dad’s, and Ron was at home in Devon. There was nobody to show.

⁂

At dinner there were not enough Gryffindors left to fill a quarter of their table, so they all sat together in the middle of it, and a sixth-year prefect pulled crackers with Harry until Harry was wearing a paper crown and had won a set of wizard chessmen, who argued with each other about which of them was in charge.

He was laughing at the chessmen when he looked across the Hall.

At the far end of the Slytherin table, as far from the two seventh-years as the table went, sat Malfoy, quite alone, with a cracker beside her plate that nobody was going to pull.

*Some people have nobody who wants them at Christmas, so they have to stay at school.*

She had said it to him under the fir tree, with Crabbe and Goyle at her shoulders and that bright, mean smile on her face, and he had smiled back and said Voldemort and won. He had not wondered why she was staying. It had not crossed his mind that she was.

He could take his own cracker over. He could walk the length of the Hall to the Slytherin table, past the staff, past the two seventh-years, and hold out the other end.

He thought about her face if he did. After the flying lesson she had stared down every person who mentioned it until nobody would. If she knew that *he* had seen her like this, she would never in her life forgive it. She would rather eat the cracker, paper and all.

He stayed where he was, in his paper crown, and the prefect asked him whether he was all right, and he said he was.

⁂

That night, for the first time, he put on his father’s cloak.

He had meant to go to the library. He went down the marble staircase instead, in the dark, with no clear idea why, and he was on the last step when a door opened on the far side of the Entrance Hall and Malfoy came out of the passage from the dungeons with a wooden box under her arm.

She went straight across the hall and into an empty classroom off it, and left the door ajar.

Harry crossed after her as quietly as he could and stopped in the doorway.

It was a classroom nobody seemed to have used in years, with the desks pushed back against the walls. She had lit one lamp. She had pulled a desk out into the middle of the floor and set a chair on either side of it, and now she opened the box and took out a chessboard, and began to set out the pieces: white on her side, black on the other.

They were wizard chessmen, like his, but old, and black and silver, and they bowed to her as she put them down.

Then she sat down with the white, with her back to the blackboard, and looked across the desk at the empty chair.

“Your move, Father,” she said.

It was not her voice that answered. It was a lower one, lazy and amused, that Harry had never heard, and it came out of her mouth. “Knight to f6, I think, my treasure. Don’t look so cross.” And she reached across the desk and moved the black knight herself.

They played. She played both sides, the white in her own voice and the black in his, and the black kept doing the same thing with its knight, and she kept telling it so, fondly, as though it were a joke the two of them had had for years. She was winning. She did not seem to want to.

“Check, Father,” she said at last.

She waited, with her hands in her lap. The empty chair did not say anything.

“You’d have seen that coming,” she told it, quite quietly, and did not move the black king.

Harry’s eyes had gone hot, and there was nothing he could do with them, and he put his hands in his pockets under the cloak because there was nothing he could do with those either. He could not go in. There was not one thing he could say to her that she would not hate him for having been there to say. He could only stand in a doorway in the dark, the way he had lain behind the cupboard door, and listen to somebody else’s Christmas through it.

After a while she got up. She left the board as it was, with the black king in check, and put out the lamp, and went past him so close that the edge of her cloak brushed his, and away across the Entrance Hall towards the dungeons. He heard a door shut somewhere below.

Harry went in and sat down in the empty chair. From there he was facing the blackboard, and somebody had left half a sentence on it, years ago, about the uses of moonstone.

The black pieces looked up at him, as much as chessmen can look at somebody they cannot see.

“Er,” said Harry. He had only learned this term, from Ron, and he was terrible. He looked at the board and could not see any way out for the king, so in the end he did the only thing he could think of, and moved a pawn in front of it.

Nothing happened.

“Please,” said Harry.

The pawn gave him a long, considering look, and then it shuffled forward one square and stood in the way.

And then, because she would come back in the morning and ask: “Don’t tell her it was me. Will you? Please.”

The black king looked at the empty air where Harry was, and at the pawn, and at the white queen, and said nothing, which Harry decided to take as a yes.

Lyra came back after breakfast on Boxing Day to finish the game, and the black king was no longer in check.

A black pawn had moved into the way. It was a stupid move. It lost the pawn, and it left the black king exactly as stuck as before, only a square further from the edge of the board; but somebody had made it, and it had not been her.

She stood over the desk and looked at it.

“Who did that?”

The black pieces looked at one another. The pawn looked at its feet, which wizard chessmen do not strictly have.

“I asked you a question.”

“He asked us not to say, miss,” said the black king at last, with enormous dignity.

“*He?*”

“He asked very nicely,” said the king, in the tone of one who had not been asked anything nicely by a Malfoy in some years.

“He put the pawn *there*,” said a black bishop, with deep disgust. “I told him. Not *there*, I said.”

Lyra opened her mouth, and shut it again. Nothing she had to say to a chessman would be worth the trouble. She sat down with the white, very straight, and took the pawn; and then she sat for a while longer, looking at the empty chair. Then she put out the lamp and left the board where it was.

The next morning the black had moved again. It was a worse move than the first.

It went on like that. Every morning one black piece had moved in the night, and every morning it was the wrong piece, to the wrong square. The black bishop complained to her about it. A black knight told her, unasked, that the new general did not know an opening from a door. None of them would say who he was. Lyra made her own move each morning, the right one, and took whatever he had left lying about, and was perfectly polite to the pieces, and did not ask again.

She worked it out on the third day, when the Bloody Baron came through the wall of the dungeon stairs beside her in his chains and did not look at her. He was the Slytherin ghost. He went where he liked at night, and nobody ever saw him do it, and he had never in his life answered a question.

Lyra stopped on the stairs and sank into her deepest curtsy, the one her mother had taught her for very old relations.

“Thank you, sir,” she said, “for your company.”

The Baron turned his blank, terrible eyes on her, and went on down through the floor without a word. Lyra took that for modesty, and was pleased.

⁂

Her father’s answer came on the Friday.

It was a whole page long, in his beautiful, careful hand. They were well; the weather abroad was tiresome; the Board of Governors met in January, and he expected to give them a great deal of trouble. He trusted she was working. The elves at school were not to feed her on pudding. Her mother sent her love.

Lyra read it twice, from the top to the bottom, to be quite sure.

Of *Please come* there was not one word.

She folded the letter in half, and in half again, and again, until it was smaller than the first one had been. Then she put it in her pocket with the other, and went down to the classroom.

The black had moved its queen in the night. It had moved her out into the middle of the board, a square in front of a white pawn, where anybody in the world could take her for nothing, and it was so completely stupid, so magnificently and helplessly wrong, that Lyra stood over the desk and laughed out loud. It went round the empty classroom and came back to her off the blackboard. She had not heard herself laugh since the Friday before.

“He’s doing his best, miss,” said the black king, a little stiffly.

“I know he is,” said Lyra, and took the queen.

⁂

On the last morning of the holidays, the black king was lying on his side in the middle of the board.

Black had resigned. Somebody had laid him down very carefully in the night, the way you are supposed to, so that he would not roll.

Lyra stood him up again, and he looked at her. Then she put the pieces in their box, black and silver, every one in its own place, and shut the lid, and carried it down to the dungeons under her arm; and at eleven o’clock she was on the front steps in her good cloak when the carriages came up the drive out of the snow.

Crabbe was the first one out of the third carriage. She came up the steps two at a time, with her plaits coming undone, and stopped in front of Lyra, out of breath, and looked at her face.

“Did you have a nice Christmas?”

“Perfectly,” said Lyra.
