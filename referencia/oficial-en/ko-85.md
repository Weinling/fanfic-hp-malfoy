When it came to the most promising talent of the year, one name could not be left out: Cedric Diggory.

A student who had stood against a corrupt and negligent administration, and ultimately brought the truth to light. The champion who had defeated Harry Potter—The Chosen One—to win the Triwizard Tournament. A man whose contributions had been recognized by Rufus Scrimgeour himself, who now served diligently as the Minister for Magic's personal aide.

Cedric Diggory.

But he carried another identity as well.

"—Oh, Harry. What, what? You've found his base of operations?"

The Order of the Phoenix. The organization Dumbledore had assembled to stand against Voldemort.

Harry had asked Cedric to join.

For the Minister's aide, accepting was not something he should do. However close Rufus Scrimgeour and Dumbledore might be in their alliance, the Minister would hardly welcome the news that his own aide had embedded himself in Dumbledore's private organization.

Yet Cedric owed Harry a great deal. During the Triwizard Tournament, Harry had given him invaluable help. When Umbridge had seized control of Hogwarts, it was Harry who had first stood against her. The very position Cedric now held owed no small part to Harry's role in events.

It was a bitter thing. He had agonized over it, and he had no shortage of excuses ready. But in the end, Cedric had chosen loyalty.

"No. Even if we hurry, tomorrow is the earliest we can manage. Several members are out on assignment, and a few others have accompanied the Minister to Bulgaria. I'll need to report to him first— What? You want me to summon them?"

But Harry's request went beyond anything Cedric had imagined.

"There are Death Eaters among the Aurors? What? You want me to summon them and buy time? You're asking me to send a few of the trustworthy ones your way?"

It was overreach. Aurors were assets of the Ministry, answerable to the Minister for Magic and the Department of Magical Law Enforcement—figures of considerable standing within the Ministry. And Harry was asking Cedric to move them.

It wasn't impossible, strictly speaking. If he reported Voldemort's location and the traitors within the Auror ranks to Rufus Scrimgeour, the Minister would be irritated that the Ministry hadn't discovered it first—but he would ultimately listen.

The problem was doing it right now. That would require Cedric to impersonate a direct order from the Minister for Magic. That was the sort of crime that would land him in Azkaban without a word of protest.

Even Harry couldn't ask Cedric to commit a crime. That wasn't right.

But.

"…If it isn't done within three days, Malfoy will be in danger…."

Harry delivered the words with quiet composure, but Cedric could feel the desperation beneath them. And beneath that, a low, steady anger.

He had no choice. This might cost him his position when it was all over. It might set his career back years.

But Cedric Diggory was young. He could afford to lose that much.

It was better than losing a friend.

"Understood. Wait."

He cut the connection, exhaled slowly, and began forging the Minister for Magic's orders.

Fenrir Greyback had not forgotten the humiliation. It had grated on him—watching the Malfoys, who had been crawling on the floor to appease Voldemort's displeasure, suddenly strutting about as though killing one small child made them something. The sight had turned his stomach.

Carina Malfoy most of all. Narcissa and Lucius made no effort to hide their contempt for Greyback, but their precious princess had sealed away every trace of her loathing and disdain behind a flawless mask. As though she fancied herself some great actress.

Paradoxically, that composure had only provoked him further.

Fenrir Greyback was a Werewolf. His hobby was biting children and turning them into Werewolves like himself.

By any measure—objective or otherwise—he was a creature of revulsion. Fenrir had long since grown accustomed to that fact; more than accustomed, he wore it as a point of pride. Whether someone recoiled openly and tried to put as much distance between themselves and him as possible, or whether they swallowed their disgust out of immediate fear—both reactions were a feast to him.

And so Carina Malfoy, with her sealed emotions and her perfectly composed expression, had been a rare delicacy wrapped in beautiful packaging. Rare enough that even he, a man with no patience to speak of, had found himself willing to wait.

Then, without warning, she had attacked him—all because she'd killed Harry Potter, apparently. No dignity. No preamble.

It had trampled his anticipation and insulted him in the same stroke.

'Let's see if you can pull the same trick with a Werewolf's teeth right in front of your face.'

Voldemort generally paid little attention to such matters, provided no one actually killed anyone. Greyback had made his way to Carina's door and was reaching silently for the handle—

A soft chime rang out, and the protective enchantments surrounding the room shattered. The room, now exposed, was full of people. They were emerging from a cabinet at the center of the space, steadying one another as they stepped out, as though disembarking from a train.

Was this not the right room? Greyback tilted his head back to check, but it was unmistakably the same room Narcissa had brought her daughter to.

Then why were there so many people swarming inside?

He was still cocking his head in bewilderment when—

"What are you doing here?"

Carina Malfoy was standing beside him. He flinched. The corridor had been empty when he arrived; she had appeared as abruptly as if she'd stepped out from beneath an Invisibility Cloak.

She looked at Greyback with undisguised contempt.

"You filth. Even I've never stooped to sneaking into a girl's room."

"W-wait. There are people in your room—"

"Oh, them?"

She pushed her door wide open with a faint smirk. Inside stood a young man who looked barely of age, several older wizards who might have been professors, and others dressed in every manner of clothing—

"Friends from school. They're all kind, decent people. I do hope you'll get along."

She said it the way a parent might speak of a child who'd fallen in with bad company—those cool, pale green eyes fixed on him as she spoke.

Pale green eyes, was it? Greyback was still puzzling over that when—

"Ah. Time."

She reached into her pocket and put on a pair of round spectacles. They were clunky, graceless things, entirely at odds with her aristocratic face. The lenses were so large that her features seemed to warp strangely beneath them.

…No. Her features were actually warping. She seemed to grow slightly taller; her platinum hair fanned out in every direction, darkening from the roots upward.
