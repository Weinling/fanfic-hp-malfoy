# Chapter 47: Trophies

“Forty-one pages of school rules,” said Professor McGonagall, “and I find I shall have to look this up.”

She was sitting behind her desk with her hands folded. In front of the desk stood Lyra Malfoy, a prefect of three weeks’ standing, whose hair had been since a quarter past eight that morning the exact scarlet of the Gryffindor banners, with gold at the ends. Nobody had yet been able to do anything about it. Madam Pomfrey had said that it was very clever work, and that it would probably wear off.

Beside her stood Potter, holding a wooden ruler. He had been given it to keep his mouth open. His two front teeth now reached to about the third button of his jumper, and were still, if one looked closely, growing.

“I said I had a curse for that,” said Lyra. “In first year. Nobody can say I wasn’t clear.”

“Hngh,” said Potter, round the ruler.

“You will not speak, Mr Potter, because you cannot. You will not speak, Miss Malfoy, because I do not wish you to.” Professor McGonagall looked from one of them to the other, over her square spectacles, for some time. “The Entrance Hall. At breakfast. In front of two hundred first- and second-years, one of whom, I understand, was your brother. I have been teaching in this castle for thirty-nine years, and I have seen a good many Slytherins and a good many Gryffindors try to kill each other, and I do not believe I have ever seen two people enjoy it so much.”

Lyra looked at the wall.

It had been necessary. That was what nobody seemed to grasp. It had been six days since the seventh floor, and in the course of those six days it had come to her notice that Harry Potter, passing her in a corridor, had begun to look at her badge and then at the nearest hourglass, in a thoughtful way, and that Parkinson had remarked at dinner that it was a long time since anybody had seen her and Potter have a proper row. There was a position to keep up. She was the Princess of Slytherin and a friend of the Ministry. She could not have people saying that she had gone soft. So when he had said “Morning, Malfoy, nice badge” at the foot of the marble staircase, she had drawn her wand with a good deal of pleasure, and he had drawn his, and it was quite possible that she had laughed.

“Saturday,” said Professor McGonagall. “Eight o’clock. The trophy room, together, with Mr Filch. By hand. Without wands. Every cup, shield and plate in the room, until I can see my face in them.” She took up her quill. “Professor Snape concurs. He asks me to tell you, Miss Malfoy, that the colour does nothing for you.”

“Together, Professor?” said Lyra.

“I find,” said Professor McGonagall, without looking up, “that I should like to know the two of you are in the same room. Good morning.”

The trophy room at Hogwarts was on the third floor, and smelt of metal polish and very old sport. It was long and narrow and lined from floor to ceiling with glass cabinets, and the cabinets were full: cups and shields and plates and medals and small unfortunate statuettes, seven hundred years of them, all gone the colour of weak tea. Mr Filch took her wand from her with both hands, as though it might be a thing he had ordered from a catalogue, and took Potter’s, and put a tin of polish and a heap of rags on the floor.

“One tin,” he said. “Between you. I’ll be back at midnight, and I’ll be looking.” He gave them each a long, happy look. “Used to be chains,” said Mr Filch, and shut the door.

They listened to him go.

There was one chair in the room. It was a hard, three-legged, rather bow-backed affair, which somebody had left beside the far cabinet, perhaps in about 1790, in order to reach something. Lyra and Potter both looked at it.

“No,” said Potter. His teeth had gone back on Thursday, and he had been enjoying being able to shut his mouth.

“I am a prefect.”

“You’re in detention.”

“I am a prefect in detention. I outrank you in every possible condition. In addition, I should like it noted that my family has given this room forty-four separate objects. I counted them on my first day. There is an entire shelf in the second cabinet. I don’t believe the Potters have given it anything but a very nasty scratch on the Quidditch Cup.”

“That’s Ron. That was a Bludger.”

“Then it is settled.”

“You’ve got pink hair.”

There was a short silence. It had been scarlet on Monday. It was fading from the roots, and in the light of Filch’s lamp it had got to about the shade of a boiled sweet.

“I had assumed,” said Lyra, in a low, even voice, “that we were not going to speak of that.”

“I’m only saying. In a court of law. I don’t think a person with pink hair would be given the chair.”

“And a person who was recently unable to close his lips? Shall we ask the court what it thinks of him?”

He grinned. It was really very difficult. She had taken thirty-five points off him in eleven days, and it did not appear to have taught him anything.

She crossed the room and sat down in the chair, with her back very straight, and arranged her robes. It wobbled. He looked at her. Then he went and fetched the tin, and sat down on the floor with his back against the cabinet and his feet on the leg of her chair, and pushed.

It was a very small movement, about an inch. It was quite enough. One of the three legs, it turned out, was a good deal shorter than the other two.

“Potter.”

“Sorry, did I—”

“I shall have you *expelled*.”

“The chair,” said Lyra, “is for the senior party.”

“We’re the same age.”

“I am a prefect. You are a convicted person.”

“So are you. You can’t be senior in a detention. It’s like being senior in a ditch.”

“Mr Filch has left it to my discretion.”

“Mr Filch said if we broke it he’d hang us up by the thumbs.”

“Then it had better be sat on by somebody who was taught how. Possession, Potter, is nine points of the law. I had that from my father.”

“Right,” said Potter, and took hold of the back. “I’m possessing it.”

She took hold of one of its three legs.

That accounted for most of the first hour. They agreed, in the end, on a wager. There were a hundred and twelve objects in the first cabinet. Whoever had polished more of them by half past nine should have the chair, and the other should say something civil.

She won. She won by four, and she would have won by more if he had not hidden one of her rags. Potter polished left-handed, she noticed, slowly, with his right hand in his pocket, and when she told him that that was the most affected thing she had ever seen, he said he had got polish in a cut. Lyra sat down in the chair, which she had wedged with a folded rag, and crossed her ankles.

“Well?”

He sat back on his heels. He had a black smear on his forehead. He looked at her for a while, with his head on one side.

“You’ve got a very good head for hexes,” said Potter.

“I know that. That isn’t civil, it’s accurate.”

“It’s hard to think of anything civil. You’re wearing a badge.” He frowned. “The teeth were really good. I couldn’t eat. Ron had to read me the end of a sentence. I looked in a mirror, and I thought, that’s the best thing anybody’s ever done to me.” He stopped, and rubbed at the smear, and made it worse. “That’s all. I’ve been happier this week than I’ve been since June, and I had to have my soup through a straw.”

She looked for something to say to that. It seemed to have been put somewhere she could not reach.

She got up, and stepped over the tin. It was only the one step. It was very brief, and there was metal polish on his chin, and when it was over she looked at him severely from about four inches away.

“That was the last one.”

“This term.”

“Forty points,” said Lyra.

They reached the second cabinet at about half past ten, and stopped for dinner, which neither of them had had. Lyra felt in the pocket of her robes for a handkerchief and found, instead, something wrapped in a napkin. It was a pumpkin pasty, slightly flattened, and rather better than the last. Nothing that had been near an elf was that shape. There was a note on it, in a large round hand. *EAT THIS. I mean it. G.*

She looked at that for a moment. Goyle had been doing this for some weeks. It was quite absurd. She had found a sugar mouse in her Arithmancy on Tuesday.

She broke the pasty in half, not very evenly, and held out the smaller piece.

“On account,” she said.

He took it. They ate sitting on the floor, side by side, with their backs against the glass and the whole weight of Hogwarts sport looking down on them. She had abandoned the chair for the moment. It was a question of being nearer the tin. It was very quiet. Somewhere behind the wall, a pipe was ticking as it cooled, and she could feel the warmth of his arm through two sleeves, which she had not gone looking for, and did not move away from.

The second cabinet was the old one: Special Awards, and Head Students, and Prefects, going back and back on long brass plates in lettering that got smaller and more self-satisfied as it went. Potter had got up to look at it with his half of the pasty. He had gone along it, reading, in a desultory way; and then he stopped.

She saw him stop. She had been seeing him stop at things for four years. She knew now what it meant, and she found that it was not any more comfortable.

“What?”

“Nothing. I’ve done this one.”

He had his finger on the glass. It was a small gold shield, rather a good one, at about the height of his chin. *T. M. Riddle. For Special Services to the School. 1943.*

“Not here,” said Potter. He seemed to hear himself. He glanced round at her, and his mouth went sideways. “I had a detention. In second year. Other one. Ron was with me, he’d got slugs. I must have done that shield about fourteen times. I couldn’t get the slime off.”

It was the first thing he had said about it, of his own accord, since the Owlery. He said it as one might mention a town one had lived in. She had spent all summer wondering what she would feel if he ever did; and it was very odd. She sat on the floor with a piece of pasty in her hand, and he stood there with his back to her and his shabby shoulders, a boy who had polished this shield in a world she had not been in; and it went through her, like a draught through a good house, that there had been a time when all the people in this room had existed, and she had not. She got up. She went and stood beside him.

“Was he any good?” said Lyra. “At detentions? Draco. In yours.”

He went quite still. Then he let out a breath, and it was nearly a laugh.

“He had one with me. First year, in the Forest. He ran.”

“He *ran*.”

“Screaming.”

Lyra looked at the shield with deep and quiet satisfaction.

“I told you to run,” said Potter. “If you remember. I shouted at you about it.”

“I remember perfectly. I said that next time I should go first.” She lifted her chin a very little. “I would point out that I have been waiting four years for you to arrange it.”

He looked at her. It went on for rather a long time. Whatever he was looking at, it was not the hair.

To get away from it she looked at the case, along the brass plates. *Prefects, 1943*, said the plate under the shield, in a list; and halfway down it, between a Lestrange and somebody who seemed to have been called Mulciber, was a name she knew.

“Oh, look,” said Lyra. “Grandfather.”

*A. Malfoy.*

It gave her a small pleased shock, like meeting somebody’s portrait in a strange house. He had been on the same plate. She had never thought about it. He had died of dragon pox in a room with the doors shut when she was too small to know him, and he slept in a corner of her father’s study; and here he was at sixteen or so, on a brass plate, directly beneath Potter’s shield, with a smear of somebody’s thumb across him.

“They must have known one another,” she said. “Your friend with the shield. I expect Grandfather lent him money. He lent everybody money. It’s what we did.” She breathed on the glass, and rubbed at it with her rag. “Father says that nobody ever died owing him. That’s a joke. Potter? I said that’s a joke.”

“Yeah,” said Potter. He was looking at the plate, and he looked rather tired. “Who was your grandfather?”

“Nobody you’d have heard of,” said Lyra.

He seemed to find that as strange as she had. He frowned at the name a moment longer, the way he looked at a chess piece, without the least idea where it ought to go. Then he picked up the tin.

She did not remember sitting down again.

It had been the third cabinet, and the plates had been very small and very many. There had been a dispute about whether the Inter-House Cup of 1926 counted as one object or two. At some point she had said that she would merely rest her eyes, on the floor, in order to deny him the satisfaction of the chair, which neither of them had sat in for an hour, and which stood in the middle of the room on its folded rag with nobody in it, as a monument. The lamp had burned low. The cabinets went away into the dark on either side, full of small gold gleams.

Something was wrong with her neck. There was something under her cheek that was warm and scratchy, and smelt of metal polish.

She opened one eye, about a quarter of an inch. It was a shoulder. She had her head on it. It was going up and down very slowly. There was a jumper over her knees that she had not had before, and a good deal of pale pink hair had got across his collar. His head had tipped back against the glass. He had his glasses on crooked, and his mouth a little open, and he had his left hand lying on the floor beside her, palm up, as though he had put something down and forgotten what.

He looked about twelve. He looked as though nothing had ever told him anything.

It must have been nearly midnight. She ought to move.

She stayed where she was for what she was prepared to describe as a moment. Nobody could see. There was not a person in the world who knew. It was the first time that she had been warm since the Owlery, and she had been wrong about that being the castle. She looked at the hand on the floor, at the long ink stain on the thumb. She thought of putting her own into it, simply to see; and did not. There was a position to keep up.

She tipped her head back instead, very carefully, until she could reach, and kissed the corner of his jaw.

Nothing happened. He went on breathing. She let her head down again, and shut her eye.

“Forty-five,” said Potter, not moving.

She came up off his shoulder as if it had been hot.

“You were *awake*!”

“Your neck’s going to hurt.”

“How long have you been— That is— You are a *liar*, Potter, you lay there with your mouth open, that is *fraud*—” She had hit him; she had got him with the flat of her hand in the middle of the chest, and he had begun to laugh, without any noise, as he had once laughed in a tent, with his head back against the cabinet and his eyes shut and the whole of him shaking. “It’s fifty! It’s fifty, for impersonating a sleeping person! I’ll have you in *minus*—”

“I haven’t moved my arm since eleven,” said Potter, through it. “I can’t. It’s dead. You’ve killed it. You dribble.”

“I do *not*—”

The door banged open.

Mr Filch stood in it, with a lantern held high, and Mrs Norris round his ankles, and on his face an expression of the purest hope. The light went down the long room. It went over a hundred and twelve shining objects, and a tin of polish, and a three-legged chair standing all by itself in the exact middle of the floor. It came to rest on Harry Potter and Lyra Malfoy, who were sitting a yard and a half apart, with their backs to separate cabinets, breathing rather hard. Lyra had her chin up. She had a jumper over one arm. She looked at it, and held it out at the full length of her fingers, as one might hold out something the cat had brought in.

“Yours, I believe,” she said. “You appear to have dropped it.”

Mrs Norris looked at her with her lamp-like eyes. She had once sat on a staircase in January and watched a girl say *It’s only me* to nobody. She looked at the jumper, and she looked at the hair.

She had never told anybody anything, and she did not begin now.
