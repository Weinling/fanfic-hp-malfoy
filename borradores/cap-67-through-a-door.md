# Chapter 67: Through a Door

You could go back along it as far as you liked, afterwards, and you still got to the Cloak.

Harry had a good deal of time to go back along it. He did it in the dormitory, and on the pitch, and over a plate of something, and each time he got to the same place: a hot Saturday at the end of May, with nine days to the exams, and an Invisibility Cloak lying on the landing, at the foot of the tapestry of the knight, where he would have sworn he hadn’t left it.

They ought not to have been there at all. Anthony Goldstein had taken to walking up and down the seventh-floor corridor in the afternoons, with his eyes shut, reciting goblin rebellions, and you could not very well walk three times past a wall with a Ravenclaw coming the other way. So there had been a scrap of parchment with *Third floor* on it, and a small moon. The desks were still stacked along the wall. Someone had left a window open, and the whole room smelt of warm dust and floor polish, and he sat on the teacher’s table with his legs hanging and looked at the place on the boards where the moon had been, the night she told him *No*.

She came in at ten past two, limping.

Neither of them had mentioned the night before. She had been very nice about it. She had said *please*, at breakfast, by moving the marmalade a quarter of an inch with one finger as she went past the end of the table, and he’d had the note by lunch, and now she came in, said “*Muffliato*” over her shoulder to the door, as you’d tell a dog to stay, and put down a pile of Transfiguration cards, and turned over her left hand to look at it. There was a long red scrape across the heel of it, with grit in.

“I wonder,” said Malfoy pleasantly, “whether anybody has ever explained to you how that thing works.”

“What thing?”

“You left it on the *landing*, Potter.”

“I didn’t.”

“I have just fallen over it. It’s invisible. That is the whole of what is remarkable about it. You cannot simply *leave* it; it’s like leaving a hole.”

“I put it on the chair.”

“There’s a chair on the landing, is there? I must have missed that too.” She picked up the top card, and held it out to him without looking. “I’ve told you about it before. I told you on Thursday. It was your father’s. You might try behaving as if you had noticed. Vanishing spells, five exceptions to the Law of—”

“What were you doing on that landing?”

He had not known he was going to say it. It had just come out, in the light careful voice, and he heard it arrive in the room.

She lowered the card.

“I beg your pardon?”

“You said you’d come up the east stairs. You always come up the east stairs from the dungeons. That landing’s on the way from the library.” He ought to have stopped. He could see himself not stopping. He had been awake since half past three, and the inside of his head felt like a mouthful of dry bread. “Nott’s in the library on Saturdays.”

For about as long as it takes to breathe in, she did not do anything. The sun lay on the side of her face. Then she put the card down on the pile, and squared the pile, with her fingertips, and when she looked up there was a small white dent at each side of her nose.

“Say that again,” said Malfoy.

It was a stupid fight, and it went on being stupid for quite a long time. They did the Cloak again, and whether a person could be held responsible for a landing. They did Nott’s handwriting. They did the ribbon: she took it out, ring and all, and shook it at him, and asked him whether he would care to have a bell put on it, since he was so fond of them. He said that was different. She said it was exactly the same, and that she had said *Pockets* last night and she had not said she proposed to live in one. He said nobody had asked her to. She said *Who were you with?* in his voice, and it was a horribly good impression, and he said at least he didn’t write home about it. They were both shouting by now. It was a relief, in a way. There were a few moments in there, God help him, when he was having a good time; she had gone pink along the cheekbones, and he thought in a minute she would call him something unforgivable about his ears, and he’d take ten points off her, and it would be all right.

She didn’t, though. She said, “You look dreadful, and you’re behaving like my father,” and she turned round to pick up her cards.

It was the turning round that did it. He got his hand into his pocket, and he kept it there. He could feel it wanting to come out. There was the back of her head, with the pins in it, going away from him, as everybody’s did; and he looked for the thing that would turn her back, the way you feel along a shelf in the dark, and it was right there. It had been right there for weeks. He’d been up on the second-floor landing, at the beginning of the month, doing nothing, with his hands in his pockets.

“Hopkins held a door for you,” said Harry.

She stopped.

“In the Charms corridor. I saw him. He did it with his left hand. He’s got rather good with it.” His voice had gone quiet and interested. He didn’t recognise it, and at the same time he knew it very well. “Did you say thank you? I just wondered. You sat in that room for three nights and watched her do it to him. You had a biscuit. I’d like to know what you say. I’d like to know what your face does.”

She did not turn round at once. She stood with the cards in her hand, and he saw her shoulders go down, very slightly, as you see them go down on someone who has picked up a heavy thing and got the weight of it.

That had been paid. That was the trouble. It was on the bill, and they had both signed it, on a hill, in the snow, *He wasn’t nobody*, and she had paid it in a great many ways that he knew about and a few, he suspected, that he didn’t. In all the years of keeping accounts, neither of them had ever once reached over and taken back something the other had paid.

She laid the cards down. She turned. All the pink had gone out of her, and she looked very like the portrait of somebody, and when she spoke she did not shout. He would have given a great deal, later on, for her to have shouted at the beginning. She started low, and she let it climb.

“I said it once,” said Malfoy. “Do you know that? Once, on the sixth floor, in about a dozen words, and you had me against a wall for what came after. I have sat in that chair for five months writing down your moths, with the rest of it by me the whole time, and I have never picked it up. I didn’t think there was anybody in the world who could make me.”

“Don’t you—”

“Don’t I what? You let a girl of eleven—”

“Don’t you *dare*—”

“You stood in Flourish and Blotts. You told me so. You stood there in your dreadful glasses, and you watched my father put it in her cauldron, in front of everybody, and you knew what it was. You knew all of it. You knew what it would do to her and how long it would take. You could have knocked the thing out of his hand, and you let her go home with it, and you let her write to it for a *year*, every night, you let it have her, because you *needed it to happen*—that’s what you said, Potter, I was there, *I needed it to happen*—and you stand on a landing and ask me what my *face* does! She was *eleven*, Potter! She was *eleven!*”

It rang a little, on the glass. A pigeon went off the sill.

He had his mouth open. He stood there, in the warm dust, with his hand in his pocket; and he saw her eyes go past him. He saw her stop breathing.

She had said *Muffliato* to that door, and it kept a conversation where you put it. It didn’t keep anybody out. It stood open by about the width of a hand, and Ron was standing just inside it, with the sleeping knight hanging at his shoulder.

He was in his Quidditch things. He’d got his shin-guards on, and a pair of scarlet robes hanging over his arm, Harry’s robes, the ones Harry had left on the end of his bed. He must have come up to look for him, and put his foot on something on the landing that wasn’t there. He had been falling over that Cloak for five years. He would have known what it was, and whose, and which tapestry, and he’d have thought, *Oh, here we go*, and come through to break it up. It was still there, round his mouth, the start of a grin he had been going to come in with.

His ears were not pink. Harry had known those ears for five years, or twelve, however you wanted to count it. He had seen them every colour there was. He had never in either life seen them white.

Nobody said anything. Far away, down on the lawn, somebody was laughing.

“She was *eleven*?” said Ron.

He did not say it loudly. He said it like someone reading a sum off the board, in a lesson he had missed, to see if he’d got it right.

There was a stone seat in the window at the end of the corridor, and a suit of armour with no head. Harry remembered that. He remembered walking to it, with Ron a little way behind him, and that he had nine lies ready before he got there. They came up by themselves. He had been at this for a long time, and he was extremely good. She had got hold of the wrong end of something; it had been a game; it was Hermione’s idea of a joke; you couldn’t believe a word a Malfoy said. He turned round, with the first of them all ready in his mouth, and there was Ron’s face, with a few freckles standing out on it, a foot above his own. It had told a wall in Hogsmeade, five weeks ago, that he had been all right lately.

The lie went away. He could not have said where.

So he told him. He told it to the headless armour, and it took about as long as boiling an egg. There had been another time. He had been through all this once already, up to seventeen, and it had gone very badly. He’d woken up in the middle of first year, remembering. He knew it wasn’t the sort of thing that people said. He’d known about the diary. He had known who had it, and where it would go, and that it would be Ginny; he had known about the Chamber and the snake and the pipe. He had known all of that on the day they went to Diagon Alley. He’d needed the thing to break, and the fang to break it with. He had thought, if he changed it, he wouldn’t know what came next.

That was as far as he got. It did not sound like much. He had expected there would be more of it.

Ron did not say he was mad. Harry had been waiting for that; he had been rather counting on it. Ron stood with his head bent and his hands hanging, and he didn’t take his eyes off the middle of Harry’s chest, and Harry had seen him look like that before. It had been a long time since anyone had remembered that Ron could beat anybody in the school at chess.

“I was there,” said Ron.

“I know.”

“In the shop. I was holding her books.” He stopped, and worked his jaw a little, as though something had got stuck in his back teeth. “Did you know she’d live?”

“Yes.”

“For definite.”

He opened his mouth. “She did last time,” said Harry.

“Last time,” said Ron. He nodded, slowly, several times. “Right. So it was just, what, it was probably going to be all right. Odds on. And you could’ve stopped it. In the shop.”

“Yes.”

“You could’ve just knocked the cauldron over.”

“Yes.”

Ron nodded. He seemed to be looking at something a long way off.

“That place on the moor,” he said. “With the roof.”

Harry opened his mouth.

“What’s that for? Who’s that going to happen to?” He waited. He gave it plenty of time. “I brought sandwiches,” said Ron.

There was a fly walking up the armour’s breastplate. It got to the top, and turned round.

“You came to ours that summer. My mum did your socks. You went down that pipe with me, and I cried, I sat down there crying, and you put your hand on my arm, you told me we’d find her.” It was all quite quiet. There was only a sort of thickness to it. “She burns her notes. Did you know that? No, you didn’t, nobody does. After every exam, every bit of paper she’s written on, she takes it round behind the broom shed and burns it. She thinks nobody’s noticed. She’s fourteen.”

He hadn’t known that. It was odd, how much that seemed to matter. Two lives, and all that time, and all those lists; and there was a girl behind a broom shed with a box of matches, every June.

“Ron—”

“Does Hermione know?”

There was an answer to that. It had been agreed, and written down, and blotted. *If he asks me, I shall say I don’t know.* All he needed to do was look surprised, and say, *Hermione? No*, and he could feel exactly how his face should go. He had the eyebrows ready. He looked up, and he got as far as breathing in.

Nothing came. He stood there with his mouth a little open. He stayed like that for what must have been about three seconds, and it was the longest he had ever spent doing anything.

Ron had stopped waiting for an answer. Harry watched him put the silence together. His face didn’t change very much. It just stopped having anybody behind it for a moment.

“Oh,” said Ron.

Then he hit him.

It was a terrible punch. He had never really hit anybody; he led with his elbow, and his eyes were shut. Harry saw it leave. He saw it from a long way off, with all the time in the world, and he knew six ways of being somewhere else, and he stood still and let it come. It got him on the cheekbone and the side of the mouth. His glasses went round on one ear. There was a taste of pennies, and a bright ringing, and he found he had sat down on the stone seat.

His right hand had come out of his pocket. He could not have told you whether he had told it to. It had his wand in it, and the wand was up, and quite steady, and pointing at the middle of Ron’s forehead.

He looked at it. He took hold of his right wrist with his left hand and pushed it down, and it went, a little at a time, like a door against a wind. He put the wand on the seat beside him and sat on it.

Ron had not seen. He was looking at his own knuckles, with a puzzled expression, and there was blood on them. He had got something out of his pocket with his other hand, and he seemed not to know that he had: his prefect’s badge, the one with the salt on it, that he took off for practice. He’d wrung it. The pin had gone into his palm and the P was bent nearly double. He looked at that for some time. He put it back.

He held out the scarlet robes. Harry took them. There didn’t seem to be anything else to do; they were being held out.

“Angelina says three o’clock,” said Ron.

He turned, and started off along the corridor, with his shin-guards knocking together.

“Ron.” It came out thick, through the lip. “Where are you— You’re not going to tell her—”

Ron stopped. He did not look round. His big shoulders went up, and they came down.

“No,” he said. “I’m not. I’m going to go and have a look at my sister.” He stood a moment longer. “That’s all. I just want to look at her.”

He walked on. You could hear him all the way to the stairs.

Malfoy was in the classroom doorway. He did not know how long she’d been there. She had her cards held against her chest with both arms, and they were not quite still, and the ribbon was hanging out of her hand. She looked about eleven herself.

“I didn’t know he was there,” she said.

He got up. He got his wand out from under him, and put his glasses straight. There were a number of things that could have been said, and he went through them, and he did not find that he wanted any of them. He walked past her, and went to look for some cold water.

Hermione was at the table in the corner of the common room when he came back from not going to practice. It was nearly nine. Somebody had opened the windows, and most of Gryffindor was out on the grass in the last of the light, and there was only Neville, by the fire, frowning at something in a pot. She was sitting very straight. There were no books in front of her. She’d got the notebook, with the elastic band round it, squared to the edge of the table, and both her hands flat on either side.

He sat down opposite. She looked at his mouth. She looked at it as she would have looked at a figure in a column, and went on to the next.

“He asked me,” said Hermione.

“I know.”

“By the lake. He’d been with Ginny. She was showing him a Wronski, or something, with a Quaffle, and he’d sat and watched for two hours, and she thought he was being funny. Then he came and found me.” Her voice was perfectly clear. Her eyes were swollen almost shut. “He said, *Did you know?* I said, *Know what?* I said, *I don’t know what you mean, Ron.* I want you to know that. I said it to his face. I’ve been practising since November. I’ve done it in the bath.”

Harry didn’t say anything.

“He looked at me. He didn’t shout. I’ve always thought I could work out what he’d say, about anything; I’ve got pages. He gave a sort of smile. He said, *You’re a rotten liar, you know. It’s one of the things.*”

She stopped. The muscles moved in her throat. She swallowed, and swallowed again, as you would get down something too large, and it took a while, and it went.

“So I kept my word,” said Hermione. “I want that clear. All the way to the end.”

“Hermione, I never—”

“I said it would finish him. I told you. I stood at that window and told you.” She took off the elastic band. She put it round her wrist, and she opened the notebook, not at the green tab or the yellow one, but at the page with two lines on it and a date; and she turned it round on the table, and pushed it across, an inch or so, so that he could see. Under the two lines there was now a third. It said *25 May*, and after that there was nothing at all. “It’s written down. I said I wasn’t doing it for you, and I’d like you to remember that I didn’t. I’m not going to shout at you. I don’t seem to have anything to do it with.”

“Is he—”

“He’s on the Astronomy Tower. He won’t want me. I’m going up there, and I’m going to sit on the other side of the door until he does, and if it’s a year, it’s a year.” She took the notebook back, and shut it. She stood up. “You told me how it comes out. You and your other life. Ron and me, with our arms full of fangs.” Her mouth moved, and for a dreadful moment he thought she was going to smile. “I suppose that’s one thing you can’t have got wrong.”

She picked up the bag from the floor, and put it on her shoulder. She came round the end of the table. He had thought she would go straight by, and she very nearly did; but at the portrait hole she stopped, with one hand on the frame, and looked back at him across the room. He had known that face for a long time. It had sat at a desk with a sandwich and rubbed out half a diagram so he could cry. It looked at him steadily now, for some while, as you’d look at a place you had lived in, to see if you had left anything.

Then she climbed out, and the Fat Lady swung to.

Neville glanced up from his pot. “All right, Harry?” he said. “What happened to your face?”

There were five beds in the dormitory. Seamus was snoring. The hangings on the next bed had not been drawn, and the blanket was pulled up straight, as Ron never did it, and on the locker was a half-eaten Chocolate Frog and a *Which Broomstick* with a corner turned down.

He lay on top of the covers in his clothes. His mouth had stopped bleeding, and his cheek had got fat. It was a hot, thick, windless night, with a great many stars.

He’d always had a list. He had started it when he was eleven, in his head, in the Hospital Wing; he had never needed to write it down. It was of who would be there. It was the one thing you could count on in a life like his: you might not know what was coming, not any more, and you couldn’t trust your hand, and you were not altogether sure who you were; but you could lie in the dark and go down the names, as you would feel in your pockets for your keys. Mum and Dad had never been on it. Dumbledore came off in the other life, on a tower. Hagrid would be there, and he had been meaning to go and see Hagrid. Sirius. There were twenty-four days of Sirius.

Ron.

He stopped there, and looked at the ceiling for a time. Then he made himself go on, because you had to, and there was only the one more, and she had gone up to the Astronomy Tower with a bag on her shoulder.

That appeared to be the list.

There was a small warmth on his right hand. He had not noticed it start. He lifted the hand, and held it up against the window, and the ring lay on his finger with a dull glow, not red, not any colour you could have put a name to. She would be sitting up, somewhere under the lake, with a ribbon wound round her fingers, and she wouldn’t say she was sorry. She knew precisely where he was. She always had. She was the worst person he had ever met, and she’d told the whole of the third floor, and he’d turned round and reached for that thing behind her back and he would do it again; and of all the people who had ever looked at him, in two lives, there was now one who had seen the worst of it, and had not yet gone out through a hole in the wall.

He ought to have minded that more.

He closed his hand round it, and turned on his side, with his back to the next bed, and waited for it to get light.
