# Year One, Part 2: Nobody Asked You for Christmas

“Harry Potter,” said Lyra. “How *dare* he.”

She said it to the snow, mostly, because she was walking very fast across the lawn away from Hagrid’s hut and the snow was what was in front of her. Crabbe and Goyle were having to trot to keep up.

It had been a complete rout. She had not had one word to say back. She had turned and fled, and the fact of having fled at all was enough, all by itself, to make her want to scream. A Malfoy did not run from anybody. A Malfoy, at the very least, walked away at a pace of her own choosing, and she had very nearly picked up her robes and run.

He had just *said* it. The Dark Lord’s name, out loud, in the snow, in front of a gamekeeper and a Weasley, as if it were anybody’s.

“He thinks he’s so clever,” said Lyra. “Going about with a blood traitor, and him nothing but an orphan himself. He’s going to pay for this.”

“But how?” said Goyle doubtfully. “If he says—you know—*His* name again…”

That was the trouble. Lyra and Crabbe and Goyle had grown up in houses where that name was not said, by parents who had stood on the Dark Lord’s side before he fell, and the fear of it had gone into them so early it was nearly reverence. Harry Potter said it the way other people asked for the salt, and there was nothing whatever they could do about it: they could not say it back. Something in them pulled away every time, like a hand from a stove.

It was a curse you could not block. A thing only a child who knew nothing could do.

“Even Weasley jumped,” said Goyle gloomily. “When he said it. So it’s not just us.”

Lyra stopped walking so suddenly that Goyle ran into her.

“Say that again.”

“Weasley jumped?”

A slow smile came over Lyra’s face. “Potter’s the only one stupid enough to say the name. So we leave him alone, and go for everybody round him.”

And there was nothing much in Gryffindor apart from Potter. A half-wit like Longbottom would burst into tears if she so much as looked at him.

“Poor little blood-traitor Weasley. Know-it-all Granger. And Longbottom, who can’t remember his own name. There’s plenty to go round. We’ll show them what happens when they make us angry.”

“Oh, *yes!*” Goyle bounced on her toes in the snow. “They’re all useless! That’s brilliant, Malfoy!”

Lyra’s nose went up a little. She waited for more applause, and found, at the moment it ought to have come, that Crabbe was standing there with a blank look on her face.

“Crabbe? What are you mooning about?”

“What? Oh—um.”

Crabbe, who had always been the first to agree with anything Lyra said, had her mouth shut.

“Have you got a better idea?” said Lyra, with a slight edge.

“No. No, it’s not that.”

“What, then? Say it.”

Crabbe went pink. She was round-faced still, that year, with her hair in the two plaits her mother made her wear, and when she went pink it went all the way up into her parting.

“It’s only… his parents are dead. Isn’t it a bit sad?”

“Who?”

“H-Harry Potter.”

Lyra screwed up her face. So that was it. Crabbe was feeling sorry for the enemy.

*Defending* Harry Potter, to Lyra’s face?

Father said there was no such thing as a small disloyalty, only ones that had not grown yet. But this was Crabbe, who had sat next to her at the Sorting and had never once disagreed with her about anything; so Lyra took a breath and was reasonable.

“Don’t be absurd, Crabbe. What has that got to do with us?”

“But he said it as if it was nothing. Both of them, gone. He just *said* it.”

“Oh, don’t be ridiculous. Not having parents. As if that were such a great matter.”

She turned and walked on towards the castle, and did not look to see whether they followed.

Since October, Harry had been the best at something, and it had gone to his head a bit.

Oliver Wood had shown him the Golden Snitch one Wednesday evening on the empty pitch, with the light going, and it had taken Harry under five minutes to catch it. At Hallowe’en he and Ron, with a lot of luck and a bit of Hermione, had knocked out a mountain troll in a girls’ lavatory. In his first match his broom had gone mad underneath him fifty feet up, and then stopped, and he had caught the Snitch more or less in his mouth, and Gryffindor had beaten Slytherin, and the rest of the school had cheered him all the way back to the castle. In the Slytherin stand there had been one fair head that had had to look up with everybody else.

Malfoy took it personally. She took to being horrible to him and Ron whenever she got the chance, in corridors, at meals, outside lessons, as though she could make the cheering stop.

A week before Christmas, he and Ron were helping Hagrid drag a fir tree up to the castle when a cold, sneering voice came from behind them.

“Trying to earn a bit of extra money, Weasley? Hoping to be gamekeeper yourself when you leave, I suppose? That hut of Hagrid’s must look like a palace to you, after what your family’s used to.”

She stood there in her good winter cloak, with Crabbe and Goyle at her shoulders, looking at Ron as if he were something she had stepped in.

“I expect your family’d be glad of one less mouth to feed. Wouldn’t they?”

Ron went for her. Harry caught him by the back of his robes just in time.

“Leave it. If you go for her, she’ll only enjoy it more.”

“But she—my *family*—”

“That’s what she wants, Ron. Don’t let her have it.”

Malfoy clicked her tongue, and looked past Ron at Harry.

“Well, at least *you’ve* got somewhere to go, Weasley. Some people have nobody who wants them at Christmas, so they have to stay at school.”

It was meant for Harry, and everybody knew it. Ron’s face went from red to purple. Hagrid, at the front end of the tree, had stopped pulling.

Harry patted Ron on the shoulder, and walked straight up to her.

She was looking enormously pleased with herself, because it had worked. It was a bright, childish, superior sort of smile, like a cat’s with a bird under one paw. And Harry, who had had that sort of thing said to him at number four for as long as he could remember and had never once been allowed to answer back, was not angry. He was only, suddenly and enormously, *free*.

He stopped in front of her, and to everybody’s astonishment, he smiled back. It was a perfectly friendly smile. He had been practising it since August.

“Can’t be helped,” he said. “My mum and dad died stopping Voldemort.”

Malfoy flinched as though he had thrown something at her. So did Crabbe and Goyle; so, out of the corner of Harry’s eye, did Ron. All three Slytherins took a step back.

Harry had grown up among Muggles, where a name was just a name, and he had not known until that moment what this one could do. He watched it go through the three of them like a draught under a door, and he took another step.

“And because of them, Voldemort’s gone. Isn’t that what everybody says? So everybody ought to be grateful to my mum and dad. Shouldn’t they? You too, Malfoy.”

She had lost already. He could talk about Voldemort and his parents’ deaths as though he were talking about the weather, and she could not even hear it calmly.

Harry kept smiling, and finished it.

“Or do you hate me? For getting rid of Voldemort?”

Malfoy turned on her heel and almost ran. Crabbe and Goyle scurried after her across the snow.

Ron, who seemed to have forgotten he had ever been angry, came bounding up.

“Harry, that was *brilliant!* You shut *Malfoy* up!”

“It was nothing much.”

Harry watched her go, her fair hair bright against all that white.

“You just answer back straight, and smile first. Then you win.”

“That’s what’s brilliant about it! She says all that, and you *smile* at her. How do you even do that?”

“Dunno,” said Harry. “You get used to it?”

Back in the common room, Ron told everybody how Harry had seen Malfoy off. A great many of them had had a bad time from Malfoy, and they all wanted to hear it twice. Neville, who had had the worst time of all, looked at Harry as though he were a hero out of a book.

Harry found he could not enjoy it much.

When everybody hated something that you liked, it turned out not to be as much fun as you would think. Not even when it made you a hero.

Ron was going home on Saturday, with the rest of the Weasleys. Harry had put his name on McGonagall’s list of people staying the day it went up. He had never in his life been so glad to be left somewhere.

The letter came on Friday, at breakfast, by the eagle owl her father had given her in June.

It came down the Great Hall low and slow, the way her father had trained it to, so that everybody would have time to see it, and it landed in front of her between the toast and the marmalade and held out its leg. Lyra opened the letter at the table. A letter from home was a thing one opened in public.

*My treasure,*

*Your mother and I are called away on business over the holidays, and it will keep us abroad until the New Year. It is tiresome, and it cannot be helped. You will be a great deal more comfortable at school than rattling about the Manor with nobody but the elves, and Draco will be looked after.*

*Something will come for you on Christmas morning. I hear Gryffindor had the better of Slytherin last month. Work hard, and do not let it happen again.*

*Your loving father*

She read it twice. Then she folded it in half, and in half again, and once more, until it was a small hard square that would go into the palm of one hand, and she put it in her pocket.

“Is that from home?” said Goyle. “Are they sending the carriage, or are you on the train with us?”

“Neither,” said Lyra. “I’ve decided to stay.”

Goyle’s mouth fell open, with porridge in it.

“Somebody ought to keep an eye on Potter over the holidays.” Lyra buttered a piece of toast with great precision. “You can’t leave a boy like that alone in a castle for a fortnight. He’ll take it apart.”

Goyle closed her mouth and looked at her with something close to worship. Crabbe did not say anything. She looked at Lyra, and at the pocket the letter had gone into, and went back to her porridge.

In the dormitory that night Crabbe sat on the end of her own bed in her nightdress and said, to the floor, “I could stay as well. I could write to my mum.”

“Your mother would have kittens,” said Lyra. “And there’s no need.”

“I know there isn’t,” said Crabbe, and did not write.

On Saturday morning Lyra put on her cloak and went out onto the front steps, as though she had come out for the air.

The carriages were drawn up along the drive in the snow, with nothing in front of them that anybody could see, and the whole school seemed to be climbing into them at once. The Slytherins went in a long green knot. Goyle waved both arms. Crabbe’s round face came to the window of the third carriage and stayed there, looking up at the steps, until the carriage moved and took it away.

At the bottom of the steps Potter was seeing Weasley off. He had his hands in his pockets and no hat, and he was laughing at something Weasley shouted out of the window; and then the carriage was gone and he stood there on his own in the trodden snow, looking after it, and did not seem to mind at all. He turned round. Lyra went back inside before he could look up.

She was halfway across the Entrance Hall when it came back to her. It came back in her own voice, exactly as she had said it under the fir tree, with Crabbe and Goyle at her shoulders and everybody listening.

*Some people have nobody who wants them at Christmas, so they have to stay at school.*

She had chosen every word of it with great care. She stood in the middle of the empty hall, where anybody might have seen her, and found that she could not walk on until she had stopped hearing it.

That night the dormitory was dark and very quiet, with four beds in it that nobody was sleeping in, and the lake moving green and black against the window. Lyra lay with her hangings drawn, the way she always did, though there was nobody to draw them against.

She had been without them once before.

It had been when she was eight. Some business had come up, something urgent that nobody explained to her, and both her parents had had to go away at once and could not say when they would be back.

She had come down in the morning to the dining room, and there had been nobody at the long table but her, and the breakfast Dobby had made, laid out in front of one chair. The table had seemed very long that day. The chair was colder than usual. Even the food did not taste of much.

She had eaten a little of it, crossly, and gone to the library, and read, and gone out and flown about the grounds on her broom by herself. She had called Dobby and ordered him about for something to do. She had poked at the fire. The family portraits never said anything but dull, old-fashioned things, but that afternoon she had sat and talked to them for a whole hour, and had rather enjoyed it.

Draco had been too small to count. He had cried for their mother at bedtime and been put to bed by Dobby, and Lyra, who was the eldest, had not cried, because somebody had to be the eldest.

She had played chess against herself. She had had her bath, and gone to bed earlier than usual, and lain there with her eyes wide open in the dark, very much as she was lying now.

Nobody knew this. Nobody would ever know it. She had thought, *What if they don’t come back tomorrow either*, and she had very nearly cried after all. Like a Malfoy, she had held it in.

They had come home just before dawn, as it turned out, with a box of sugared violets from her mother’s confectioner, all for her. Her mother had not taken off her travelling cloak, and her father’s wand hand was bandaged, and nobody mentioned either. Lyra had let the violets carry off the whole of that bad day, and had not thought about it since.

*But what if*, she had thought then, lying in the dark at eight years old, *what if that day came every day?*

She had decided it was rubbish. If you had not got parents, you had elves, and relations, and a house. What a fuss about nothing.

It had come, then. Not every day: only at Christmas, and by owl, folded very small. She had the square of it under her pillow, and she did not take it out to read it again, because she knew what it said.

Lyra turned over hard and pulled the blankets up to her ears. What mattered was not that. What mattered was making Harry Potter sorry, and until then she would allow herself no weakness of any kind. None whatever.

“After Christmas, Potter,” said Lyra, to the dark. “You’ll see.”

The arrow was already off the string.
