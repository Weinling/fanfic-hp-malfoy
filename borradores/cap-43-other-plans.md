# Chapter 43: Other Plans

“Well done,” said Harry.

He had been practising it since the train. He had worked out the pitch of it and the width of the smile, and he got it out exactly on time, across the kitchen table at Grimmauld Place, with Ron standing on the far side of the toast holding a small envelope in one hand and a silver badge in the other and looking as if somebody had hit him with a plank.

“Me?” said Ron. “*Me?* There’s been a— Harry, they’ve got it wrong, it’ll be you, they’ve just—”

“It’s you. Well done. Honestly.”

In the other life this had been a bad morning. He remembered it very well. He had stood in a bedroom with a badge in somebody else’s hand and felt something small and hot and shameful go through him, and he had been fifteen, and he had minded for days. He had thought about that on the train. He had decided that he was not going to mind this time, and he had gone to some trouble over it, and now it had come, and he looked for the small hot thing, and it was not there. He did not want the badge. He had never wanted anything less.

So that had gone very well.

Hermione came through the door at a run with her hair on end and her own envelope in her fist, saw what Ron was holding, and stopped dead; and then she made a noise that brought Crookshanks out from under the dresser and went straight across the kitchen and flung both arms round Ron’s neck. Ron went the colour of the Chudley Cannons. He stood with his hands in the air, as he had once stood at the foot of a marble staircase, opening and shutting his fingers. Then, very slowly, as though it might be taken for something else, he put one of them on her back.

Fred, at the end of the table, took a long look at this, and lowered his paper.

“Prefect,” he said, in a hollow voice. “Ickle Ronnie. George, get the good knife. It’s the only honourable thing.”

“Don’t,” said George. “Think of Mum.”

Mrs Weasley, at that moment, was being thought of by everybody in the house, since she could be heard coming down three flights of stairs in the voice she kept for births. Harry got out of the way. He took his toast over to the stove, and leaned there, and went on smiling for as long as it seemed to be wanted. Every so often Ginny looked at him, and looked at Ron’s badge, and looked at him again, as if she were waiting for something to go off.

It did not go off. He felt fine. He was almost sure of it.

He had three tickets in his back pocket.

It was the sort of plan that only looks stupid afterwards.

He had got the idea on his birthday, on the swing, before the lamps went out. Nobody had ever taken Ron Weasley to the pictures. Ron had asked about it once, in second year, with great caution, as one asks about a foreign religion. Hermione went with her parents and had opinions. Harry had been twice in his life that he could remember, both times by accident, both times with a bag of Dudley’s to carry; and it had come to him, sitting there, that in all the years of the other life, through everything, the three of them had never once done a single thing that had no point. They had been in a tent together for a winter. They had never gone out for chips.

So he had asked Sirius, who had asked Moody, who had said no. He had asked Sirius again. He had got some Muggle money out of the bottom of his trunk, and Sirius had looked up what was on, in a newspaper he held at arm’s length, and on Monday afternoon Harry had gone round the corner to the Odeon on the Holloway Road with a hat on and come back with three tickets for Thursday afternoon, for a film about a man who dressed as a bat. He had not told them. He had thought he would tell them over breakfast, on the Tuesday. He had rather been looking forward to Ron’s face.

He found them on the second landing, after lunch. They were sitting on the top stair with their heads together over a sheet of parchment, Hermione’s finger on it and Ron’s ears red, and they both looked up.

“Harry! Look at this, McGonagall’s sent the rota. Mate, they give you a *list*.” Ron held it up. He had his badge on. He had pinned it to his jumper and then, by the look of it, moved it a couple of times. “Listen, Mum says I can have a broom. For getting it. A *new* one. She says we can go up to Diagon Alley on Thursday, me and Hermione, on our own, on account of us being responsible, because she’s got the drawing room to do. On our *own*, Harry. She’s never let me go to the end of the *road*.”

“Thursday,” said Harry.

“And there’s a thing at Flourish and Blotts I want for the—” Hermione stopped, and pushed her hair behind her ear. She had gone rather pink. She looked at Ron, and then she looked down at the rota. “Well. We thought we’d get some lunch.”

“You can come,” said Ron.

He said it a second late. It was perfectly friendly. It was the voice of somebody who has been running for a bus, and has heard a shout, and has looked round and remembered that he had a friend with him.

“No, go on,” said Harry. “Sirius wants me for something. Get the Cleansweep, it’s got a better grip. Have a good time.”

“You sure?”

“’Course I’m sure.”

“Brilliant,” said Ron. He had already gone back to the list.

Harry went on up the stairs. He heard Hermione say something, low, and Ron laugh, and it was a laugh he knew very well. He had heard it in a tent, once, towards the end, on a night when he had gone out to take the watch and left the two of them with the wireless. Nobody had done anything wrong. That was the thing about it. He had worked that out a long time ago, in another life, on the way to a forest: nobody had ever done anything wrong. They had just got to each other first.

That night, when Ron had finally stopped talking, he got up and went to the trunk.

He lifted the lid with the terrier on it. He left the twist of tissue paper where it was; he knew what colour that was, and he had a rule. He took out the note from beside it, and sat on the floor with his back against the bed and his wand lit behind his hand.

*That will be all, Potter. You were, on the whole, worth the ink.*

He had read it enough to have stopped seeing the words. He looked at the gap instead.

*It was always going to be them. You knew that before I did.*

He had known it. That was the trouble with having a good memory. He had known it in the Shack in third year, when he used Draco’s name to send her home. He had known it in the classroom on the third floor, with the moon on the lake, when he had looked at her and said what he thought of her father and watched her nod. He had gone into that Owlery in June with a great many things ready, and it had turned out that he was ready for all of them. She could hate him; he had earned it. She could leave. He had stood in the straw and thought, quite calmly, *yes, this is what I would do*. And then he had asked her one question, like an idiot, like a child holding out its hand to see if it would get hit, and she had looked him in the eye and told him the truth.

He did the sum. He could not have said why. It was three in the morning, and he had been doing sums all summer.

His mother. That was the first one. She had stood in front of a cot and been told to move, and she had not moved. Nobody could say that hadn’t been first. He had been hearing it on trains since he was thirteen. But it was hers. It had been done to him, at one, the way the scar was done, and it had got into his blood and his aunt’s, and for the rest of both his lives people had looked at him and seen what she had done. He had never had the use of her. He had the protection.

His father. Who had left his wand on the sofa, and gone down the hall, and shouted for the two of them to run. Who had been dead by the time it was any use.

Sirius, who looked at him across the table every morning, delighted, and saw somebody else.

Dumbledore had been fond of him. Dumbledore had been so fond of him that he had brought him up, over seven years, with great gentleness and every attention, for the purpose of being killed at the right moment. There had been nobody in that life who was not fond of him. The whole of the wizarding world had been fond of him, and it had stood on a staircase and watched him go.

Ginny, for a while. It had not worked. He had come somewhere after Quidditch and her brothers and not making a fool of herself, and she had been quite right, and they had both been very nice about it.

Ron and Hermione. He stopped there for some time.

Ron had gone off and left him once, in the rain, for a very good reason, and come back. Hermione had stayed. He would have died for either of them, and had; he thought they would have done the same for him. He had no doubt of it at all. They would have died for him, side by side, holding hands.

It was a very short list. He went over it twice, as he went over the Horcruxes. It was not a thing he had ever thought to count. He had supposed, in the other life, when he thought about it at all, that he was the sort of person who did not need that kind of thing; he had supposed it the way you suppose you don’t care for a food you have never been given.

There had been a girl on a stool once, who had looked at him as if he had handed her something.

*You’ll do*, she had said. *Even if you don’t know anything.*

He folded the note along its creases, and put it back, and put the tin back under the jumpers. There was no use in any of this. She had picked. She was quite right. He was fifteen, and he had a war to lose. Tomorrow he was going to stop waiting for Dumbledore.

He went on sitting on the floor until it got light.

“The man’s a *lunatic*,” said Sirius happily.

It was Thursday, and they had the back row of the Odeon on the Holloway Road nearly to themselves. Harry’s scar had been aching since the small hours of Wednesday; his knees still had the print of a floorboard on them, from a shack with a hole in it. He had said nothing about it at breakfast. Sirius had looked at him over the teapot, and looked at the three tickets, which Harry had not known he was using as a bookmark, and had announced, as he announced everything, that what was wanted was chips.

He had come in a dead man’s Muggle overcoat, out of the wardrobe on the third floor, and a hat of Moody’s. Nobody at the Odeon had looked at him twice. He had bought a bag of popcorn the size of his head, and he sat with his long legs over the seat in front and his eyes shining in the light from the screen, and had not stopped talking since the lights went down.

“Why doesn’t he just Apparate? He’s got all that money. What’s the rubber for? Is it armour? Harry, is it armour? It can’t be armour, look at it. Now *that* one’s got the right idea, the one with the face. Your mother would have liked that one.” He ate a handful of popcorn. A woman two rows down turned round and looked at him. He beamed at her. “*Terrible* film,” he said, in a loud, confiding voice. “Marvellous. I haven’t been to the pictures since nineteen-eighty.”

Harry laughed. It came out by itself. It was the first time since the swing, and it hurt slightly, like using a leg that has gone to sleep.

There was a third seat, and they had put the popcorn on it.

Afterwards they got chips, in a shop on the Seven Sisters Road, and ate them out of the paper on a bench, in the long yellow end of the afternoon, with the buses going past. Sirius had taken off the hat. He had got a little colour, and he looked, for the moment, like a man with nothing whatever to do; he stretched out his boots and tipped his head back and shut his eyes in the sun. June, thought Harry. Ten months. He took a chip, and looked at it, and made himself eat it.

“It’s not so bad, you know,” said Sirius, not opening his eyes. “Being left out of things. You get used to it. I’ve been left out of things by experts.” He held out one hand, palm up, and waggled the fingers. “Pass the salt, Prongs.”

Harry looked at the hand.

There was a little paper twist of salt on the bench between them. He picked it up, and put it in the hand, and the fingers closed on it. Sirius shook it over his chips without looking, and went on sitting in the sun with his eyes shut, smiling.

He had not heard himself. He would have died sooner than have heard himself.

Harry ate another chip.

They went to Diagon Alley again on the Saturday, all of them, for the school lists, in a long loud straggle behind Mrs Weasley. Ron had his new broom in brown paper and would not put it down. He and Hermione were walking a little ahead of the rest; they were arguing about something, with great energy, and every so often their shoulders bumped, and neither of them seemed to find it worth mentioning.

Harry was outside the apothecary, with his hands in his pockets, thinking about a hole in a floor, when somebody on the other side of the street laughed.

He would have known it through a wall.

It was not very loud. He had turned his head before he had heard it. There was a terrace across the way with little iron tables on it, and the sun on them, and at the nearest table a small boy in a bow tie was saying something with a spoon in his hand, with his whole face, with ice cream on his chin; and sitting opposite him, leaning back with her head thrown up and her eyes shut, laughing, with her hair coming down over the back of the chair, was Lyra Malfoy.

He stopped walking. Somebody went into the back of him, and apologised, and went round.

He had not seen her since the platform. He had seen the side of her face at King’s Cross, turning away from him as one turns from a draught. He had spent seven weeks thinking of that face, and of what he had done to it, and he had thought he knew how it would be: smooth and pale and careful, with nothing in it. He had supposed that she would be going about Wiltshire like somebody carrying a full glass. He had seen a long room by the sea in his sleep, and people on their knees, and he knew whose arm her father had come home with. He had lain on Dudley’s spare bed and been frightened for her in great detail.

And there she was, in the sun, laughing at her brother.

She looked very well. That was what he could not get past. She had a little colour; she was wearing something blue. She was fifteen and on a terrace with an ice cream, with her whole family at the bank, and somebody who was hers sitting across the table making her laugh. He had never once made her laugh like that. He had made her laugh in a great many ways, and all of them had been against her will.

*Them.* That was what they looked like. He had never seen it from the front.

Something went through him then, and it was small, and hot, and he knew it. He had looked for it on Tuesday and not found it, and it turned out that he had been looking in the wrong place.

She had opened her eyes. He saw her find him, over the top of the crowd, and he saw the laugh stop, and the whole of it go in, like something drawn up on a string; and she sat and looked at him, with a spoon in her hand, and her face was the one from the platform.

Ron said something in his ear. It was about Dervish and Banges. Harry took his hands out of his pockets.

“Harry?” said Hermione, a long way off. “Harry, that’s the *road*—”

He had no idea what he was going to say. It seemed to him, crossing, that it would come to him; it was quite a wide street. There was a woman with a pram. There was a man selling something out of a tray. He saw Malfoy put down her spoon and say a word to the boy, and get up, and walk away from the table without looking at him, not fast, along the shop-fronts to the door of Flourish and Blotts; and it stood open behind her.

It was dim in there and smelt of paper. She had gone straight down the long aisle at the back, to Arithmancy, where nobody was, and she was standing with her back to the shelves and her arms folded when he came round the end of it.

“You have approximately ten minutes,” said Malfoy. “My father is counting his money. What do you want?”

He had come two hundred yards and seven weeks. She was a yard away. There was a thread of hair stuck to the side of her mouth, and she had ice cream on her thumb. He had it all ready, suddenly: he had it in his mouth. *You were laughing.*

“Your brother’s bow tie’s crooked,” said Harry.

Nothing moved in her face for a moment.

“I beg your pardon?”

“It’s sort of—” He showed her, on his own neck. He could hear himself doing it. “Under his ear.”

“You crossed Diagon Alley,” she said, very slowly and clearly, as one speaks to somebody on a ledge, “in broad daylight, in front of every acquaintance my mother has in the world, and followed me into a shop. To tell me about a bow tie.”

“Yes.”

“He *likes* it under his ear. He tied it himself. It took him until nine.” Her voice had dropped to almost nothing, and it shook very slightly, and he could not tell with what. “Have you gone mad? Is that what it is? My father is sixty yards away, Potter, and there is a witch in Potions who plays bridge with Mrs Parkinson. We are not *speaking*. I sent you a *tin*.”

“I know. I’ve got it.”

“Then what in the name of—who cut your hair?”

“Mrs Weasley.”

“Has she something against you?” She looked at him. She seemed to see the rest of it then, and he watched her not want to. “You look appalling. You look as if you’ve been dug up. Don’t they feed you? Go back to your family and let them—”

“They’re not my—”

It got out before he could stop it. It was not even very loud.

She stopped.

He shut his mouth. He stood in the aisle with his ears going hot, and she looked at him across a yard of dusty floor. Her eyes went over his face from one side to the other, as he had seen them go down a column of figures, and something happened to them. It was gone almost before he had seen it. He had been reading that face since before he could read anything else. He thought that for perhaps a second she had forgotten to hold it up.

“What’s the matter with you?” said Malfoy.

It was very quiet. It did not sound like her at all.

He could have told her. There was nobody else in the aisle, and it was all there, the swing, the salt, the list, a hole under a floor. *I’ve got no idea what’s coming and I saw you laugh and I wanted to kill somebody.* He looked at the thread of hair. His hand had started to come up, to move it, as he had moved one once in a corridor; he noticed that, and he put it in his pocket.

“Nothing,” said Harry.

“Right,” said Malfoy.

He had not known it could be done to him. He felt it go in.

She unfolded her arms. She looked along the shelf beside her, and took down the thickest book on it, without hurry, as somebody might choose a stick; and she stepped forward and pushed it into his chest, hard, with the flat of her hand, so that he had to take it or drop it. For a moment her hand stayed there, on the cover, with the book between them. He could smell vanilla.

“*Numerology and Grammatica*,” she said. “Buy that. You’re going to fail Arithmancy.”

“I don’t do Arithmancy.”

“I know you don’t.”

She went past him. She did not hurry, and she did not look back; he heard her heels go up the long aisle and stop at the door, and go out into the light.

It cost him three Galleons and four Sickles. The witch at the till looked at the title and then at him, and asked if it was a present.

“Mate,” said Ron, on the pavement, staring at it. “What was that? You’ve got a *book*.”

Hermione said nothing. She took his arm, quite gently, and turned him round, and they went on up the street after Mrs Weasley. She went on holding it for a little longer than the crowd required, and she looked at the book, and then she looked at his face.

He sat up with it that night, after Ron had gone to sleep, on the floor by the trunk.

He told himself that it was a precaution. She had handled it for ten seconds, in a shop, in a temper, with no quill. There could not possibly be anything in it. He lit his wand, and started at the title page, and laid the tip against the first blank he came to, and waited. Then he turned over. He went through the preface, and the tables of values, and the long chapter on the properties of seven. He touched every margin. He touched the space at the end of every paragraph, and the white at the foot of every page, and after a while he stopped telling himself anything.

It was four hundred and twelve pages long. It was getting light by the time he reached the index.

She did not leave gaps.
