# Year Four, Part 1: Repayable on Demand

“Who taught you to play?” said Lyra.

“Ron.”

“I see. And did he finish?”

It was the second Saturday of April, in the year of the Tournament, and it had been raining since breakfast. Nobody was trying to kill anybody. The Third Task was eleven weeks off, and the pitch was still a pitch. There was no match, no Hogsmeade and no essay due before Tuesday, and at some time after eleven o’clock Lyra Malfoy had made the alarming discovery that she had nothing whatever to do. She had gone up to the classroom behind the tapestry of the knight with her father’s chess set under her arm. Potter had been there already, on a desk with his feet on a chair, doing nothing as well. He seemed to have a gift for it.

She had asked him, in passing, whether he played. He had said, “Sort of.” She had then watched him lose to the pieces in eleven moves, while they advised him, and she had sat down opposite him and turned the board round, because there were limits.

“It’s a disgrace,” said Lyra. “You are a person of some public standing. You may be asked to play by an ambassador. Sit up. You’ll take the black, because it’s the side I’d like you to lose with.”

“I’m not going to get better, you know.”

“I am aware of that. I am doing this for the board.”

She moved her king’s pawn and sat back. The set was her father’s old one, black and silver. She had known those pieces all her life. They were the rudest chessmen in Wiltshire. They had reduced two governesses to tears, and they had told the Bulgarian Minister what he could do with his bishop, and a boy who put a hand on them without knowing what he was about could expect to be spoken to, at length, by a pawn.

Potter considered the board with his chin on his fist. He had got rather long in the face that year; it made him look like somebody thinking, which was misleading. He put out his hand, and it wavered about over the back rank for a time, and came down on a black knight, and he lifted it, and looked at it, and set it down on the edge of the board, directly in front of its own pawn, where it could not have reached a thing if it had been given a week and a ladder.

The knight looked at the square. It looked up at Potter. It sheathed its sword, with a very small click, and stood there.

Lyra began to laugh.

She could not help it. It came up from somewhere under her ribs and it got out before she could do anything about it; it was such a *bad* move. There was so much confidence in it. It had taken him a whole minute, and he had looked at the entire board, and he had found, with his unerring instinct, the one square from which a knight could do nothing for anybody. She had to put her hand on the desk.

“What?” said Potter.

“Nothing. Oh, nothing. Do it again. No, I mean it, I’ve missed it. You play exactly like the Baron.”

He went red.

It was a remarkable thing to watch. It began at his collar and went up without hurrying, to his ears and his hair. He looked at the ceiling. She could not imagine why. She supposed nobody cared to be compared with a dead man in chains, and she remembered, vaguely, that she had seen him do that once before.

“The Baron,” said Potter. “Right.”

“It’s a compliment. In a sense. He’s the worst player I ever met. Your move. Why have they gone quiet?”

They had. It had only just struck her. Nobody on the black side had said a word. The bishop had his hands folded. The queen, who had once told Lucius Malfoy that he castled like a tradesman, was looking straight in front of her with an expression of long-suffering and affection, like an old nurse whose charge has come home from school no cleverer than he went. When Potter reached for a pawn, it stepped out a square before he had quite touched it.

“I don’t know,” said Potter. He was still rather pink. “Perhaps they like me.”

“Nobody likes you. Have you been feeding them?”

She took his queen on the fourteenth move. He seemed to feel that this was unfair.

“That’s three times.”

“It’s the same game, Potter. You keep having it. Your deal.”

“It’s chess. There isn’t a deal.” He had slid down in the chair with his feet stuck out, and somewhere around the tenth move his left hand had found hers, under the edge of the desk, much as it had under the table at the Three Broomsticks; and she had let it stay, since it was out of the way there, and had taken his rook with the other. “What do I owe you, anyway? For the lesson.”

It was the sort of thing he said. He said it idly, not looking up. He had no idea what he had done.

“Do you know,” said Lyra, “I am very glad that you asked.”

She took her hand back. She opened her bag, and took out her book.

It was a small flat book in dark green boards, with a loop for a pencil. Every Malfoy had one from the age of eleven, and the leaves were house paper, thick and cream-coloured and faintly ribbed, from the long drawer in her father’s study. *One writes it down*, her father had said, giving it to her, *on one’s own paper. Then it’s true.* She turned to the back, and smoothed the page flat on the desk, and Potter sat up.

“Is that *me*?”

“It’s a running account. I am a very busy person. I can’t be expected to carry you in my head.”

“There’s *pages*.”

“There are four and a half. Item,” said Lyra. “One scarf, grey, cashmere, savaged by an owl of yours in the Owlery in October 1993, with interest. Item, one butterbeer, under protest. Item, the use of a Summoning Charm, for which you were to have given me something, and didn’t. Item, a remark of yours about my hair in the second week of February, which I’ve valued at five Sickles. I was generous. Item—”

“Hang on. Hang on. What about you? You owe me for a dragon.”

“You were not fighting that dragon on my behalf.”

“You owe me for the *lake*.”

“That,” said Lyra, “is on the other side of the ledger, and is under dispute.”

“How’s it under dispute? I was *in* it!”

“Nobody asked you to be.”

They went into the lake. They went into it at some length. They went back through the Whomping Willow, and a sugar quill, and who had first said what to whom about a hippogriff; Potter proved to have views on the subject of interest, and no grasp at all of how it was reckoned. By half past three he was on his feet with the chalk, and there were two columns on the blackboard. He was claiming a Bludger from their second year as a charitable donation, and she was laughing again, and she had given up pretending that she was not. She could hear it, going round the empty room. The rain ran down the window. On the board between them the black knight had sat down on the edge of its square, with its legs dangling.

It came to her that she could not think of anybody else in the world she would rather be having this argument with. She looked at the thought, and she put it in the column with the five Sickles.

“It’s no good,” said Potter. He had got chalk on his nose. He threw the stub up, and caught it, and came back and looked down at the four and a half pages. “I’ll never pay all that. You’ve got me down for *breathing*.”

“That was in a confined space.”

“Give it here.”

“I beg your pardon?”

“Give it here. I’ll settle up. Give me the pencil.”

She gave it to him, chiefly in order to see what he would do. He bent over the desk with his tongue between his teeth. He wrote slowly, at the foot of the last page, in his small untidy hand, which was not improved by the fact that he was using her book as if it were a wall. When he had done he turned it round with one finger, and pushed it across to her, and stood back.

*Everything, repayable on demand. H. Potter.*

Lyra read it.

“You can’t put that,” she said.

“Why not?”

“It isn’t a sum.”

“’Course it’s a sum. It’s all of it. It’s the scarf and the butterbeer and the hair and anything else you think of. You can stop adding it up.” He was grinning at her, with the chalk on his nose, entirely pleased with himself. “Whatever you like. Whenever you like. You just have to say.”

“It’s a blank instrument. Nobody signs a blank instrument. My father would have you in a home.” She looked up. “Potter, have you the faintest idea what you’ve just done? I could ask you for anything. I could ask you for that ridiculous broom.”

“You’ve got a broom.”

“I could ask you for the Gryffindor Cup. I could ask you to stand on one leg in the Great Hall and recite the Goblin Rebellions. You have put your name to it, on my paper, in front of witnesses.”

The black bishop inclined his head, slightly.

“Yeah,” said Potter. “I know.”

He said it perfectly lightly. He had his hands in his pockets. It was only that he went on looking at her, for perhaps a moment longer than the joke required, with his hair on end and his eyes very green in the wet light; and she found that she had forgotten what she was going to say next, which did not often happen to her, and was not at all a thing she cared to have witnessed by a bishop.

She drew herself up.

There was a way of doing these things. She took the pencil from him. She took a small sheet of blotting paper out of the back of the book, although there was nothing to blot, and she laid it on the line, and pressed it down with the flat of her thumb. She lifted it. She examined it. She blew on the page, twice, with very great ceremony, and she initialled it in the margin, *L. M.*, and the date; and she closed the book, and put its little strap across, and laid it on the desk, and folded her hands on top.

“I shall take it under advisement,” said Lyra.

“You do that.”

“I don’t say that I shall ever call it in. I should think it’s worth about fourpence.”

“Probably.”

“Sit down, Potter. You’ve still got a bishop.”

He sat. He put his feet back where they had been, and his hand came back to where it had been, and it was warm and rather chalky. Outside, it had at last stopped raining, and neither of them mentioned it. It was nearly time for tea. He looked at the board for a long while, with his chin on his fist and his eyes narrowed, like a general on a hill, while all the black pieces watched him with their faces full of love.

Then he picked up his other knight.

“Oh, *don’t*,” said Lyra, who could already feel it starting. “Oh, please. Not there.”

He put it there.
