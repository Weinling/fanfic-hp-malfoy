# Chapter 39: Never Lied

On the Sunday nobody knew anything.

The Durmstrang boys ate breakfast at the Slytherin table, very quietly, and went down to the shore afterwards in a body and stood there looking at the water. Professor Dumbledore made a short speech at lunch about courage, and the Cup, and Cedric Diggory, who had been brave and was in the hospital wing with a lump on his head the size of an egg and had asked, apparently, that nobody throw anything at Harry Potter on his account. Potter was not at lunch. Moody was, and Snape was, and so was everybody else; and nobody said a word about a coat on a chair, or a man in a neat dark suit carrying a crate across a lawn, because nobody but Lyra seemed to have seen any of it.

On the Monday her father’s owl came down the Great Hall at breakfast.

It landed by her plate as it always did, and held out its leg as it always did, and the letter was in her father’s hand, the beautiful sloping hand that had written to her every week of her life; and she knew before she had broken the seal. She knew from the way he had written her name on the outside. He had pressed so hard on the *L* that the nib had caught.

She read it at the table, with the Slytherins round her eating their kippers. It was a page about Draco and a broom in a catalogue, and whether a boy of ten could be trusted with a Nimbus, and what her mother thought of it; and in the middle of the second paragraph, between *your mother* and *says*, there was a gap in the line, the width of two fingers.

She put her wand under the table, and touched its tip to the blank, and the parchment split along its thickness into two thin layers, and between them, in her father’s hand but very much smaller, was the other letter.

*My treasure,*

*Our master has returned.*

*I would have you hear it from me before you hear it from anybody else. He is himself again. He is gracious. And our family stands higher in his favour than I had dared to hope, thanks in no small measure to you, my darling. Your errand in November was a piece of a great work, and he knows whose hand carried it. You know what they have been saying of us for nine hundred years: never owe a Malfoy. He tells me, smiling, that he does.*

*He has asked after a certain book. I have told him what we know: that it was taken in the Ministry’s raids, the summer that Weasley’s people went through our cellars, and never seen again. You will say the same, if you are asked.*

*You were never anything but ours. I have not doubted it for a moment.*

*Your father.*

Lyra touched the blank again, and the layers closed, and she folded it along its creases.

Her hands did it quite neatly. She put the letter into her sleeve, against the inside of her wrist, where her wand usually lay, and she buttered a piece of toast, and ate half of it.

*Your errand in November.* A sealed letter in green wax, in a hand she did not know, carried up a gangplank by a girl who thought she was using her father to open a door. A piece of a great work.

*The raids.* She had thought of that herself. She had sat opposite him in his study at Christmas, eighteen months ago, and handed it to him like a gift, and watched his face light up; and he had used it, and it had held, and now it was what he had told the Dark Lord, and it was what she would say if she were asked. She would say it, too. She knew that already. She would say it beautifully.

And on the end of her bed, that same Christmas, she had sworn to be at his feet before the others had found their masks: at the front, on her knees, making herself so useful for so long that one lost diary would be a thing he could afford to overlook. Even if it meant turning on Potter.

It had been an idea, then. A thing a girl promised herself in a bedroom, in the dark, against a day that might never come.

It had come.

Crabbe, across the table, had stopped eating and was watching her. Lyra put down the rest of the toast, and stood up, and went to Charms.

She carried the letter in her sleeve all day: through Charms and Transfiguration and lunch and a double free period in the library where she did not open a book, against the inside of her wrist, where she could feel the edge of it every time she moved. At six o’clock, on the stairs up from the dungeons, she turned the ring once round her finger with her thumb and thought, very clearly and very coldly, of a boy; and felt it begin to warm.

He was in the Owlery when she got there.

She had known he would be. The ring had pulled her up the west staircase and out along the cold stone passage and up the last winding flight to the top of the tower, warm and then warmer, and it had stopped pulling at the door; and when she pushed it open he was standing at the far window, in the straw, with his back to the light and his hands in his pockets, and every owl in the place awake and watching him.

He had not stood at the foot of the dungeon steps where anybody could see him for three weeks, and he did not come towards her now. He had his weight off the left leg, and the bandage had come through his trousers in a long brown stain; nobody in their senses would have let him up that tower. He looked at her face, and whatever he saw in it, he stayed where he was.

“You called,” said Potter.

“Yes.”

She shut the door behind her. It was very quiet up there, at that hour: the wind, and the shuffle of feathers, and somewhere above them in the rafters an owl turning a mouse over and over in its beak. The last time she had stood in this room with him she had been thirteen, and the light had been the same, and his owl had been eating her scarf; and she had told him what Malfoys did instead of favours, and he had agreed to her terms.

She did not take out the letter. Not yet.

“I want to tell you something, and you are going to stand there and let me get to the end of it.”

He did not move.

“You knew about the diary,” she said. “The night the Chamber was closed. You knew what it was before anybody had told you anything, and you knew there were seven, and you knew about a house in the north that is in no register in this country. My father made sure of that. You said *somewhere up north* as if it were the weather.”

The owls shifted on their perches.

“You knew a hippogriff was going to go for somebody before it had so much as lifted a claw. You were on your knees beside me before anybody else had moved. You told me to feed a dog, on a train, before you had ever met it; and when I met it, it was starving, exactly as you said. You knew what Flint was going to do with that match before Flint had thought of it, and he only thought of it at lunch. You went out on Hallowe’en and came back and said *just missed him*, and I thought you meant you were unlucky. You weren’t unlucky. You were early. You knew what night he would come.”

“You stood on a pitch in November and looked at the gates and said *rotten weather, I’ll be quick*. And there were Dementors. You came and found me at the World Cup in the middle of the night, under a cloak, and told me to go back to my mother and stay with her, and an hour later there were Muggles in the air over the tents.” She took a breath. “You were *sure* about Moody. You told me so. You were so sure that you broke into his office with Sirius Black, and you were wrong; and you still couldn’t tell me why you’d been sure. You knew about the beetle, when I’d told nobody, not one living soul. You knew Krum would ask Granger a month before Krum did. And you said a dead man was dead twice, and you have never in your life needed to say anything twice.”

He had gone very pale. He did not look away from her.

“And the Cup.”

She heard her own voice change on it, and could not stop it.

“In the tent, before the dragon. Bagman shouted it through the canvas, *one step nearer the Triwizard Cup*, and you turned your head. You had sat through every roar of that dragon without moving, and you turned your head at that, as if somebody had said your name. And on Saturday you came up out of that maze on to the podium crouched like a cat, with your wand up, looking round you for something that wasn’t there. And then you looked at the Cup in your hand as if it had lied to you.” She stopped. “You thought it was going to take you somewhere else.”

Potter said nothing.

“You expected all of it. Black, the Dementors, the dragons, the Goblet. Ever since first year.” She looked at him. “You don’t look at any of them the way you look at me. You don’t know what I’m going to do.”

The wind came in at the window and lifted the straw round his feet.

“You’ve done this before.”

She had meant to ask. The words came out flat and final.

He did not deny it.

“Yes,” said Potter.

He said it to the floor first, and then he lifted his head and said it to her.

“Yes. I have.”

Lyra stood by the door. She had known. She had known since Saturday night on a trampled lawn, and before that, if she was honest, since a beetle and a word; and it made no difference to hear it. It went into her like cold water all the same.

“Not here,” said Potter. “Not this—it wasn’t this. Somewhere very like it.” He took his hands out of his pockets, and looked at them, as though he were not sure what to do with them, and put them back. “I don’t know how to say it so it doesn’t sound mad.”

“Try.”

He tried.

He told it badly, as he had told her about the trunk at Christmas. There had been another life. He had lived it. It had gone on a long way past where they were now, years past it; he did not say how far. And then he had been eleven, in a corridor, in the winter of their first year, with his scar splitting open and Quirrell walking past with a turban on, and he had remembered it. Not all at once. In pieces, like a dream you go on remembering all morning. That January he had been a boy who did not know what a Horcrux was; by Easter he had known nearly all of it.

“Everything.”

“Nearly everything. Not you.”

She waited.

“You weren’t there,” said Potter. “In the other one. There wasn’t any Lyra Malfoy. There was a Malfoy in our year, but it wasn’t you.” He looked at her steadily. “It was Draco.”

The owls had gone quiet.

“He was in Madam Malkin’s,” said Potter, “the day I got my robes. On a stool, being pinned. He talked at me the whole time about Quidditch and Houses and people who oughtn’t to be let in. He offered me his hand on the train and I said no. He was in Slytherin, and he hated me, and I hated him, for seven years. Half of what I knew happened to Draco.” He stopped. “I’d been looking at you since Madam Malkin’s. After the corridor I couldn’t stop. By Easter I could go down to breakfast knowing exactly who everybody was, and what they were going to do, and how most of them were going to die. And there was a girl sitting at the Slytherin table where Draco ought to have been, and I didn’t know one single thing about her.”

“You said once,” she managed at last, “that Draco hadn’t done a thing to anybody.”

“He hasn’t.” Potter’s voice was very quiet. “Not here. He’s ten, and he plays cards, and he writes you letters about Krum.”

“And in yours?”

“In mine, he had.”

He did not say what. She did not ask. She stood by the door with her back against the wood and looked at him, at the boy who had been sitting in the same rooms as her for four years with seven of them already behind his eyes, and tried to make the two fit, and could not.

“How did it end? Your other one.”

“Can’t.” His eyes went to the window, where the Forest was.

“*Can’t?*”

“Can’t.” He held her eyes. “That one I can’t. Not won’t. It isn’t— Lyra, I can’t. Not yet.”

It was the word he had used at Hallowe’en, she remembered, in an empty classroom, when she had asked him whether he had known about the Goblet. *Can’t.*

“Dumbledore knows.”

“Since the end of second year. A year before the cave.” He rubbed his face. “He’s the only one. Not Sirius. Not Ron, not Hermione. Him, and now you.”

Him, and now you. He said it as though it were something he was giving her. She supposed it was.

⁂

She had meant to stop there. She had meant to take the letter out of her sleeve and say what she had come to say, and go. But there was a thing at the back of all of it that she had not looked at yet, and she was going to look at it whether she liked it or not.

“The diary.”

“If you knew everything. If you’d done it all before.” She heard her voice go very level, as her father’s did across a desk when somebody had made him a bad offer. “Then you knew about the diary before it ever got to Hogwarts. You knew my father had it. You knew he was going to put it in that little Weasley girl’s cauldron, in Flourish and Blotts, in front of everybody.”

“Yes.”

“And you let him.”

“Yes.”

“She was *eleven*.”

“I know.” It was barely a word. “I know she was.”

“Then why?”

He looked at her before he answered, and when he did, he did not try to make it sound better than it was.

“Because if I’d stopped it,” said Potter, “I’d have stopped knowing what came next. That’s all I had. Knowing. If I changed one big thing, everything after it would come out different, and I’d be as blind as everybody else, and I wasn’t— I didn’t dare. Not with him coming back.” He swallowed. “And I needed it. The diary. I needed it to happen the way it happened, so I could get it, and the fang. You can’t— there aren’t many things that will break one of those. That was one.”

Lyra said nothing for a moment.

She thought of a girl she had never once spoken to, with red hair and a second-hand cauldron, writing to a book every night for a year. She thought of an empty classroom on the night the Chamber was closed, and a boy putting her father’s diary down on a desk between them. *Hostage.* The pact, the lake, the Owlery, the credit: so much of what had passed between them since that night had grown out of a thing he had watched happen to an eleven-year-old and decided to let be.

She had liked him for it. She had stood in that classroom with her heart going, and thought him the cleverest person she had ever met, and liked him for it.

“You said to me once, in a shack, with the sleet coming down outside, that you had never lied to me.”

He shut his eyes.

“*I’ve never lied to you, Malfoy.*” She used his voice. “You had, of course. That nonsense about the preface of the book. I knew that. But I thought I could tell when you were joking.” She waited until he looked at her. “The important things—you let me find out what you wanted me to find out, in the order you wanted me to find it. And I thought I was catching up with you.”

He did not answer.

“The egg.”

He opened his eyes.

“You knew. You knew what was in it before you ever opened it. You knew about the lake, and the merpeople, and the song. You sat in that library and read the page I gave you, and you let me—” Her voice did something, and she stopped it. “You let me solve it.”

“Yes.”

“*Why?*”

He looked at her across the straw, with the light behind him and the owls all round, and for the first time since she had come through the door he looked as though it hurt him to be looked at.

“I wanted you to have it,” said Potter.

⁂

She took the letter out of her sleeve.

She did not give it to him. She touched the blank, and the layers came apart, and she held it up so that he could see her father’s hand, very small, across the inside of the page; and she read him the first line of it, in a voice she did not recognise.

“*Our master has returned.*”

She saw it go into him, and saw that it was not news, and saw that it was worse than news, because he had been standing on a podium on Saturday night with his wand up, waiting for it, and it had happened somewhere else.

“He’s back,” said Lyra. “Since Saturday. My father says our family stands very high in his favour. Higher than he’d dared to hope.” She folded the letter again, along the same creases. “Thanks in no small measure to me. My errand in November was a piece of a great work, he says, and he knows whose hand carried it.”

“Lyra—”

“You’re going to let me finish. You owe me that, at the very least, and you know it.”

He stopped.

“My family is in it,” she said. “All the way in. They were in it before I ever went up that gangplank, and I carried it up there for them, and now they’re in his favour because of me. That’s the first thing.” She put the letter back into her sleeve. “And I lied to you. In that classroom, when I’d made you swear. You kept your word and I lied to you, and I am not going to tell you what about. Not now. Probably not ever. That’s the second thing.”

He took a step towards her through the straw, and she held up one hand, and he stopped again.

“And the third thing is you. You put me on credit that night, Potter, and you never once told me the terms.”

“That isn’t—”

“Isn’t it?”

He did not finish.

“I can’t be with you.”

She looked at him while she said it. She had promised herself that on the stairs. She had said it over and over, coming up, until it had no edges left, and it came out quite steady, and nothing in the room fell down. It was only that she could see the exact place in his face where it arrived. She had not thought about that. She would rather have had the dragon.

She looked down at the ring on her finger. She did not take it off. Not here; not in front of him.

“Because of them,” said Potter.

“Yes.”

“If it came to it.” He stopped, and began again, carefully, like somebody putting his weight on a stair he did not trust. “Them or me.”

“It has come to it. That’s what this is.” She made herself go on looking. “It was always going to be them, Potter. You knew that before I did.”

He nodded. He went on nodding for a little longer than the answer required. She had told him a great many worse things in the last half-hour, and she had watched a dragon’s tail go through his shoulder with less to show for it, and she could not think why that should have been the one to do it. He put out a hand behind him and found the window-sill. He seemed to be having some trouble with the air; he took a breath, and it did not appear to go anywhere, and he took another.

Then he laughed.

It was one breath of a laugh, with nothing in it. She had not known that he owned such a thing.

“You could have lied,” said Potter.

“I beg your pardon?”

“You’re good at it. You’ve just told me so. You did it in that classroom with the moon on the lake, and I never—” His voice cracked straight up the middle. “Once more. That’s all. It wouldn’t have cost you anything. You could have said *I don’t know*. You could have said *don’t ask me that*. You could have looked at the *floor!*”

He had shouted it. In four years she had heard him angry in a great many voices, and they had all been quiet. Every owl in the tower went up at once, and the air was full of wings and straw and a hard dry clatter; and Lyra stood where she was and waited for it to settle, and found that she was angrier than she had ever been in her life. It was a great relief.

“*I* could have lied,” she said.

She came away from the door.

“You. *You* stand there and say that to me. You have just finished telling me that you let a child of eleven write to a book for a year. You haven’t said a word to me since I was twelve without another one standing behind it. I have lied for you to my father, Potter. At his own table, with his hand on my hair. I have lied to Snape, and to Moody, and to the whole of my House, in the Great Hall, with my hair done. I have been at it so long that I have to think how my face goes. And the one time, the *one* time in four years that I look you in the eye and tell you the truth, you would like your money back?”

Her voice had gone on the last word. She would have given a good deal to have that back.

He had turned white to the lips. He came off the sill and across the straw, dragging the leg and not appearing to know it, and he stopped about a yard from her, and she did not give him an inch of it. He was pulling at the room in great uneven breaths, like somebody just come up from the bottom of a lake, and none of them seemed to be doing him any good.

“Everybody’s got a *them*.” There was hardly any air in it. “Have you noticed that? Everybody I’ve ever— And I’m always so bloody *understanding* about it. I’ve had the practice. I’ve had two goes. I was *good* at it. And then you came along, and I thought—”

“Don’t.”

“I thought you—”

“*Don’t*, Potter. Please.”

It was not a word she used. She did not know what he had thought. She would have given the Firebolt to know, and every Galleon with her name on it; and she knew, as one knows in the dark where the edge of a roof is, that if he got to the end of that sentence it would be a good deal further to the door.

He stopped. He stood with his mouth a little open and his chest going. The words seemed to have gone out of him, all but a few.

“I don’t want you to go.”

Nobody had ever said that to her. She had been sent to a great many places by people who wished her well. It came across a yard of straw with nothing standing behind it, no plan and no other sentence, and it cracked on the last word.

“I don’t want to,” said Lyra.

It got out before she could do anything about it. It was a very small voice, and she had no idea whose it was. It was true, and they both stood and looked at it, and it did not alter one single thing.

“But you’re going.”

“Yes.”

He looked at her for about as long as it takes to read a line. She saw it reach the bottom of him. Then she saw him leave off looking for the words, and begin to look for something else. She had seen him do that to other people. She had seen him do it to Snape. When he spoke again, his voice had gone quite quiet and clear.

“*When there was nobody*,” said Potter.

She did not understand, for a moment.

“That’s what you told me. First year, in front of a mirror. Every night of the Christmas holidays in an empty classroom, eleven years old, with your father’s chess set and a ghost.” He took a breath, and it shook, and he went on. “*Them*, is it? Where were they, Malfoy? Go on. You’re so sure. Who came?”

It went in under her ribs and stopped there.

She had told that to one person in the world. She had handed it to him at midnight as if it were a sweet, and he had kept it for three years, and known exactly where it was. He had gone a very strange colour. His mouth had stayed a little open, as if there were more of it, as if he had heard something in what he had said that she had not; and then he shut it.

Something had gone wrong with her eyes. They had filled, without permission, right up to the lid. She fixed them on a brown owl that had come down on the beam behind his left shoulder, and she held them wide open, and got one small breath in through her nose. She was Lucius Malfoy’s daughter. It was only water. It could stay where it was.

It did not stay where it was.

She felt it go, down one side of her nose and then the other, hot and perfectly visible, and there was nothing behind it to stop the rest. She had been told what to do about this when she was four. Her mouth had started to shake. She could not find the thing one did with it.

“Nobody,” said Lyra.

It took her two attempts. The first had no sound in it.

“Nobody *came!* Is that what you want? Nobody came, nobody ever— I know what they are, Potter, thank you! I have been reading my father’s face since before I could read! I know what he sent me up that gangplank with, and I know what he calls me, and I know what he’d spend, you needn’t tell me, you needn’t stand there and *tell* me— They didn’t come, and they’re mine, and I am going home! That’s what the word *means!* Do you imagine that if one got to choose—”

It broke. It went out from under her in the middle, like a stair. The noise that came instead was high and wet and not at all the noise of a Malfoy, and every owl on the near perches turned to look. She put the back of her hand against her mouth. It did no good. She stood in front of him with her shoulders going and her nose running, and she could not make it stop, and he could see all of it.

She saw what it did to him. He had come across the room to break something, and it had not been that. He looked, which was absurd, as though he had seen it once before.

“Lyra,” said Potter. “I didn’t—”

“You *did!*” It came out in pieces. “You’ve been— saving it. It was very well done.”

He opened his mouth, and he shut it. He had.

“They’ll want things from you.” He was speaking fast and low now, and he did not sound like anybody she knew. “Your father, and him. They’ll want you to tell them things. About me, about Dumbledore. Let them. I’ll make sure you’ve got enough to give them—enough that they keep you, enough that you’re useful—and nothing that costs you anything. Nothing that can come back on you. I can do that. I can keep you safe inside it, Lyra. You only have to—”

“Don’t insult me, Potter.”

She could hardly get it out. He stopped anyway.

“I have never in my life needed looking after, and I am not going to start with you. If I am going to be on the other side of this, then I shall be on the other side of it, all the way, as myself. I am not going to stand in my father’s house being handed scraps by you to feed to my own family, like a bird you’ve trained to come to the window. I’d rather be nothing at all.”

She wiped her face on her sleeve, all down one side, like a stable boy. It made no difference. There was more.

“*Never owe a Malfoy*. They have been saying it for nine hundred years, and going on doing it, because we extend credit. I told you so in this room, with your owl eating my scarf. You could have owed me for the rest of your life, and I should have sent you a very civil reminder every Christmas.” It was a moment before she could go on. “I should have liked that. But you’ve *kept* me. You’ve had me in a drawer with everything else you know, and taken me out, and now you’ve stood there and offered to go on doing it with the window left open, and you’ve called that keeping me safe.”

She looked at him.

“Never *own* a Malfoy, Potter.”

There was only one person in the world she would have told it to.

He did not say anything. There did not seem to be anything left in him to say it with. He looked at her a moment longer, and then he turned and went back across the straw to the window, slowly, like a man carrying something up a flight of stairs, and stood with his back to her and both hands on the sill.

So that was that. She crossed to the door and put her hand on the latch. There was a long dark mark on the straw where he had walked, and she looked at it. It would want seeing to. It would be somebody else who saw to it.

“You’ve come through your bandage,” said Lyra, to the door. “Go to Pomfrey.”

“Malfoy.”

She stopped. She always did, at doors in this castle; it was a failing.

He had let go of the sill. He was coming back across the straw, dragging the leg, and taking his time about it. His face was grey, and his eyes were wet, and he did not seem to care who knew it, and he was looking at her as she had seen him look at a dragon, from the mouth of a tent: as one looks at a thing that one has been reliably informed cannot be done.

He stopped in front of her.

“I’m going to kiss you,” said Potter.

Lyra stared at him.

“I beg your pardon?”

“You said I never told you the terms.” His voice had almost nothing left in it, and it was perfectly steady. “Those are the terms. You’ve got a wand. I’m telling you first, that’s all.”

“Did you hear *one word* that I—”

“Every word.” The corner of his mouth moved. It was the most insolent thing she had ever seen, and his eyes were still wet. “Never own a Malfoy. I heard. Put it on the bill.”

She had finished him. She had taken him apart, in order, under headings, and he was standing there on one leg. She had her wand in her sleeve and the door at her back and all the time in the world.

She took her hand off the latch.

There had been a boy behind a broom shed once who had asked, and waited to be told. He did not ask now. He kissed her as he had gone out to that dragon, and his hands came up and held her arms, very hard, and she felt him shaking, or herself, and could not tell which.

He had heard every word. He knew that she was going down those stairs, and that she would go on going down them for as long as she lived. He wanted her to stay. There was no more to it than that. It was in his hands, which were hurting her, and in the way he held on, like somebody going over the edge of something. He could not keep her, and he knew it, and he was going to see to it that she took this with her. It would go up to Wiltshire in the carriage. It would sit at her father’s table. There was no tin it could be sent back in and no column it could be put down to. It was the most selfish thing that anybody had ever done to her. He had told her the terms, and put her in his debt, and he was holding on as hard as he could.

It was very well done.

She wanted it clearly understood that nothing was forgiven. She did not stop to say so. She had the front of his robes in both her hands, and she held on, with her father’s letter in her sleeve and the ring warm on her finger. She was still crying. It did not seem to be a thing that stopped for anybody. All round them the owls came down, by ones and twos, to their rows in the evening light, and watched, and did not make a sound.

When it stopped, she did not step back. Her breath was coming in hiccups, like a child’s. She stood with her forehead against his collar and his chin on the top of her head, and all the insolence had gone out of him; there had been a breath or two that had not quite been breaths.

“That was the last one,” said Lyra.

It had been going to sound like a door shutting. She heard what it sounded like.

He did not answer at once. She could feel his heart going.

“I don’t want it to be,” said Potter.

She took her hands out of his robes, the left and then the right. She had never had to make them do anything so difficult. She did not look at his face. She turned round, and found the latch at the second try, and lifted it.

“This year,” said Potter, behind her.

He had tried to make it a joke. She heard him try. It came out on no breath at all, and it cracked, and there was not an owl in that tower that could have taken it for one.

She went down the stairs.
