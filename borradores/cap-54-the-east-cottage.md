# Chapter 58: The East Cottage

She gave her mother the note on the first evening, in the dressing room at the end of the west corridor, with the door shut.

She took the little square of paper out of the inside of her robes, put it into her mother’s hand, folded, and stepped back.

Her mother looked at it, and left it folded, for a long time.

“Where did you get this?”

“From the person who was asking.”

Narcissa Malfoy sat down, quite slowly, on the stool in front of her glass. Then she unfolded it.

Lyra left her to read it alone. She went and stood by the window, with her back to the room, and looked out at the snow on the lawn, and the yew hedges, and the path that ran east between them to the cottage at the end, where a lamp was lit; and she heard behind her the small sound of paper being smoothed flat on a glass table-top, and then nothing at all, for a long time, except her mother breathing.

When she turned round, her mother was sitting with the note under her hand, flat, as she had sat with the postscript in August; and her face was quite dry, and quite still, and older than Lyra had ever seen it.

“It’s his hand,” said her mother. “At the end. When he knew.” She kept her eyes on the paper. “He wrote very badly when he was frightened. He always did. He held the quill too tight.”

“Mother—”

“Go to bed, darling,” said Narcissa. “Thank you. Thank—” She stopped. “Tell him thank you. If you speak to him. I can’t.”

Lyra went to bed.

In the morning the note was not on the dressing table, nor anywhere Lyra could see; and her mother came down to breakfast in grey, as she always did, and asked Draco whether he had slept, and was in every way exactly as she always was, except that she was wearing, on a thin chain round her neck, under her collar, something small and flat that had not been there the day before.

Draco would not put the box down.

He had carried it in from the carriage himself, in both arms, and up to his room, and he had slept with it, Pobble said, on the end of his bed, like a dog. On Christmas Eve he brought it down to breakfast, wrapped in his scarf, and set it on the chair beside him, and fed it nothing, and patted it now and then as though it might need reassuring.

“What *is* that, darling?” said their mother.

“A secret,” said Draco, with enormous happiness. “It’s for the gentleman. I *found* it.”

Their father, at the head of the table, looked at the box over the top of his *Prophet*. He looked at it for rather longer than he looked at most things. Then he smiled at Draco, very warmly, and said that he was sure the gentleman would be delighted, and went back to the paper; and Lyra, who had been watching her father’s face across breakfast tables for fifteen years, saw that he did not know what was in it either, and did not want to.

“Your Professor Umbridge writes that you have been of real service,” said her father, as she went past his chair. He did not look up from the paper. He put out one hand and laid it for a moment against her cheek. “A Hufflepuff, I gather. Well done, my darling. It has been noticed. Do wear the little badge tonight.”

“Thank you, Father,” said Lyra.

His hand was warm. It was exactly what she had wanted. She went back to her place and finished her toast.

The guests began to come that evening.

They came after dark, as such guests always had, by the drawing-room fire and up the drive on foot, one and two at a time, in heavy cloaks, and were shown into the long dining room where the table had been laid for twenty with the silver that only came out at Christmas. Lyra knew most of them. She had known them all her life: men who had sat at that table over the port when she was small and been charming to her, and slipped her a Sickle, and asked about her pony. Nott’s father, very old, with his stick. Montague’s, florid and loud. Mr Avery. Two she did not know, a pair of brothers with heavy eyelids who did not take off their gloves. And Mr Crabbe.

He found her before dinner, by the fire, with a glass of something in his hand. He was a big man, broad and slow, with Vanessa’s pale eyes in a face that had none of Vanessa in it at all.

“Miss Malfoy.” He bowed, very correctly. “My daughter writes that she has stayed at school. At your suggestion, I understand. Something about her Arithmancy.”

“She is a great deal behind, sir,” said Lyra. “I thought a quiet fortnight would help.”

“How kind.” Mr Crabbe smiled. It was not an unpleasant smile; it was a courteous one, the smile of a man at a Christmas party being gracious to the daughter of his host. It went on for a moment longer than it needed to, and his pale eyes took no part in it. “She thinks a great deal of you. She always has. I am glad she has such a friend, at a time like this.” He raised his glass to her, a little. “It is good for the children, isn’t it, to have friends who understand. Who know how things are.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Do give her my love,” said Mr Crabbe, “when you see her,” and went away to talk to her father, and laughed, loudly, at something her father said that was not funny.

She stood by the fire with her hands quite cold.

Theodore Nott drew out her chair at dinner, with his very clean hands, and asked in an undertone whether she had got over her cold.

“What cold?”

“Potter,” said Theodore, and unfolded his napkin.

It was really very restful.

At the end of the meat old Mr Nott got to his feet with the help of his stick and his neighbour’s arm, and raised his glass, and the long table went quiet for him.

“Absent friends,” said Mr Nott.

“Absent friends,” said the table, and drank.

And Lyra, who was watching, as she was always watching, saw half the men at that table turn their heads as they drank, very slightly, without meaning to, as people turn towards a fire on a cold night: east. Towards the window at the end of the room, and the dark, and the snow on the lawn; and the path between the yew hedges, and the lamp that was lit at the end of it.

On Christmas morning she went into her father’s study to fetch a book he had promised her, and stopped, just inside the door.

The study was the darkest room in the Manor and in winter the warmest, and Pobble had the fire up, and the four generations of Malfoys looked down from the panelling as they always had, faintly flushed, faintly disappointed in her for coming in.

Three of them.

She stood in the doorway with her hand still on the handle and counted them. The great-uncle who cleared his throat. The ladies at the far end, who talked about hats. The stern man in the ruff above the fireplace, who never said anything. And low on the wall in the darkest corner, where the curtain’s shadow fell, where an old man in grey had slept with his chin on his chest and his hands on the head of his cane for as long as she could remember—

Nothing.

A rectangle of panelling, a shade lighter than the panelling round it, where the sun from the window had not reached for forty years. And at the top of the rectangle, a nail.

She went and stood in front of it.

In July her father had lifted his glass to that corner. *Your grandfather would have given a great deal to see this summer.* She had looked at the sleeping face, and thought it had her father’s long chin, and nothing else of anybody’s that she could see.

Somebody had taken him down. Somebody had come into her father’s study, between July and Christmas, and lifted a dead man’s portrait off its nail, and carried it away; and nobody had said a word about it, and nobody would, because in this house you did not.

“Lyra?” Draco, in the doorway behind her, with the box in his arms. “Mother says presents. *Now.* She says if you don’t come she’s giving yours to Pobble.”

“Coming.”

She did not ask whether he had noticed. She knew he had not. He had been in and out of this study every day of his life and had never once looked at the corner, because there had never been anything in it but an old man asleep.

Draco went that night.

She had known he would. He had been waiting all day, through the presents, and the long Christmas lunch, and the walk on the frozen lawn, and the crackers, with his face alight and one hand never far from the box; and when the house had gone to bed, which in that house was not the same as asleep, she sat on her window seat in the dark in her dressing gown and watched.

At a little after midnight a small figure in a coat over its pyjamas came out of the garden door below her, with a box in its arms and a lantern on the crook of its elbow, and went away across the snow, east, between the yew hedges, leaving a line of small footprints behind it.

Lyra put on her boots.

The snow had stopped. The sky had cleared, and there was a moon, very bright, and every star there was; and the lawn was blue and white and silver and perfectly silent, and her brother’s footprints went across it like a line of stitches. She walked in them. There was no need of a light. At the end of the path, between the last of the yews, the east cottage stood with its roof thick with snow and smoke going up straight from its chimney into the still air, and a lamp lit in the window, and the door an inch open, as Draco had left it.

She had not been this close to it since she was small. She had been told not to. She had always done as she was told about the cottages, because there were men who came up the drive after dark and were gone by breakfast, and it was not a thing one asked about.

She stopped outside the door, in the snow, with her hand on the cold latch, and looked through the gap.

It was a small room, and warm, and full of books. There was a fire, and a chessboard on a table beside it with its pieces set out in the middle of a game, black and silver, arguing quietly among themselves; and in a high-backed chair by the fire, with Draco kneeling on the hearthrug in front of him in his coat and pyjamas, holding up the box, there was an old man.

He was very old. His hair was white, and long, and lay on his shoulders, and his face was long and pale and had once been handsome, and still was, nearly; there were marks on it, small round pale ones, on the cheeks and the forehead, like the marks a thing leaves that has gone very deep and then healed, almost, so that one had to look to see them. There was a cane leaning against the arm of his chair, black, with a head of silver.

He took the box from Draco. He took it in both hands; and his hands were long and thin and spotted with age, and perfectly, entirely steady.

Lyra looked at the long chin, and the cane, and the pale marks on his cheeks.

*He says Father cheated much worse than me when he was my age.*

In her father’s study there was a nail where that face should have been. She had been told the dragon pox had killed him, too quickly for her to remember, behind closed doors. *Better to remember him as the painter had.*

She looked at the long pale face by the fire, with its marks almost healed, and its long chin, which was her father’s chin, and nothing else of anybody’s she had been able to see in a sleeping portrait; and saw it now, awake, and knew it.

She pushed the door open, and went in.

Draco gave a little shriek and nearly fell into the fire. The old man was perfectly still. He turned his head, quite slowly, and looked at her standing in the doorway in her dressing gown and boots, with snow on the hem, and the cold coming in round her; and his eyes were grey, and pale, and very clear, and exactly like her own.

“Grandfather,” said Lyra.

The old man looked at her for a long moment. Then the corner of his mouth went in, a quarter of an inch, exactly like the one her father wore across a chessboard when she had made a move he had not seen coming.

“Shut the door, child,” said Abraxas Malfoy. “You’re letting in the cold. And a Malfoy does not stand in doorways. She comes in, or she goes.”

She came in. She shut the door.

“Lyra!” Draco had scrambled up on to his knees, scarlet, furious, delighted, bewildered. “You *followed* me! That’s—he’s—you can’t—it’s a *secret*—” And then, catching up: “*Grandfather?*”

“Your sister,” said the old man, “has always been the cleverer of you, Draco. Do not take it to heart. So was your grandmother, and I married her for it.” His eyes stayed on Lyra. “Sit down, girl. There. Where I can see you.”

It was what her father said. *Sit there, where I can see you.* She had never known where he had got it.

She stayed by the door.

*Never felt better in his life.* She looked at the steady hands and the nearly healed marks. She thought of a cure, and of what he might have promised to get it.

Her eyes had gone to the table, because it was easier than his face. Beside the chessmen lay a long narrow book with its lock hanging open. She knew the binding. It had stood on the low shelf in her father’s study for as long as she could remember, and Pobble dusted it on Thursdays. It lay open somewhere near the middle: two columns of names, in brown ink and black and a rusty colour that was neither, in a great many hands, going back and back; and some of the names had a line ruled through them, very straight.

The old man did not turn his head. He put out one long finger and shut it, and the lock closed by itself with a small dry click.

“Everybody who ever owed us anything,” he said.

“You’ll want to know,” said Abraxas Malfoy, “a great many things. I imagine you will ask them all. You have your father’s habit of the question next to the one you want answered; I taught it him. But not tonight.” He looked down, at last, at the box in his lap. “Tonight your brother has brought me a present, and I should like to see it.”

“It’s the thing on the list,” said Draco, recovering. “I found it. Behind the wall. You walk past three times and think about hiding things, and a door comes, and inside it’s *enormous*, Grandfather, it’s like a cathedral made of junk, there’s everything, there’s a stuffed troll, and I found the bust with the wig, like you said, and it was on its head, and I didn’t tell *anybody*—”

“Good boy,” said the old man. “Your sister would have wanted to know what it was for. You did as you were asked.”

He broke the seal with his thumb. The green wax cracked, and fell away in pieces on to his knees. He tore the strip of parchment and lifted the lid.

Lyra could see into the box from where she stood. There was a cloth in it, a piece of an old velvet curtain, and in the cloth there was a thing that caught the firelight: a diadem. A woman’s diadem, old, silver gone dark, with a blue stone set in the front of it and words engraved round the band in a small fine script; the kind of thing her mother’s grandmother might have worn to a ball two hundred years ago.

It was broken.

It had been broken a long time. It had cracked right across, through the band, beside the stone; and round the crack, and down the inside of the band, and spreading out from it like a stain in water, the silver had gone black. Not tarnished: black. Burnt, as if something had been poured into it that was hotter than anything should be, and had eaten its way out.

Abraxas Malfoy looked at it.

The colour went out of his face, from the forehead down, until the marks on his cheeks stood out against the white. His hands were still steady. He held the box without spilling a flake of the broken wax.

“Grandfather?” said Draco. His voice had gone small. “Is it—is it the right one? It is a crown, isn’t it? Like on the list? I didn’t break it. It was like that. I *promise*, it was like that when I—”

“I know it was,” said the old man.

He put the lid back on the box. He did it gently, and precisely, as if the thing inside were asleep and must not be woken. Then he sat with his steady hands flat on the lid, and looked, not at Draco, but at Lyra; and in his pale eyes, which were her own eyes, there was something she had never seen in any grown face in that house, not her father’s on the platform, not her mother’s on the stairs.

He was afraid.

“Somebody,” said Abraxas Malfoy, very quietly, “in that castle, knows what this is.”

The fire ticked. On the table, the chessmen had stopped arguing.

“And I,” said her grandfather, “shall have to tell him so.”

Lyra stood with her back against the door of the east cottage, with the snow melting on the hem of her dressing gown, and the cold of the latch against her spine, and her brother’s frightened face turned up between them; and in the pocket of her dressing gown, where she had put it without thinking when she put on her boots, her hand had found the ring, and closed on it.

The bare metal warmed against her fingers.
