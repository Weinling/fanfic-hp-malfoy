# Interlude: The Seventh Lock

The voice came out of the dark corner of the office, where there had been nothing a moment before, and Harry did not turn round at once.

He knew whose it was. He had known, he thought afterwards, from the creak of the wooden leg, before the voice had said a word; and he stood with his lit wand held out over the edge of the empty shaft and looked down at the swept stone floor ten feet below, where somebody ought to have been, and let himself have one more second of not having to turn round.

“Wands,” said Moody. “On the desk. Both of you. Slowly.”

Sirius was already moving. Harry heard him do it, heard the small click of wood on wood, and then there was nothing to do but straighten up and turn and put his own beside it.

Moody was standing by the window. He had the Marauder’s Map in one hand, folded small, and his wand in the other, and his face in the light of Harry’s wand was quite calm. The blue eye was on Harry. The other one was on Sirius. Neither of them moved.

“A week,” said Moody. “You’ve been watching this office for a week, Potter. Every night. Lovely bit of parchment, this; I could see you coming up the stairs and going down again like a man counting his own front steps.” He put the map away inside his coat. “And tonight you bring *him*.”

“Alastor—” said Sirius.

“Not a word from you. Not one.” Moody did not raise his voice. He had never needed to. “You’re a man the Ministry’s stopped looking for, Black. That isn’t the same thing as a man the Ministry’s finished with, and well you know it. You’re in my office in the middle of the night with a champion of this Tournament, and he’s just opened the seventh lock of my trunk as if he’d been given the key.” The blue eye swivelled, very slowly, to the shaft, and back. “What were you looking for, Potter?”

Harry said nothing.

“No,” said Moody. “I didn’t think you’d say.”

He jerked his wand at the trunk.

“Down.”

It was ten feet. Sirius went first, lowering himself over the lip and dropping the last of it with a grunt, and then turned and put his hands up; and Harry sat on the edge with his legs in the dark and looked up once, at the square of lamplit ceiling, and at Moody standing over him with the wand.

“If you’re right about me,” said Harry, “you’re making a mistake.”

“And if I’m wrong,” said Moody, “I’ve lost a night’s sleep. Down.”

He went down.

The lid came over. The light went, all of it, and then in the dark they heard the locks: seven of them, one after another, each with its own small sound, a click and a turn and a click, unhurried, like a man doing up the buttons on a coat.

Then nothing.

Harry stood on the swept stone floor in the pitch black with his heart going and his hands empty.

There was no one here. There never had been. He had gone straight to the seventh lock as if he had been told where to look, and there was nobody at the bottom of it, and the floor was clean.

Somewhere close to him, Sirius let out a long breath.

“Well,” said Sirius. “That went well.”

Harry nearly laughed. It came up into his throat and stuck there, and turned into something else, and he put his back against the stone wall and slid down it until he was sitting on the floor.

*If you’re wrong—*

*Then I’ll say sorry.*

He had said it on the stairs, an hour ago, quite easily. He had been so sure that he would not have to.

Moody came down twice a day.

They learned to tell the time by him, since there was no other way. The locks would start, far above, one after another, and then the lid would go up and there would be a square of grey light, which was morning, or yellow, which was night; and then Moody would come down a ladder that had not been there before, very slowly, wooden leg first, with his wand out and a covered basket on his arm.

He fed them. That was the thing Harry had not expected. Bread, and cheese, and cold sausages, and a jug of water, and once a slab of fruit cake that Sirius said must have come from the kitchens and Moody said was none of his business. He put the basket down on the stone and stood back from it, and watched them eat; and when they had finished he asked his questions.

“Who’s working you, Potter?”

“Nobody.”

“Who’s working you?”

“Nobody. Who’s working *you*?”

That got him nothing but a long look from the blue eye, which went over Harry as though it were reading the inside of his head off the back of his skull, and found nothing there it liked.

“A champion,” said Moody, on the Thursday night, “that nobody put in for, out of a cup that can’t be fooled. A boy who walks round with his godfather, who’s supposed to be in hiding, and doesn’t tell his own Head of House. Who lends me his map and then watches my door every night for a week. Who goes to the seventh lock of my trunk at midnight like a man going to his own front gate.” He leaned on his staff. “You tell me what that looks like, Potter, from where I’m standing.”

Harry told him the truth, as far as it went. That he had thought somebody was working Moody. That he had thought they were close, and hidden. That the trunk was the one place in the office the map did not show.

“And you thought,” said Moody, “that I’d not know if there was a man living in my own trunk.” The blue eye narrowed. “Who?”

Harry had not meant to say it. He was cold, and tired, and he had been wrong, and the name was the only thing he had left that was still true.

“Barty Crouch’s son.”

Moody went quite still on the ladder.

“Barty Crouch’s son,” he said at last, very quietly, “has been in the ground twelve years. I stood at the grave, boy. I watched them fill it in.” He came down one more rung. “You weren’t born when they put him away. How do you know his name?”

Harry did not answer that. He sat on the cold stone with his back to the wall and looked up at the old man on the ladder, with his scarred face and the chunk out of his nose and his leg, and there were things he could have told Moody about trunks, and he did not tell him any of them. He only looked at him. He was sorry for him, and it was not a thing he could explain.

Moody looked back for a long time. Then he took the basket and went up the ladder, and the lid came down, and the locks went one by one.

On the Friday night he tried something new.

He came down without the basket, and told Sirius to stand against the far wall and stay there, and pointed his wand at Harry’s chest, and said a word Harry did not know.

Harry felt it go into him. It was cold and very thorough, like water running through a pipe, looking for something to push out; it went through him from his chest to his fingers and down into his feet, and searched, and found nothing to push against, and ran out of him again and was gone. He sat and shivered on the stone afterwards and could not stop for some time.

Moody lowered his wand.

“Clean,” he said. He sounded, for the first time, uncertain. “Or good.”

“I’m clean,” said Harry, through his teeth.

“Maybe.” Moody put the wand away. “The ones who are good at it always are.”

The ring on his right hand had been cold since the lid came down.

He had not noticed at first. Then, on the Thursday, sitting in the dark after the first basket, he had turned it on his finger out of habit and felt nothing at all: no warmth, no pull, nothing. He had held it up in front of his face and lit the tip of a wand he did not have, and remembered he did not have it, and sat there with his hand up in the black, feeling for the thing that had told him since February, every night, where she was and how.

Nothing. Plain silver, as though it had never been anything else.

He thought about her, hard, the way you were supposed to. He thought about her on a desk in a classroom on the third floor with the moon on the lake behind her, saying *whether I do is my decision*, and on the edge of a Quidditch pitch on a borrowed black broom with her hair coming down, and bent over a library table, upside down, correcting his Arithmancy without being asked. It did not warm. Wherever this was, the ring did not think it was anywhere.

So she would be holding hers, he thought. Up there. She would be holding it and it would be silver, and she would not know why.

That was worse than the floor, and the cold, and the cheese.

He went over the plan instead, because it was something to do with his head. It was a good plan. It had been a good plan on the Wednesday afternoon, and it was a good plan now, as long as he was out by Sunday. Krum would ask Hermione; Krum always— Krum would ask Hermione, and Hermione would say yes, and keep it to herself, because he had asked her to. And Malfoy would go down to that ship and do what he had asked, because he had asked; and Krum would turn her down, because he had already asked somebody else; and on Sunday night the two of them would be the only champion and the only girl in the Great Hall without anybody, and nobody in Slytherin, or in Wiltshire, would be able to say she had chosen it.

He had not told her. He had told himself there would be time, afterwards, when it was done.

He thought of her going up that gangplank on her own. She would hate it. She would do it beautifully and hate every step.

That night Sirius changed, without a word, and lay down against him in the dark, a great warm heavy weight of dog with his chin on Harry’s knee, and after a while Harry stopped shivering.

He did not sleep. In the small hours he got up.

The shaft went up into the dark, and the walls were rough stone, and he had been feeling them for two days. There were handholds, if you looked; and at the top of the seventh compartment, on the left, a foot below the lid, there was a gap, narrow and black, where one level of the trunk opened on to the next. Moody kept things up there. Harry had heard them moving.

He climbed. He got his fingers over the lip of the gap and his elbow after them, and pulled, and put his head and his right arm through into a dry, close, leafy-smelling dark where something enormous was asleep.

It did not stay asleep.

Something came out of the black like a whip and wound round his wrist and *pulled*, and his wrist was on fire. He did not shout. He had his teeth shut on it. He hung there by one arm in the gap with the thing hauling at him and his feet going on the rough stone, and then there was a weight on his ankle, and teeth in the back of his jeans, and Sirius dragged him down bodily out of the gap and the pair of them fell the last six feet on to the floor of the seventh compartment in a heap.

Far above them, something rustled furiously for a while in the dark, and then went quiet.

Harry lay on his back on the stone, holding his wrist. It was swelling already. He could feel the ridge of it coming up under his fingers, hot as a stove.

“Well,” said Sirius, human again, out of breath, somewhere near his feet. “That’s the way out ruled out, then.”

In the morning Moody came down the ladder with the basket, and looked at the wrist, and did not say *I told you so*, because he had not told them anything.

“South American,” he said. “Took it off a man in Lima in seventy-three. It holds a grudge.” He put the basket down. “Your girl was on the fourth floor yesterday afternoon, Potter. Asking her ring where you were.”

Harry sat up.

“It’s silver, she says. Just silver.” The blue eye rested on him. “Frightened her, I should think. She’s Lucius Malfoy’s, but she’s fourteen.”

“What did you tell her?”

“The truth. That wherever you are, that ring can’t reach you.”

“That isn’t the truth.”

“Every word of it,” said Moody, and went back up the ladder.

On the Saturday he did not climb anything. He sat against the wall with his wrist in his lap, and thought.

If Moody was Moody, and he was; if the trunk was empty, and it had been swept, and had been empty a long time; then the strings were somewhere else. Somebody had put his name in the Goblet. Somebody had stood in the top box at the World Cup and sent that thing up into the sky over the tents. Somebody was close, and hidden, and close enough to do it again; and it was not in this trunk, and it was not the man on the ladder.

He went through it again. He went through it a third time, slowly, the whole year, every face at every table, as if he were going through a pocket for a key he knew he had put there.

He did not know.

It was a very strange feeling. He sat in the dark and turned it over and could not get used to it. He had not not-known anything that mattered for so long that he had forgotten what it was like: the cold of it, the size. It was like stepping off the last stair in the dark and finding there was one more.

“Harry,” said Sirius, after a long while, from the other side of the dark. “You all right?”

“I got it wrong.”

“Yes.” Sirius did not try to make it better. Harry was grateful for that. “Happens.”

“Not to me,” said Harry, and heard how it sounded, and did not take it back.

On the Sunday the locks began at the wrong time.

It was not morning; the morning basket had come and gone hours ago. Harry was on his feet before the third lock had turned. The lid went up, and the light that came down the shaft was the low gold of a winter afternoon, and the face that looked over the edge of it was not Moody’s.

It was long, and old, and bearded, and it wore half-moon spectacles, and it looked down at the two of them for a moment without saying anything at all.

“I gather,” said Professor Dumbledore, “that you have been looking for somebody, Harry.”

Harry looked up at him.

He knew that look. He had known it all his life, both of his— he had known it for years: the blue eyes over the half-moon glasses, light and kind and seeing straight through to the back of whatever you had decided not to say. It was not that look. It was something else. It rested on him from the top of the shaft, and it was kind still, and underneath the kindness, for the first time Harry could remember, there was a question.

“Yes, sir,” said Harry. “I got it wrong.”

“So I understand.” Dumbledore’s voice was very mild. “Alastor has been telling me so, at some length, since a little after four.” He looked at Sirius. “Sirius, I think you had better go, and go a long way, and quickly. Harry—”

“What time is it?”

Dumbledore blinked.

“A little after six.”

The Ball. It was Christmas Day. It was six o’clock on Christmas Day, and the Ball was at eight, and Krum would be there, and Hermione, and all of Slytherin, and Malfoy in whatever her mother had sent her, on her own, at a table, with everybody looking.

“Go,” said Sirius, behind him, and pushed.
