# Chapter 37: The North

The rain came sideways off the lake, and the Durmstrang ship groaned in it like a man in his sleep.

Lyra had cast the Impervius Charm on her cloak at the top of the slope, and it had lasted as far as the gangplank. After that the wind had simply gone round it. By the time a boy with a lantern let her into Professor Karkaroff’s cabin, she was wet to the knees and her hair was coming down, and the whole room was moving: the lamp swinging on its chain, the shadows going up the walls and down again, the mast overhead letting out a long, slow creak every time the wind leaned on it, as though it were thinking of giving up.

Karkaroff did not get up.

He was sitting at his table in his shirtsleeves, with no furs on; she had never seen him without them; and he had not shaved, and the goatee had gone ragged at the edges, and he looked as if he had not slept since Easter. There was a glass at his elbow with something brown in it, and a bottle beside the glass, and he did not offer her any.

“Miss Malfoy.” He waved her to the chair opposite. “Sit. Sit. You will forgive me. It is not a night for ceremony.”

She sat.

“The time has come,” said Karkaroff, “to choose.”

He said it to the glass, not to her. Then he looked up, and his eyes were very bright and not steady.

“You read the *Prophet*. You will have seen that a woman from the Ministry has gone missing. Jorkins. Bertha Jorkins. She went off on her holidays last summer and has not come back, and your Ministry, which cannot keep its own clerks, says she has got lost.” He laughed, a short, unpleasant sound. “I have friends abroad. Not many, now. Enough. She was seen in Albania, Miss Malfoy. In a village inn, at the edge of the forest. And then she was not seen anywhere.”

Lyra had read something about a missing witch, once, at the bottom of a page, between an advertisement for broom polish and a letter about goblins. She had not given it a second thought.

“Albania,” said Karkaroff softly, “is a very quiet country. Full of old forests. A good country for a man who wishes to be quiet for a long time.” His hand had gone to his left forearm, through his shirtsleeve, and was rubbing at it, slowly, as it had in December; but there was something different in it now. He was not rubbing at an ache. He was holding on. “You understand me.”

She understood him. She did not intend to say so.

“I’m afraid I don’t follow the foreign news very closely, sir,” said Lyra. “My father says it’s mostly gossip.”

“Your father.” Karkaroff leaned across the table, and the lamp swung, and his face went yellow and then dark and then yellow again. “Your father knows. Do not tell me he does not know. He has felt it. We have all of us felt it, since the summer, every one of us who—” He stopped himself. His eyes went to the door, as they had in November. Then they came back to her, and he lowered his voice to almost nothing. “It is getting stronger. Every week. It is not finished yet, but it will be. And when it is, there will be no more choosing for anybody. You understand? Those who are already there will be remembered. Those who are not—”

He drank, suddenly, the whole glass.

“I have done my part,” he said. “I want that known. I have done everything the letter asked. Everything. The ship will be ready. You will tell him that? You will tell your father that Igor Karkaroff did not hesitate? That when we are all together in the north, he will remember who—”

He stopped.

He had heard it himself. She saw him hear it: the word going out of his mouth into the swinging lamplight, and his face, a moment after, as a man’s face goes when he has stepped on something in the dark and it has moved.

Lyra kept her face where it was.

There was only one house in the north. There had only ever been one, in her whole life: a house on a cold coast that was in no register in the country, where she had been taken exactly once, at seven, and told afterwards never to mention to anybody, not even Crabbe. A boy in an empty classroom had once said *somewhere up north* to her, the night the Chamber was closed, as carelessly as if he were remarking on the weather, and she had spent two years wondering how he could possibly know.

*Everything the letter asked.* She had carried it up his gangplank herself, in November, in green wax, addressed in a hand she did not know.

“Of course, sir,” said Lyra. “I’ll tell him.”

She smiled at him. It was the smile her mother used on bishops, and it had never once failed her. Karkaroff looked at it as a drowning man looks at a rope thrown from the bank, and let out his breath, and poured himself another glass.

She walked back up the slope in the rain with her hood down and her charm in pieces, and did not bother to mend it. By the time she reached the doors she was wet to the skin, and she did not feel any of it.

The Entrance Hall was full of Gryffindors.

They had come in from Herbology a few minutes before her, by the look of it, and were standing about in a wet, cheerful knot at the foot of the marble staircase, shaking out their cloaks, with Potter in the middle of it. Somebody had made him laugh. Lyra saw it from the doors: his head back, his hair plastered flat with rain, one of the Weasley twins with an arm round his neck.

“—telling you, he’ll put Skrewts in it. He’ll have been saving them. He’ll put in the biggest one he’s got and tell you it’s *misunderstood*—”

“He wouldn’t.” Potter was grinning. “They’re not even legal. Even Hagrid—” He stopped, and considered. “He would, wouldn’t he.”

The whole knot of them collapsed laughing.

Lyra had taken two steps across the flagstones towards them. Her mouth was already open on his name. It was what she did now; it was what she had done every morning since January, at the foot of the dungeon steps, in front of anybody who cared to look.

“Har—”

She shut her mouth on it.

*Those who are already there will be remembered.*

Nobody had heard her. Nobody had turned round. Lyra stood in the doorway with the rain running off her into a puddle on the flagstones, and watched him laugh, and then turned and went down the dungeon steps the other way, quickly, before he could look up.

She did not go down to dinner.

She changed out of her wet things, and sat on her bed with the hangings drawn and the ring in her hand, and for a long while did nothing. Then she put it on, and held it, and thought of him, hard, as she had thought of him once on a slope above a ship in December, when it had lain in her palm as cold as a coin; and this time it warmed. It warmed, and pulsed, warm and cool and warm, and somewhere up in a tower the other one would be doing the same against his finger.

She had never once called him to her for something like this. She went up to the classroom on the third floor, behind the tapestry of the knight asleep on his horse, and sat down at a desk by the window to wait.

The rain had stopped. The clouds were going off the lake in long grey rags, and there was a moon behind them somewhere, and she watched the light come and go on the water and did not think about anything in particular, which took a great deal of effort.

He came in under the cloak. She did not hear the door; she felt the air change, and then he was pulling the cloak off by the desk beside her with his hair standing on end, looking at her.

“You’re soaked,” said Potter.

“I changed.”

“Your hair’s soaked.” He took out his wand and pointed it at her head before she could stop him, and a gust of warm air went through her hair and out the other side and left it dry and full of static, standing out round her face. He looked at the result. “You look like the lake won.”

It was a good joke. On any other night she would have had something for it. She looked at him, and could not find anything, and after a moment he stopped smiling.

“Lyra. What’s happened?”

“Sit down,” she said. “And swear something.”

He sat down on the desk opposite, slowly.

“Swear you won’t lie to me. Not for the next ten minutes. Whatever I ask.”

He looked at her for a moment. She thought he would ask why, and he did not.

“All right,” said Potter. “I swear.”

She folded her hands on the desk.

“What do you think of my father?”

He had not expected that. She saw it go into him and stop.

“A man I owe something to,” said Potter.

“Not that.”

She knew what that was: the diary. She had sworn him for the other one.

Potter took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes, and put them back on, and looked at her through them, and his face had gone cool and steady and a long way off, the face he wore for things that mattered.

“You want the truth.”

“I want the truth.”

“A man who lives off the Ministry’s favours,” said Potter, “and spends his money trying to pull it down. Who stood in a field last summer in a mask, with a Muggle family turning upside down in the air over his head, and shook hands with Fudge the next week at a charity lunch. Who put a diary in an eleven-year-old girl’s cauldron to get even with her father, and very nearly killed her with it.” He paused. “And who brought the diary into this castle, in the end, where it could be got at. Without meaning to.” He held her eyes. “A useful enemy.”

Lyra nodded.

She had known what he would say. She had known since the lake, at least; probably since the train. It was not a surprise to hear it. It was only that it was the first time he had ever said it to her, in words, with nothing over the top of it to make it easier to carry.

“Thank you,” she said.

“Lyra—”

“No. You’ve said yours. Now I’ll say mine, and you will listen to it, because nobody has ever said it to you, I imagine, and somebody ought to.” She did not raise her voice. “I understand my father.”

He did not interrupt.

“I don’t mean I approve of the field. I don’t. I don’t care for masks, and I think what they did to those Muggles was vulgar, and stupid, and beneath him.” She meant every word of that, and was surprised by how much. “But I understand what he wants, and I think a good deal of it is right, and I’m not going to sit here and pretend to you that I don’t.”

She leaned forward.

“Some people are better at things than other people. That isn’t wickedness, Potter. It’s arithmetic. The Muggles know it perfectly well; they put the clever ones and the rich ones at the top and take their hats off to them, and nobody calls it a crime. And we can do things no Muggle has ever been able to do, not one, not since the beginning of the world, and what does the Ministry do with us? It makes us hide. There are families in this country older than this castle living in two rooms on nothing, because the Statute won’t let them lift a finger, and on the other side of their hedge there is a Muggle with a motor car and a television and no idea he’s next door to anything at all. That’s not the natural order of things. That’s the order turned upside down, on purpose, by people who are frightened of what we are.”

She said it in her own voice, as she had heard it said at her father’s table, over the port, a hundred times; and the odd thing was that, saying it, she believed it, all of it, every word, and at the same time could hear it, from the outside, the way he must be hearing it.

Potter was quiet for a moment.

“Hermione’s parents are dentists,” he said. “In Hampstead. They’re the Muggles on the other side of the hedge.” He said it quite evenly. “If your father ever gets the world turned the right way up, the way you mean it—where’s she?”

Lyra looked at the window, where the moon had come out properly now and laid a long white road across the lake towards the Durmstrang ship.

Potter did not press it. He sat on the desk with his hands between his knees and let it lie there between them.

After a while, he said, in a different voice, “You’ve been on that ship.”

“Yes.”

“In this.” He nodded at the window, the wet glass, the rags of cloud. “What did Karkaroff want?”

She thought of the lamp swinging, and the bottle, and a man holding on to his own arm. *When we are all together in the north.* She thought of a house on a cold coast that was in no register in the country, and a boy who had known about it before she had ever said a word.

“Nothing,” said Lyra.

Somewhere, she thought, a Sneakoscope would have gone off. Potter looked at her. She held his eyes and did not look away, as she had held Moody’s, and after a long moment he nodded slowly, as though he had been handed something and had decided not to turn it over.

“Right,” he said.

He did not ask again. He picked up his cloak from the desk, and stood there with it in his hands, and looked as though he were going to say something else, and then he did not. He put the cloak on, and was gone, and she heard the door.

Lyra sat by the window until the moon had gone right across the lake.

She had made him swear, and he had kept it, every word. She had needed only one.

In the morning he was not at the foot of the dungeon steps.

Lyra came up them at the usual time, with Crabbe and Goyle, and looked, once, at the place by the wall where he had leaned every morning since January with his hands in his pockets; and there was nobody there but a Hufflepuff second-year doing up a shoe. Crabbe looked too, and then at Lyra, and said nothing whatever, and went in to breakfast.

Lyra went up to Arithmancy by herself.

On the seventh floor the windows of the long corridor looked south, over the grounds, and the storm had left everything rinsed and sharp and very green. She stopped at the third window, as she sometimes did, to see what the weather meant to do; and she stood there instead with her bag on her shoulder and her hand flat on the cold stone of the sill.

The Quidditch pitch had gone.

The stands were still there, and the goal hoops at either end. But the long smooth oval of grass between them, where she had flown every autumn and spring since her second year, was not grass any more. It had been ruled across, overnight, with lines: long low dark lines, crossing and turning and doubling back on themselves, running everywhere and nowhere, like writing in a language she did not know. Hedges. Little hedges, no higher than a man’s knee, planted in the night. A wizard in Ministry robes was walking slowly down one of the alleys between them with a tape measure, and another was following him with a clipboard, and behind them, even as she watched, the hedge they had just passed was very slightly taller than it had been.

It was growing.

By the end of the month it would be over their heads. Over anybody’s head. Somebody would walk into the middle of it, on the twenty-fourth, and the walls would close up behind him, and nobody in the stands would be able to see what was in there with him.

Lyra stood at the window with her hand on the sill and watched the hedges grow, a quarter of an inch at a time, in the clean wet light; and thought about the north.
