# Chapter 37: The North

The rain came sideways off the lake, and the Durmstrang ship groaned in it like a man in his sleep.

Lyra had cast the Impervius Charm on her cloak at the top of the slope, and it had lasted as far as the gangplank. After that the wind had simply gone round it. By the time a boy with a lantern let her into Professor Karkaroff’s cabin, she was wet to the knees and her hair was coming down, and the whole room was moving: the lamp swinging on its chain, the shadows going up the walls and down again, the mast overhead letting out a long, slow creak every time the wind leaned on it, as though it were thinking of giving up.

Karkaroff did not get up.

He was sitting at his table in his shirtsleeves, with no furs on, which she had never seen; and he had not shaved, and the goatee had gone ragged at the edges, and he looked as if he had not slept since Easter. There was a glass at his elbow with something brown in it, and a bottle beside the glass, and he did not offer her any.

“Miss Malfoy.” He waved her to the chair opposite. “Sit. Sit. You will forgive me. It is not a night for ceremony.”

She sat.

“The time has come,” said Karkaroff, “to choose.”

He said it to the glass, not to her. Then he looked up, and his eyes were very bright and not at all steady.

“You read the *Prophet*. You will have seen that a woman from the Ministry has gone missing. Jorkins. Bertha Jorkins. She went off on her holidays last summer and has not come back, and your Ministry, which cannot keep its own clerks, says she has got lost.” He laughed, a short, unpleasant sound. “I have friends abroad. Not many, now. Enough. She was seen in Albania, Miss Malfoy. In a village inn, at the edge of the forest. And then she was not seen anywhere.”

Lyra had read something about a missing witch, once, at the bottom of a page, between an advertisement for broom polish and a letter about goblins. She had not given it a second thought.

“Albania,” said Karkaroff softly, “is a very quiet country. Full of old forests. A good country for a man who wishes to be quiet for a long time.” His hand had gone to his left forearm, through his shirtsleeve, and was rubbing at it, slowly, as it had in December; but there was something different in it now. He was not rubbing at an ache. He was holding on. “You understand me.”

She understood him. She did not intend to say so.

“I’m afraid I don’t follow the foreign news very closely, sir,” said Lyra. “My father says it’s mostly gossip.”

“Your father.” Karkaroff leaned across the table, and the lamp swung, and his face went yellow and then dark and then yellow again. “Your father knows. Do not tell me he does not know. He has felt it. We have all of us felt it, since the summer, every one of us who—” He stopped himself. His eyes went to the door, as they had in November. Then they came back to her, and he lowered his voice to almost nothing. “It is getting stronger. Every week. It is not finished yet, but it will be. And when it is, there will be no more choosing for anybody. You understand? Those who are already there will be remembered. Those who are not—”

He drank, suddenly, the whole glass.

“I have done my part,” he said. “I want that known. I have done everything the letter asked. Everything. The ship will be ready. You will tell him that? You will tell your father that Igor Karkaroff did not hesitate? That when we are all together in the north, he will remember who—”

He stopped.

He had heard it himself. She saw him hear it: the word going out of his mouth into the swinging lamplight, and his face, a moment after, as a man’s face goes when he has stepped on something in the dark and it has moved.

Lyra did not let it reach her own.

There was only one house in the north. There had only ever been one, in her whole life: a house on a cold coast that was in no register in the country, which her father had made very sure of, and where she had been taken exactly once, at seven, and told afterwards never to mention to anybody, not even Crabbe. A boy on a train had once said *somewhere up north* to her as carelessly as if he were remarking on the weather, and she had spent two years wondering how he could possibly know.

“Of course, sir,” said Lyra. “I’ll tell him.”

She smiled at him. It was her mother’s smile, the one for the wives of junior ministers, and it had never once failed her. Karkaroff looked at it as a drowning man looks at a rope, and let out his breath, and poured himself another glass.

She walked back up the slope in the rain with her hood down and her charm in pieces, and did not bother to mend it. By the time she reached the doors she was wet to the skin, and she did not feel any of it.
