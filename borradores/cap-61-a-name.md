# Chapter 61: A Name

“One does so worry about the Ministry, Professor,” said Lyra.

It was the first Saturday of March, and she was on her third cup. Professor Umbridge had taken to having her in on Saturdays. It was something to do with the Squad, officially. The kittens were at play. The pink chair still stood beside the pink chair, with the small table between them, and Lyra sat in it with her ankles crossed and did not look at the little table under the window.

“Do you, dear? How very nice. So few young people do.”

“My father says it’s the junior people one should feel for. He says nobody thinks of them. All those poor quiet men in the basement, with no prospects. He was saying that some of them have been down there for thirty years, on the same floor, and that they can’t even get a transfer.”

She had come at it from a long way off. It had taken three Saturdays. She had started with the Minister’s health, and gone on by way of the price of owl post, and she had been bored for her country. One never asked the question one wanted answered. One asked the question next to it, and one looked a little stupid, and one took a biscuit.

Professor Umbridge’s wide mouth widened. She laid down her cup, and patted her lips with a small lace napkin.

“Your father is a *dear* man,” she said. “But I’m afraid he has been listening to Broderick Bode.”

Lyra took a wafer. She had got rather used to them.

“I don’t believe I know the name, Professor.”

“No, why should you? Nobody does. That’s rather the idea, down there. They’re called Unspeakables, dear, which I always think is so *unfortunate*. He has applied for a transfer four times, to Magical Games of all things, and four times I have had the pleasure of retaining him. One can’t have them wandering off upstairs. He has been looking after those dreadful shelves since before you were born.” She gave her little girlish cough. “A *most* regular little man. In at nine, out at six. He lodges with his sister in Battersea. He walks home by the river on a Tuesday and a Friday, whatever the weather, because she has the Ladies’ Charms Circle, and there’s no supper, and he has a pie. I have it all in his file. I do so think that one ought to know one’s people.”

“How very sad,” said Lyra.

“Isn’t it? Another wafer, dear. You’re looking peaky.”

She took another. It was pink, and it tasted of nothing whatever, and she ate it all.

She had meant to tell him.

She wished to have that noted. She had got as far as the seventh floor with the words ready. It was a Sunday, reports, and he was sitting on the rug with his back to the chair and his long legs stuck out, chewing a sugar quill and trying to think of something humiliating to do with a hot-water bottle. He looked up when she came in. He had got shadows under his eyes that had not been there a fortnight ago; he had a detention with Snape every Tuesday, and it seemed to be something to do with floors. He grinned at her. It started at one corner.

*Nits will not do*, she thought. *There is a man.*

She sat down. She took up the ink.

She had three reasons. She went through them that night, behind her hangings, with some care, for she was a person who liked to know what she was doing. The first was Draco. There was a very small pale head at the end of the Slytherin table, and somebody had to be useful, and it was not going to be him. The second was that if she told Potter he would say something. He would go quiet. He would look at the fire, and then he’d tell her what to do about it; or he would not tell her, and he’d go away and do it himself, and she would find out from a newspaper. He would put his hand on it. And the third was that he would look at her.

He would not mean to. He would be extremely careful. He had got a look now, for when she mentioned Umbridge, and she had seen it in the Great Hall with a jar in his hand.

She lay on her back and regarded the green light moving on the ceiling. It was a strange thing. She had heard all of that before. She had heard it in an Owlery, with a great many owls looking on, from somebody who had been caught out; she had thought at the time that they were the three feeblest reasons she had ever heard in her life.

*Nothing moves without asking.* That had been her. She had said it on a hill, with her teeth chattering.

Well, it was only a name. It was a man in a basement whom she had never met. She did not know the colour of his eyes. He did not owe her anything and he had never dropped a card in front of her in a queue. That was what recommended it.

She noticed what she had just said. She turned over, and punched the pillow, and lay looking at the hangings for some time.

“Greer,” she said. “I want you to do something for me.”

It was six o’clock on the Tuesday morning, and it was still dark. The lake went by outside the high window. Goyle was sitting on the edge of her bed, in a vast flannel nightgown, doing up her shoes. She had been getting up at six for some weeks, it had emerged, to be first at the kitchens, on account of something that an elf had told her about porridge.

Lyra held out the letter. It was a single sheet, sealed, with nothing on the outside. She had written it on Monday night in the bathroom, by herself, with the door locked. It had taken no time. It was the best hand she had ever written.

“It’s to go up to the Owlery before breakfast. The grey one, on the third perch; he knows the way. I’ve got rounds. I’d rather not be seen.”

Goyle looked at the letter. She did not take it. She looked at it as she had looked at a tin with a terrier on the lid, in June, with both hands in her lap. Her big soft face had nothing much on it at this hour. Then she looked up.

“Is it a nice letter?” said Goyle.

Lyra opened her mouth.

In five years nobody had ever asked her that. Greer Goyle had carried things for her since they were eleven. She had carried tins and notes and sealed envelopes and a dead Bludger; she had once gone up to the hospital wing with a thing that might have gone off, scarlet to the ears, and sat down afterwards and not eaten a Chocolate Frog. She had asked for nothing. She had never read a word that wasn’t on a sweet wrapper.

“It’s to my father.”

“I know.” Goyle’s voice was as it always was: slow and rather thick, as though it were coming up from a long way under a good dinner. “Only you’ve got the face on. You had it in November. You had it when you went to tea, that Monday, before the Hufflepuff. I don’t know what it’s for, it’s not for me to know. Vanessa says I’m not to ask you things.” She finished with the left shoe, and tied a large careful bow. She sat with her hands on her knees. “I don’t want to, Lyra.”

The lake moved on the ceiling.

“I beg your pardon?”

“I don’t want to take it. I’ll do anything else.”

She had gone very red. She did not look away. It was the first time Lyra had ever seen her do something that frightened her, and she appeared to be doing it as she did everything: slowly, with her whole weight. She felt about in the pocket of her dressing gown. She drew out something wrapped in a napkin, and she got up, and came across the cold floor in her shoes and her enormous nightgown, and put it into Lyra’s free hand, and folded her fingers over it.

“It’s a bacon one,” said Goyle. “Eat that first.”

She went out. Lyra heard her going up the stairs, one at a time, towards the kitchens.

She stood there. She was still holding the letter out. After some time she lowered it, and she looked at the thing in her other hand, which was warm, and had started to come through the napkin.

She did not eat it. She put it down on Goyle’s bed, quite carefully, and she took up her cloak.

It was a long way up to the Owlery at that time of the morning. The stairs were very cold, and the wind came in at the arrow-slits. She had not set foot there since June. There was the straw. There were the owls, in their rows, all awake, and every one of them turned its head. She did not look at the far window. She found the grey one on the third perch, and held up the letter, and he put out his leg, as he always did.

*Thank you, my treasure*, said her father. It came on the Friday. *He is so pleased.*

That was all. The hand was rather better.

It was a remarkable thing, how little difference it made. She had thought it would make some. She went up to the seventh floor that same night, it being on the timetable, and Potter had got hold of a pack of Exploding Snap from somewhere. He was very bad at that, too. They played until the fire went down, for points. He lost eleven times by her count, and he sat there singed and smelling of burnt toast, disputing the rules. She laughed. She heard herself do it; it was a perfectly good laugh, and it came in the right place. She kissed him on the singed bit, because it made him yelp. She went to sleep on his shoulder, and woke, and did up her hair.

“You’ve gone thin,” said Goyle, at breakfast. She was looking at her plate.

“I am perfectly well, Greer.”

Goyle pushed the toast rack an inch towards her, and said no more about it. She had not mentioned the Tuesday, and she was not going to. Two places along, Crabbe turned over a page of the *Prophet*, and read something, and turned it back, and went on reading. Lyra took a piece of toast. There was a bird singing somewhere up in the rafters. The snow had started to go. Every day, in the middle of all the other things one had to do, she looked in the paper. She did it as one puts one’s tongue to a tooth. There was nothing. There went on being nothing, and she stopped holding her breath at the page with the small notices. He had a pie on Tuesdays. He was very likely having it now. Very likely they had only wanted to talk to him. Her father had been talking to people at the Ministry for twenty years, and it was mostly a matter of luncheon.

It was a Sunday, the seventeenth. It had rained, and they were on the floor, with the report half done and yesterday’s *Prophet* in pieces round them, because Potter had wanted the Quidditch and she had wanted the shipping. He was lying on his front. She had her back against the chair. She had got him a very good thing about a hot-water bottle, and she was reading it over, with her feet on his calf, in a state of something she had stopped troubling to find a name for.

He had gone quiet. He had stopped chewing the quill.

She looked up. He had pulled a sheet of the paper towards him, an inside page, one of those that she had already been through. He was propped on his elbows and his hair was hanging in his eyes. He had his finger on something low down in the right-hand column, among the auction notices. All the cheerfulness had gone out of his face, as water goes out of a basin.

“Oh, no,” said Potter.

“What is it?”

“I’d forgotten about him.” He said it to the page. “Oh, Merlin. I’d completely forgotten about him. Poor sod.”

It was only four lines. She had not seen it. She took it out from under his finger. It had been on page nine.

*Mr Broderick Bode, 58, a clerk of the Ministry of Magic, was discovered on Thursday evening in the kitchen of his sister’s house in Battersea, where he had been for some hours. It is understood that he has received the Dementor’s Kiss. A spokesman said that the Ministry deeply regretted this tragic accident, and reminded the public to keep their doors closed after dark. Mr Bode is being cared for at St Mungo’s.*

She read it through. It seemed to be taking a long time. There was a smell of wet wool from somewhere, and of burnt toast, and the rain was running down the small square window. She was aware of her feet. She kept them where they were. She had a room for this. She had worked on it for six months, on Thursdays, and she walked into it now, and shut the door behind her, and there was a breakfast table, and the light coming in sideways.

“Who is he?” said Lyra.

It came out quite well. It had a little frown in it.

“He’s nobody. He’s a— He was in the Department of Mysteries. He’s one of the ones that look after the—” He sat up, and pushed his hand through his hair, and left it there. “I knew him. I met him. Not met; he was in St Mungo’s, in the other one. At Christmas. He was in the bed by the window, and he thought he was a teapot. They’d got to him. Somebody put the Imperius on him, to get him to fetch something, and it went wrong, and it took his mind. He was just starting to get better; he’d started to say a few words. And somebody sent him a plant.” He stopped. He looked sick. “I *knew* that. I’ve known that since I was eleven. I’ve got a list, I had a whole list of people, I went through it every summer. And I lost him. He’s a man with a name, he’s got a sister, and I knew they’d come for him, and it’s two years since I even thought about it. It’s been so— I’ve been—”

He looked round at the fire, and the timetable, and the bald rug. He looked at the sheets of paper with their hot-water bottle, and then, and it was very quick, at her. She saw him decide not to finish that.

“I could have told someone,” said Potter. “I could have told Dumbledore. One word. That’s all it would have taken. Nobody would have had to get hurt.”

There was a thing that one did. She knew the thing. It was, she supposed, the only thing in the whole of her life that she had ever been properly taught. She looked at the boy on the floor in front of her, with his ink and his shadows and his hand in his hair, who had just looked down a column of the *Daily Prophet* and picked up what was hers, the whole weight of it, without a word, and put it on his own back.

He had only to look at her. She kept her face exactly as it was.

“You couldn’t possibly have known,” said Lyra. “It says it was an accident.”

She reached out. Her hand was perfectly steady. She laid it against the side of his head, and pushed the hair back, and left it there a moment. He shut his eyes, and turned his face into it, and breathed in. It was the sort of thing that he did now, without thinking. She could feel the place on his temple where the blood was going.

“It wasn’t your fault,” she said. “Potter. Look at me. There was nothing you could have done.”

That was true. She noticed that it was true.

After a little while he nodded. He rubbed his face with his sleeve, and drew a long breath; and she took up the sheet with the four lines on it, and folded it over, and laid it with the others, and turned to the back of the paper. There was a crossword.

“Seven across,” said Lyra. “*Goblin for ‘silver’*. Four letters. You must know that, you’ve done it before.”
