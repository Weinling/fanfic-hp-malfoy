# Chapter 74: Ask Me

“I think I ate him,” said Harry.

He hadn’t meant to say it. He had meant to say something about Charms.

Tomorrow was the third of June. Charms was at half past nine, in the Great Hall, with the Practical after lunch, and it was the first time in eight days that he had got as far as the chair. Goldstein had gone off to be sick somewhere. The wall had let them in at two. There was a report to do, and it lay on the rug with nothing on it, and through the small square window you could see a strip of perfectly blue sky, which had been going on all week, like somebody whistling at a funeral.

He had not slept. He had probably slept, here and there, on a Tuesday; he had come to, once, in Herbology, with his cheek on a tray of something. Otherwise he had lain on his bed looking at the blanket on the bed beside him, which was pulled up straight. Ron had not said a word to him in eight days. He didn’t do it in any particular way. He passed the salt if you asked. He had taken his things across to the far end of the Gryffindor table, and Hermione sat beside him with her hands in her lap and did not eat, and Ginny, who didn’t know anything, had looked from one end of the bench to the other on the Tuesday and said, “What’s the matter with all of you? Has someone died?” Nobody had said anything to Malfoy about the third floor. She had come to the fifth-floor landing on the Sunday morning, and stood there, and he had looked at her, and she’d looked at him. Neither of them was sorry. They had neither of them said so. She had taken a corner of his sleeve between her finger and thumb, for about a second, as you’d test a fabric, and he had gone on breathing, and that was as much as anybody was going to get.

She was on the rug now, with her back against the fender and a stack of revision cards, and she had been hearing him on Banishing Charms for an hour and a half in the tone of somebody feeding a very stupid horse. She had something in the pocket of her robes that she kept touching. He had noticed that. He was in no condition to notice anything else. She had brought a travelling cloak with her, too, on the second of June, and it was hanging over the back of the chair, by his head.

She looked up from the card.

“I beg your pardon?” said Malfoy.

There was still time to say something about Charms.

“Him. The boy.” His voice had a dry, scraped sound, like something being pulled along a floor. “The one you keep—with the egg. He’d been there since September. Hermione asked me where he went, in November. I said I didn’t know. I said he hadn’t gone anywhere. I’ve been looking. I go in there and I look, every night, I look for him, and there’s the bed and there’s the chair and there’s nobody—” He stopped, and tried it again from a different side. “You said somebody came. In Christmas week, with your chess set. He came. He didn’t know anything. He’d never had anyone in his whole life, and he went down there by himself in his slippers, because you were on your own. And that was him. And in January he woke up one morning and there was this—this—I came in. Whatever I am. Seventeen years old, I came in through his head, and I sat down in his chair, and I’ve been answering to his name ever since. You keep asking me if I remember. You lie there looking so *happy*, and you tell me how he laughed. There isn’t any him. There’s only what was left. I think I ate him, Malfoy. I think I got hungry, and I ate him, and you’ve been in love with what I did it with.”

The fire ticked. He looked at his hands.

He had never said that to anybody. He had not known it had words. He sat with his elbows on his knees, feeling quite calm and rather sick, and he waited for it to have happened.

She didn’t say anything for some time. She had let the card sink down on to her knee. She looked at him with her head on one side, with her small pale frowning face, down the column and up again, and he had seen her look like that at a page of Arithmancy that somebody had told her couldn’t be done.

“What did he taste of?” said Malfoy.

He stared at her.

“I’m asking for information. I should have thought he’d be stringy. There can’t have been very much on him. All that time in a cupboard.” She picked a bit of lint off the card. Her voice was entirely serious. “Did you do it all at once, or did you leave the feet?”

A noise came out of him.

It was a laugh. It had started as a laugh; he would have sworn to that. It got up out of the bottom of his chest, a great cracked bray of a thing, and it went on getting up, and he heard it, and he saw her eyes change. Somewhere on the way out it turned over. He tried to get hold of it. He tried to say *Don’t*, and his mouth wouldn’t shut. He had his arm across his face, and his whole body had started to shake, and he slid down out of the chair on to the bald rug, because there was nothing in him any more that was holding him up in it, and he was crying.

He had done this on Hermione’s desk, like a tap left on. He had done it on this rug in April, for Sirius, with his hand over his eyes. He had never done it for himself. It was an ugly, whooping, snotty noise, like a child’s, like a small boy’s in a cupboard, and he couldn’t stop it, and he couldn’t breathe. It was Ron. It was *I was holding her books*. It was a girl behind a shed with a box of matches, and sixteen days, and a dog with his head on one side, and a boy with egg on his tie who’d said *thank you* to the armour; and all of it came up together, and it had been going to come up for about four years.

He felt his glasses being taken off. Somebody folded them, with two small clicks. A handkerchief was put into his hand, very old and soft, and closed in his fingers.

She didn’t tell him it wasn’t true. He would remember that. She did not say one word. Lyra Malfoy, who had an answer to the Sorting Hat, got across the rug on her knees, in her good robes, and sat down on the floor beside him with her shoulder against his shoulder and her legs stuck out in front of her, and stayed there, looking at the fire, for as long as it took.

It took a while.

The strip of sky had gone a deeper colour. He lay with his head against the seat of the chair, emptied out, with a stuffed nose and a sort of ringing peacefulness behind the eyes. He felt as if he had been very ill, and somebody had changed the sheets.

“Sorry,” said Harry. It came out as *Dorry*.

“Don’t be disgusting.”

She had a look on her face that he couldn’t make out, without his glasses. She gave them back to him. He put them on. She had taken the thing out of her pocket, and she was holding it in her lap with both hands, and he saw it was a letter. It had been folded small, and carried about, and handled until the creases had gone soft. There was a green seal, broken. And there was the clock on the shelf, which the room had never had before today, and which said twenty to six.

She saw him look.

“It came at breakfast,” said Malfoy.

She held it out. She did not read it to him, as she read the others. He took it. The hand was thin and sloping and it shook. He had seen a good deal of it this year, and he thought it had got worse.

*My darling. Your grandfather is failing. It cannot be more than a matter of days, and I need not tell you what must be settled before it is. Draco has been sent for already and comes by the noon carriage. He wishes the family to be at his table tonight: all of it. He was most particular. The enclosed will act at six o’clock. You will wish to wear the grey.*

He read it twice. Something small and hard had slid down to the fold of the paper, and he shook it out on to his palm. It was a coat button, a plain horn one, off a child’s coat. He tipped it quickly on to the rug.

*He wishes.* There was only one person in that house who was ever written about without a name.

He sat holding the letter. He was aware of the fire on one side of his face, and of her shoulder not being against his any more. He thought it through. He found that he could; there was nothing left in him to get in the way. It went by in perfect order. An old man was dying, and there was a table, and a chair at it with a boy of eleven already sitting there, with his hair combed. He would have come by the noon carriage. He’d be swinging his feet. He had a bow tie. In that dining room, tonight, the man from the south wing would look round the table and count; and she’d been at Hogwarts for five years learning to be counted, she had been doing it since she was four, and she would have to walk in there in the grey, with her chin up, and sit down, and be there, because if she wasn’t, there would be a space. And it would be next to Draco.

Well. That was that, then.

It was strange. He had thought, when this came, that it would feel like something. He had been frightened of it since he was thirteen. He had got it wrong in the Owlery, with his leg bleeding, and laughed; he had taken it to the Odeon, with Sirius in the next seat. He knew what she was, and what was hers; he had always known. She’d said so. She had told him to his face, and it was the truest thing that anyone had ever told him, and now it had come, on a nice evening, and there was nothing to be done about it. He didn’t ask himself what he wanted. It did not seem to come into it. He looked for the part of him that would have asked, and he’d left it on the rug with everything else.

You didn’t ask a person not to go to her brother.

He got up. It took him two tries; his knees had gone. He stood for a moment holding on to the arm of the chair. Then he took the cloak off the back of it. It was heavier than it looked, and lined with something grey, and he shook it out by the shoulders and found which way round it went. He had seen Mr Weasley do this. He’d never done it for anybody, in either life.

He held it open.

“You’ll want to go down by the east stairs,” said Harry. His voice was quite steady, if you ignored the nose. “Filch is on the front. You’ve got twenty minutes. It’ll have to be outside the gates, won’t it, for that to work. I can walk you as far as the— I’ll get the Cloak.”

She didn’t move.

She was still sitting on the floor. She looked up at him, with her hands empty in her lap. She looked at his face, which must have been something to see; and at the cloak, held out in his two hands, a little crooked; and at his face again. He had seen every look she had. He had made a study of them. There was the one for Weasleys, and the one for her father, and the one with the small dent at each side of her nose. He’d never seen this. Nothing about it moved at all. It was the way you might look at a sum you’d been doing for five years, on a great many sheets of paper, when you turn over the last one and it comes out.

“What are you doing?” said Malfoy.

“You’ll be late.”

“You aren’t going to say anything.”

“What?”

“You’re not going to *say* anything. You have no one. You have just sat on this floor and howled like a dog. You’re standing there with your nose running, holding up my *coat*.” Her voice shook on that, and she stopped, and she took a breath through her nose, and got up. She did it slowly. She stood a yard off with her hands by her sides. “It hasn’t even crossed your mind,” she said. “Has it? It hasn’t so much as occurred to you.”

He looked at her. He did not know what she was talking about. He stood there, with the arms aching a little, and he tried to think what he’d done.

She held out her hand. For a moment he thought she wanted the cloak, and he moved to give it to her. It was the letter that she took. She took it out of the fingers that held the collar. She looked down at it, at her father’s hand, shaking, and the place where it said *My darling*, as if she were making sure of the spelling.

Then she turned round and dropped it in the fire.

It lay there on the coals for a second or two, curling, and went up all at once. There was a small flare of yellow in the grate. A flake of ash lifted and turned over.

“What are you doing?” said Harry.

It came out in a whisper. He was still holding up the cloak.

“Ask me,” said Malfoy.

“What?”

“What you asked me in the Owlery.” She had turned back. She had gone a dreadful colour, and her chin was up, and her hands had closed at her sides. “You know perfectly well. You had your hand on the sill. Ask me.”

He could not do it.

He opened his mouth, and there wasn’t anything there. His throat had shut. The cloak had started to shake, and he looked at that, in a distant sort of way; it was going up and down in his hands like something in a wind. He had asked once. He had asked once, at fourteen, as you’d put your weight on a stair, and he’d heard the answer, and he had been hearing it since. He’d heard it on a swing, in Surrey. He had heard it in every room of Grimmauld Place. *It was always going to be*— He couldn’t. She didn’t know what she was asking. Nobody could ask that twice. He shook his head.

“Ask me, Potter.”

“I—” It broke, right in the middle, like a thing he’d stepped on. He tried again. He had to get it out in pieces, on no air at all, and it was hardly a sound. “Them,” said Harry. “Them or me.”

“You,” said Lyra Malfoy.

The fire settled.

He stood there.

He heard it. He had definitely heard it. It seemed to have gone in somewhere, a long way, and it was still going. It had not hit anything yet. There were the books on the shelf and a strip of dark blue sky. He looked at her mouth. He thought it might do something else.

“It was always going to be you,” she said. Her voice was not at all steady, and she was glaring at him as if he had stolen something. “You knew that before I did.”

“I didn’t,” said Harry.

“No,” said Malfoy. “You didn’t. You’re an idiot.”

She took one step, and took hold of the cloak, and pulled it down out of his hands. It went on to the floor. There was nothing now between them but about a foot of bald rug, and she stood looking up at him, with her eyes very bright. She had said his name before. She had said it in a bad temper, on a certain February night, because of a rule of his; she had shouted it across a lawn, with a rat getting away. She said it to Weasleys, in quotation marks. It had been shouted at him across the whole of two lives, and put on a goblet, and on badges.

She said it very low. She said it as if she had found it in the bottom of a drawer, and hadn’t known that it was hers.

“Harry.”

“Yours,” said Lyra. “Don’t make me say it twice.”

His legs went.

It wasn’t a figure of speech. They just stopped; he sat down on the floor, on the cloak, with a bump, and looked up at her with his mouth open.

“What?” said Harry.

“I said I wouldn’t.”

“What did you say? Say it again.”

“I shall do nothing of the kind.”

“You said—you said *yours*. You did, I heard you. You said *Harry*. Say it. Say it again. *Please*.”

“Yours,” said Lyra, with her nose in the air and her mouth starting to go. “That’s twice. I warned you.”

He made a noise. He had no idea what noise it was; he thought it might be the one that Fang made when you came home. He was up again. He didn’t know how he’d got up. He had her by both hands. They were freezing cold, and she was laughing, or crying, or something, her whole face had come apart, and he said, “Again,” and she said, “Yours, you imbecile, that’s three, I’ve never been so embarrassed,” and he kissed her. He kissed her on the forehead. He got the left eyebrow and the right eyebrow and the end of her nose, and her chin, and both sets of knuckles, one after another, with a loud noise, like somebody stamping a great many forms in a hurry.

“Stop it! You’re *dripping!* Potter—you’ve got your glasses in my eye—that’s a hundred points—”

“Harry.”

“That’s a hundred points, *Harry*, you lunatic, get off—”

“Again!”

“*Yours!*” she shouted. “Yours, yours, oh, for heaven’s sake, is that what you want? *Yours!* Do you want it in Latin?”

“Yes!”

“*Tua!*” she shouted. “*Tua*, you illiterate, it agrees with *me!*”

He let go of her, because he had to, because there was too much of it to stand still with. He ran. He ran round the armchair, with his arms out, in his socks, on the eve of his O.W.L.s, making a long thin noise through his teeth. He hit the bookcase. “She said me!” he told the bookcase. “She said *me!*” He had lost a sock somewhere. He came back to the middle of the rug, heaving for breath, and there she was, with her pins coming out and her hand over her mouth, and that was no help at all.

“Never own a Malfoy,” said Harry, when he could speak. “You said. You stood there. *Never own a Malfoy, Potter*. You can’t—”

“You can’t,” said Lyra. She took her hand down, and she drew herself up to her entire height, which was not much, and looked at him through wet lashes with immense severity. “I haven’t changed my position in the least. Nobody owns me. Nobody could afford it. I am *giving* it to you. There is a difference, and I shall explain it to you when you’re sane.”

He laughed until he got hiccups.

She stopped laughing first. He saw it go; she stood with her breath still catching, and she turned her head, and looked at the fire, where there wasn’t anything left to see. She looked at it for a moment or two.

“It’s the worst thing I have ever done,” she said.

He knew what she meant. He straightened up.

“I know,” said Harry.

She looked round at him. Her chin came up. “Don’t you *dare* be sorry.”

“I’m not,” said Harry.

And he wasn’t. He looked for it, to be fair, like a man going through his coat, and there wasn’t a scrap of it anywhere. He grinned at her, with his swollen nose and his one sock; and very slowly, in spite of everything she could do, the corner of her mouth went up to meet it.

The clock on the shelf struck six. It had a small, silly, tinny strike. They both turned their heads. On the rug, a little way from the fender, a plain horn button gave a shiver and went blue from the edges in, the blue of a gas ring, and it stayed like that for about as long as it took to say so. Then it was a button.

Lyra walked over. She looked down at it. She nudged it with the toe of her shoe, and it rolled a little way, and fell over.

“Well,” she said, “that’s dinner.”

He thought he would probably die. He thought it would be a good way to go.

“I want it in writing,” said Harry.

“You want *what?*”

“One writes it down. On one’s own—how’s it go? You told me. I’ve been in that book since I was fourteen. I want it in the book.”

She gave him a long look. Then she went over to her bag, and got out the small flat book in dark green boards, and slid the pencil from its loop. She sat down in the chair to do it, in the exact middle, and arranged her robes. He came and hung over the arm. It fell open at the last page by itself; it had been opened there a good deal. There was his own terrible handwriting, at fourteen, going uphill. *Everything, repayable on demand. H. Potter.* She licked the point of the pencil. Her hand was not altogether steady, but she had been very well taught, and she wrote a line beneath it in her small clear upright script, and dated it, and turned the book round on the arm of the chair and held it up for his inspection.

*Yours. L. Malfoy. Not repayable.*

He read it. He read it about eleven times.

“You’ve spelt it wrong,” said Harry.

“I have *not* spelt it—”

She looked, and he got her round the shoulders; and that was as much as the chair could stand. It had taken a great deal from them, one way and another, since January. It gave a long despairing sigh of horsehair, and a crack from somewhere underneath, and one leg folded up, and the whole thing tipped slowly and gracefully over on its side and put them out on to the rug, in a heap, with the book, the cards and a cushion. She landed on his elbow. He got a mouthful of cloak. He heard her start, somewhere near his ear, a thin helpless wheezing sound, and that set him off, and for a while there was nothing in the world that could be done about either of them.

It was a long time later. The strip of sky had gone a dark clear green, with one star in it, and somebody had lit the lamp, or it had lit itself. There was a smell of toast. The room had found them a plate of it from somewhere, and a jar of marmalade. He had eaten seven pieces. He couldn’t remember when he’d last been hungry.

He was lying on his back on the rug with a cushion under his head and the grey cloak over him. Lyra was sitting beside him against the wreck of the chair, with her knees drawn up, and she had got out the revision cards.

“Banishing Charms,” she said. “Five exceptions.”

“Yours.”

“That’s not one of them.”

“Say it again.”

“You have Charms in twelve hours, and you’re going to stand in front of an examiner with your mouth open. I’ve seen you. Five exceptions.”

“Lyra,” said Harry.

He had said it before, in a number of emergencies. He had never said it for no reason. It was very like the toast.

She didn’t answer. She looked at him over the top of the card, and whatever she saw, she didn’t take any points for it. She reached over, and took off his glasses, for the second time that day, and folded them, and put them on the fender.

Everything went soft and golden and rather far away. He could hear the fire, and the small sound of a card being laid on a pile. The floor seemed a long way off. There wasn’t anything he needed to check. He noticed that with mild surprise. He felt for the list, from habit, as you feel for your keys, and he couldn’t be bothered. He didn’t count the days. He didn’t go in and look for anybody. Nobody was on the stairs.

When he was seven there had been a game at school, at break, in the playground, where two big boys stood facing everyone and took it in turns. *You. You. You.* Dudley had always been one of the big boys. It went on until there was nobody left standing on the tarmac but you, with your taped glasses and your enormous jumper, looking at your shoes, and whichever side had lost the toss said, *Oh, all right, then*. Every day. For years. He hadn’t thought about that since he didn’t know when.

He started to laugh. It didn’t come to much; it hardly stirred the cloak.

“What?” said Lyra.

“Nothing,” said Harry. “First.”

“You’re asleep.”

“Mm.”

He was. He went to sleep smiling, with marmalade on his chin. He did not hear her go down to her rounds. He slept for eleven hours, and he didn’t dream of anything at all.
