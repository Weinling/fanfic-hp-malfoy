# Chapter 31: Whether I Do

“Lyra,” said Panther Parkinson, “I was wondering.”

He had cleared his throat first, which was how she had known it was coming, and he said it to the back of her head, loudly enough for the notice board and most of the common room. Lyra finished reading the last line about dress robes, for the third time, and turned round.

He had combed his hair. She could not think when he had found the time.

“The Ball,” said Parkinson. “Christmas. I thought, since we’re both— since everybody knows we’d look—” He gave it up and went straight at it, like a Beater at a Bludger. “Will you go with me?”

The common room had gone quiet in the particular way of a room that has stopped talking in order to listen better. Somebody by the fire put down a chess piece without moving it.

“Panther,” said Lyra, “that is very kind of you.”

He brightened.

“And I wouldn’t dream of it,” said Lyra, with exactly the same warmth, “because you would spend the whole evening watching me for my reaction to things, and you deserve a girl who will let you look at the decorations.” She smiled at him, the smile her mother kept for the wives of junior ministers. “Ask Daphne. She’s been wanting you to for a week.”

Daphne Greengrass, on the sofa, went the colour of a radish and hit the girl next to her for laughing. Parkinson looked at Daphne, and then at Lyra, and then at Daphne again, like a man who has been handed his own coat and is not sure he was leaving; and the common room, which had been holding its breath, let it out all at once and began to guess.

It guessed all evening. It guessed Montague, who was in sixth year and had a moustache, and Warrington, who had asked three people already; it guessed a Durmstrang seventh-year with a scar through one eyebrow, and somebody at the back said *Diggory* and was told not to be disgusting. Lyra let it guess. She sat in her chair by the fire with her Arithmancy open on her knee and did not turn a page, and every time a new name went round she looked mildly amused, which was the only expression that did not confirm anything.

“I’ve got one already,” said Goyle, beside her, to nobody in particular.

“*Who?*” said three people at once.

“Shan’t say.” Goyle folded her arms. “You’d tell everybody.”

“We would *not*—”

“You told everybody about my aunt’s goitre.”

That was unanswerable, and they left her alone. Goyle went pink with satisfaction and ate a Chocolate Frog.

Crabbe said nothing at all.

She sat on Lyra’s other side, sewing a button back on her cardigan with her tongue between her teeth, and she did not ask whom Lyra was going with. Lyra waited for it. It was the first thing Crabbe would want to know, ahead of Goyle’s secret and well ahead of the button, and Lyra had her answer ready, the light one, the one that committed her to nothing; and Crabbe bit off the thread, and held the cardigan up to look at it, and still did not ask.

Lyra turned a page she had not read.

Crabbe, she thought, had stopped asking her things she already knew the answer to. She could not remember when. That was the trouble with Crabbe. You never saw her do anything; you only looked up and found it done.

Professor Dumbledore’s chair at the staff table had been empty since the night of the dragons. Nobody had said why. Professor McGonagall, asked at breakfast on the Friday by a Hufflepuff prefect who was braver than he looked, had said “The Headmaster has been called away,” in a voice that closed the subject and locked it; and Potter, who had gone into the hospital wing on the Thursday night with a hole in his shoulder, had come out of it on the Friday morning without one, and was eating toast at the Gryffindor table as though dragons were something that happened to other people.

So on Tuesday morning, when Goyle came down late with the *Daily Prophet* folded open in both hands and a face like a burst pipe, there was no Headmaster to look up from the staff table and see it.

“Rita Skeeter,” said Goyle. “*Again.*”

Lyra held out her hand for it. She had found, the last time, that it was better to read these things oneself than to have them read aloud by Goyle, who did the voices.

**HARRY POTTER: A TRIANGLE?**

*It is barely a week since this paper brought its readers the touching story of Harry Potter and his devoted Hermione Granger, and already this correspondent can reveal that the Boy Who Lived has a secret even closer to his heart.*

*Before the First Task, while his fellow champions waited in grim silence, young Harry was overheard whispering sweet nothings to an unseen companion by means of a long-range Listening Charm. They were apart in body, it seems, but not in spirit. The companion was identified to this correspondent, by a fellow student, as none other than the Princess of Slytherin herself: Miss Lyra Malfoy, daughter of Lucius Malfoy, whose family needs no introduction.*

*“Potter and Malfoy?” laughs Panther Parkinson, fourteen, of Slytherin House. “They’ve loathed each other since first year. Ask anybody.”*

*But this correspondent has asked, and the answer is rather different. Like Romeo and Juliet before them, these two young lovers have hidden behind the ancient hatred of their Houses. And one cannot help but wonder whether poor Miss Granger was ever more than a plaything, a pretty decoy for the eyes of the world . . .* (continued on page seven)

Lyra read it once. Then she read the paragraph with Parkinson in it again, because it had her own words in it, in his mouth, in print, and she wanted to be sure.

She looked down the table. Parkinson had seen her face and was finding something of enormous interest in his porridge.

“She asked me who *Lyra* was,” he said to the porridge. “Last week. Outside the library. I didn’t know she was going to *print* it.”

Lyra took out her wand, laid the tip of it against the corner of the *Prophet*, and said, “*Incendio.*”

It went up in a single soft rush, like the last of her father’s letters, and left nothing behind on the table but a faint smell of hot ink and a ring of silence that went most of the way down the Slytherin bench.

“Gossip,” said Lyra. “I shan’t trouble to deny it. It doesn’t deserve it.”

“No,” said Goyle, a little too quickly. “No. Course not.”

“A *triangle*.” Lyra buttered a piece of toast. “With Potter. And a Listening Charm. If I wanted to whisper sweet nothings to anybody, which I do not, I should hardly do it by charm, through a tent, like a scullery maid with a two-way mirror.”

The tone was the thing, and the tone was right, and she could feel the bench begin, cautiously, to believe her, or at least to see that it was wiser to look as if it did.

Parkinson cleared his throat.

“So it isn’t true,” he said. “Any of it. So you haven’t got— so you’re not going with—”

Crabbe and Goyle turned and looked at him with deep and identical pity.

She did not weigh it. It came out because the whole table was waiting for it, and because there was only one answer she could give that nobody in Slytherin would dream of doubting.

“I’m going with Viktor Krum,” she said.

The bench went still.

She knew exactly whom Viktor Krum looked at over the top of his library books, and it was not her. She said it anyway. Krum ate at their table every day of the week. Nobody here had ever seen him so much as look at a girl from another House, and nobody would think to ask him.

“*Krum?*” said Goyle.

“Naturally. My father and Professor Karkaroff are old friends.” Lyra bit into her toast. “It’s a little obvious, I grant you. But it would be absurd to go with anybody less than a champion.”

Parkinson put down his spoon and went away towards the end of the table with the air of a man who had expected nothing better and had got it.

Crabbe had not said a word. She was looking at Lyra with her head slightly on one side, as she looked at a Transfiguration problem that would not come out, and it went on for so long that Lyra was obliged to raise her eyebrows at her.

“What about Harry Potter?” said Crabbe at last, very carefully. “He’s a champion too.”

“Vanessa,” said Lyra, “you don’t believe the *Prophet*, surely.”

“No,” said Crabbe slowly. “No. I’ll tell people that.”

She had a free period after Herbology and spent it on the shore path, walking fast, with the Durmstrang ship lying out on the grey water like something that had drowned and been too proud to stay down.

A Listening Charm. It was a stupid lie, and it was a very particular kind of stupid lie: the kind you told when you had heard something and could not explain how. Nobody had seen her in that tent. She had been under the cloak from the moment she went down the slope to the moment she came running back up it, and the only people in the tent had been Krum, who had looked at the floor, and Delacour, who had looked at nothing, and Potter, who could not have seen her if he had tried. *It’s a dragon, Lyra.* He had said her name twice. Anybody listening would have heard a boy on a bench talking to the air, and a girl’s voice answering out of nowhere.

So somebody had been listening, and had not been seen either.

Lyra stopped on the path.

There had been a beetle. A fat, glossy, bluish thing, blundering about against the canvas near the roof, the whole time, and nobody had minded it. And on her way out, running, something had gone droning past her ear, close, and she had not stopped for it.

A beetle was a stupid idea, and she turned it over carefully because of that. Sirius Black had spent a year as a dog, eating chocolate out of her hand, and not a soul in the Ministry had known he could do it. Peter Pettigrew had spent twelve years as a rat on a Gryffindor’s pillow. Neither of them had been in any register. If two of them could do it, so could a third; and if the third was a woman who made her living by being in rooms she had not been invited to—

She was nearly sure. She did not know how she would ever prove it, and she did not need to prove it. She only needed to know it, and to know that nobody else did, and she stood there on the wet path and felt it settle into her like a coin into a purse.

She did not think she would tell anybody. Not Crabbe. Not Potter, either; not yet. Potter had told her a great many things since the summer, and every one of them had arrived a little late, after he had already decided what to do with it. She could be late too.

She walked on.

It was not the article she minded, not really. She minded the Ball.

She had known for a month what she would look like at it. She had not told herself so in as many words, but she had known: which way her hair would go, and that her mother would choose the robes, and that she would come down the marble staircase a little after everybody else, so that the Hall had time to turn round. That part was old. She had always meant to be the best-looking girl in any room she walked into, and she generally was.

The new part was smaller, and worse, and she had not known it was there until this morning, when Rita Skeeter took it away. She had wanted him to see her first. Before the Hall, before anybody. She had wanted to watch his face do whatever it was going to do, and then to take his arm as if she had not noticed.

There was no chance of that now. The *Prophet* had seen to it.

The wind came off the lake and found the back of her neck, and Lyra put her collar up and went back to the castle for Charms.

The ring began in Charms.

It was in her pocket, where it lived now, and halfway through Cheering Charms it started up against her fingers: warm, and then cool, and then warm again. The other one was looking for her. Twice in October she had felt it do that and gone the other way.

This time she went. When the bell rang she let Crabbe and Goyle go on down to lunch, and turned on the stairs with her hand in her pocket until the ring grew warm towards the first floor, and followed it to the classroom with the bad window, where a door that had been shut that morning stood an inch open.

Potter was sitting on a desk with his feet on a chair. He did not get up.

“We can’t go together,” he said. “Not now. Not after—”

“I know.” She shut the door behind her. “I read it. So did every Durmstrang boy at our table, out loud, and two of them did the voices.”

She had been carrying his cloak in her bag for five days. She took it out, and shook it once, and dropped the whole cold slippery armful of it into his lap, as he had dropped it into hers outside the Great Hall.

Potter looked down at it. He did not say *thanks*. He looked tired, the way he had looked on the train with the frost on the window, and she disliked that and did not let it show.

“It was in the tent,” he said. “Somebody was. I didn’t see anybody.”

“No,” said Lyra, and kept the beetle to herself.

“It’s not just the Ball, Lyra. If people know—”

“If people know, it’s dangerous, and not for you. For all of us.” She had heard this speech under a cloister in May, with a ring in it. “You needn’t do it again. I remember it.”

He shut his mouth. For a moment he looked as if he had a great deal more of it ready and nowhere to put it.

“I could get away,” he said. “Halfway through. Meet you somewhere. Nobody would know, and we could—”

“Dance in a broom cupboard?”

“Well. Not a cupboard.”

“Potter,” said Lyra, “do I look like something that waits under the table for whatever is left on somebody else’s plate?”

He looked at her. Whatever he had been about to say, he did not say it.

“I thought not.” She picked up her bag. “Well. I shan’t keep you. I have a great deal to do before Christmas, as it happens. I have dress robes to try on, if my mother ever sends them, and a scent to find, because the one I have is for daytime, and I shall have to do something about my hair that takes rather longer than this.” She settled the strap on her shoulder. “So if you don’t mind.”

She was at the door with her hand on it when he said, “Wait.”

She did not turn round. She waited, which was not the same thing.

She had said it quite lightly, like a list of errands. She heard it now from the inside of his silence, the way he must have heard it: the robes, and the scent, and the hair. A month of it. She had not meant him to hear any of that, or she had, and she found she did not know which, and that she was not going to look at him until she did.

“Lyra,” said Potter. “Do you trust me?”

That made her turn.

“Why on earth would I?”

He laughed. It was not his loud laugh; it came out of him short and surprised, as if she had trodden on his foot and he had found he did not mind.

“Thanks,” he said. “Honestly. Look. I’ll sort something out. I don’t know what yet, but I will. Only go with Krum.” He held up a hand before she could speak. “Or look as if you are. Let everybody think you are. Can you do that?”

“I already have.”

“What?”

“At breakfast. In front of the whole of Slytherin.” She watched it reach him. “Parkinson asked again, and the entire bench was waiting, and Krum was the only name nobody would dare laugh at. So they think I’m going with Krum. They have for four hours.”

Potter stared at her. Then he put his face in his hands, on top of the cloak, and laughed properly, the real one, so that his shoulders went.

“You did it before I asked,” he said, through his fingers.

“Most things worth doing, I do before you ask.”

He took his hands away. He was still grinning, and it was the first time since the dragon that she had seen it, and she would not have admitted for anything that she had been waiting to.

“Then make it true,” he said. “Or make it look true.”

Lyra opened the door.

“Whether I do,” she said, “is my decision, Potter.”

She went out, and shut it behind her, and did not hurry on the stairs.

The robes came on the second Saturday in December, by the Manor’s grey owl, in a long flat box tied with silver ribbon.

Lyra opened it on her bed with the hangings drawn. Inside, in tissue paper, was a fall of dark violet silk, the colour of the sugared violets her mother had bought her since she was small, cut close and plain and very expensive, with nothing on it anywhere that a person could point to and call decoration. It was perfect. Her mother did not make mistakes about clothes.

On top of the tissue lay a card. It was not in the hand her mother used for invitations, with the loops. It was the other one, small and upright, the one Lyra had seen exactly once before, at the bottom of a letter about Regulus Black.

*I chose the colour. You will tell me, I hope, whom it is for.*

Lyra read it twice. Then she put it inside the cover of her Arithmancy book, behind the tables of values, next to a strip of paper in the same hand that said *You may tell me anything*, and closed the book on both of them.

She did not write back. She did not know what she would say.

There was nothing from her father. That was the other thing, and she had been not thinking about it for eleven days. The *Prophet* went to the Manor every morning with his breakfast, and her father had answered every letter she had ever sent him within two days, usually within one, and generally at greater length than she wanted. She had not written to him since the ship. He had not written to her since the *Prophet*. It sat in the box beside the violet silk like a second present, and it was much heavier.

On Sunday she went down to the ship.

She did it because Potter had asked her to, and she had told him it was her decision, and it was: she had decided. That did not make the gangplank any shorter. A girl did not ask a boy to a Ball. A girl let it be known, in the right quarter, that she would not be displeased to be asked, and then she waited; and the right quarter, in this case, was a man with yellow teeth who called her father his friend.

Professor Karkaroff was delighted. He was so delighted that he did not seem to notice she had come to him, rather than Krum to her, and Lyra let him not notice. He poured her something hot that she did not drink. He told her that Viktor was out on the water with the others, that Viktor was a serious boy and would be honoured, that he would speak to Viktor himself that very evening; he answered for Viktor so fluently, and at such length, that by the end of it Lyra was not sure Viktor would be required to say anything at all.

Then he stopped in the middle of a sentence about dress robes, and rubbed his left forearm through his sleeve, slowly, as a man rubs a place where an old injury has started to ache in cold weather.

“Your father,” he said. “He is well? He has said nothing to you, I suppose. About the Mark.”

Lyra thought of a green skull over a field of tents, and a serpent sliding out between its teeth.

“Nothing at all, sir,” she said. “He says it was somebody who got carried away.”

“Yes.” Karkaroff’s hand stayed where it was. “Yes, of course. Carried away.”

She went back down the gangplank in the early dark with her collar up. The lamps were lit in the windows of the castle, a long way up the slope, and halfway there, on the path, there was a staff, and a shape leaning on it, and an eye.

She had met him here once before. She did not stop, this time. She walked straight on, and he let her get level with him before he spoke, and he did not ask after Igor.

“Krum, is it?” said Moody.

Then he went stumping off along the bank into the dark, *clunk*, *clunk*, and left her standing on the path.

She had been alone in that cabin with Karkaroff. There had been nobody else on that ship within three walls of them.
