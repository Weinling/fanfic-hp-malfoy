# Chapter 28: Credit

Potter had given her wand back under the cloak, handle first, with a small, solemn bow from the waist, as if they were about to duel. She had taken it without a word. There had not been room under the cloak to hit him again.

“Where were you at lunch?” Crabbe had asked, that evening.

“Detention.”

Crabbe had looked at her for a moment. Then she had reached over and taken a beech leaf out of Lyra’s hair, and put it on the table between them, and gone back to her homework.

That had been Thursday. On Friday afternoon the fourth-year Slytherins and the fourth-year Gryffindors went down across the frozen lawn to Hagrid’s hut, as they did every week, and stood along the paddock fence in two separate lines with their breath smoking, and looked at the crates.

There were about a hundred of them. They were open at the top, and from inside them came a strange rattling noise, like very small explosions, and a smell like rotten fish.

“Blast-Ended Skrewts!” said Hagrid, beaming over the top of the nearest crate. “Newly hatched. Thought we’d do a project on ’em. Yeh’ll be raisin’ ’em, see what they turn into.”

Lyra looked into the crate.

The skrewts looked like deformed, shell-less lobsters, horribly pale and slimy, with legs sticking out in very odd places and no visible heads. Some of them had stings. Some of them had little sucking things on their bellies, which she did not care to think about. Every so often one of them gave off a spurt of sparks from its back end with a small *phut*, and shot forward several inches, and landed on another one.

“And what,” said Lyra, “are they *for*?”

“For?” Hagrid looked down at her over the crates, puzzled, as though she had asked what a sunset was for.

“What do they *do*, Professor? What is the point of them? I only ask because I should like to know what I’m going to be risking my fingers on.”

“That’s next lesson,” said Hagrid, a little huffily. “Today yer jus’ feedin’ ’em. Now, I’m not sure what they eat, so I’ve got a few diff’rent things—ant eggs, frog livers, bit o’ grass snake. Try ’em with a bit of each.”

He did not know what they ate. He had a hundred of them in boxes and he did not know what they ate. Lyra exchanged a long look with the nearest crate, and pulled on her dragon-hide gloves.

Behind the hut, in the paddock where the pumpkins had been, a large grey shape lifted its head.

Buckbeak was tethered where he had been since last year, on a long rope to a stake in the frozen earth, and he had seen her. He looked at her down the length of his beak with one fierce orange eye. Then, slowly, with great dignity, he bent his scaly knees and bowed.

Lyra glanced along the fence. The Slytherins were peering into crates, and the Gryffindors were peering into theirs, and Hagrid was bent double over a box of frog livers. She inclined her head to the hippogriff, very slightly, as her mother inclined her head to people at the opera whom she did not intend to speak to but had no wish to offend. Buckbeak seemed to find it satisfactory. He went back to the pumpkin he had been eating.

She owed him a steak. She had told him so in February, with a werewolf behind them. She did not like to think how long that had been on the books.

“Ugh! It bit me—the filthy thing *bit* me!” Parkinson was shaking his hand at the next crate along. Nothing had bitten him; he had his gloves on. “We ought to kill the lot of them, if you ask me. Before they kill us. Stamp on them. It’d be a kindness.”

“Ah, don’ say that,” said Hagrid, wounded. “They’re only babies.”

“Hang on,” said Potter.

He had come round the end of the fence from the Gryffindor side, pulling on a pair of thick gloves, and before anybody could tell him not to, he reached into Parkinson’s crate and lifted one of the skrewts out by the middle. It was about six inches long and it did not like it. Its legs went in every direction at once. Potter turned it over, so that its pale soft belly, with all its sucking things, was pointing up at Parkinson’s face.

“You just haven’t given it a chance,” he said earnestly. “It’s quite sweet, if you look at it long enough.” He held it a little closer. “*Boo*.”

The skrewt went *phut*.

Parkinson went over backwards into the frozen mud, with a noise like a wardrobe falling downstairs. Somebody on the Gryffindor side laughed out loud. Goyle, who had been standing next to him, made a small sound and ran into Hagrid’s hut and shut the door.

Then Potter turned round, with the skrewt still wriggling in his hands, and came towards Lyra.

He did it in full view of both Houses. He was grinning, and there was nothing in it but the old war, the one the whole school had been watching for three years: Potter and Malfoy, at it again. Nobody looking on could have seen anything else. Lyra knew that perfectly well. She also knew that he was enjoying himself far too much.

“Malfoy. Want to hold him?”

“Come one step closer,” said Lyra, “and I’ll—”

He came one step closer.

There was a broken branch on the ground by her foot. She picked it up and threw it, overarm, and it hit the skrewt exactly where she had aimed: on the end of its tail.

The tail exploded.

There was a bang and a shower of sparks and a smell of burnt fish, and Potter yelped and dropped the skrewt, which shot away across the frozen mud on a little jet of flame and buried itself in a pumpkin. Potter stood looking at the front of his robes. They were smoking slightly.

“Ah,” said Hagrid. “Yeah. Forgot ter mention. The tails explode.”

Lyra brushed her gloves together and went back to her crate.

When she looked up again, Granger was watching her across the paddock. Granger had a frog liver in one hand and an expression on her face that Lyra did not care for at all: the expression of somebody who had recently seen a ferret, and done some arithmetic, and was trying very hard not to smile. Lyra looked back at her coldly until she went pink and turned away.

At the far end of the Gryffindor line, Weasley was feeding his skrewts on his own. He had not laughed at Parkinson. He had not looked up when the tail went off. Potter went back to the Gryffindor side with his robes still smoking, and stood next to Granger, and was cheerful for the rest of the lesson in a loud, careless way that did not fool Lyra for a moment.
