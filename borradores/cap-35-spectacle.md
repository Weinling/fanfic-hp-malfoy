# Chapter 35: Spectacle

“It screams,” said Potter. “That’s all it does. I’ve had it open in the dormitory, I’ve had it open in the common room, I’ve had it open in an empty classroom with the door shut, and it screams. Seamus says if I open it again he’s going to drop it out of the window.”

“Then don’t open it in the dormitory.”

“Thanks. Very helpful.”

They were in the library, on the Saturday afternoon, a week before the second task, at a table at the back where the shelves of Magical Law came down to meet them and nobody ever came. The golden egg sat on the table between them, shut, shining softly, like an ornament off a very expensive mantelpiece. He had brought it with him under his arm, everywhere, for a fortnight. It had begun to look, Lyra thought, rather like a pet.

“You haven’t worked it out,” she said.

“No.”

She had heard it once. Everybody in the castle had heard it once, the night after the dragon, when he opened it in the middle of the Gryffindor celebrations and the noise came all the way down the Great Hall: a dreadful, sawing, shrieking wail, like a dozen kettles being murdered. She had sat at the Slytherin table with her hands over her ears like everybody else and thought, quite clearly, *I have heard something like that before*; and it had taken her a week of going through the back shelves in her free periods to remember where.

She had not heard it. She had read it.

She took a book out of her bag and opened it at the place she had marked, and turned it round, and pushed it across the table to him with one finger, as Granger had pushed her the Horntails.

*The language of the merpeople is beautiful and musical below the surface, but to the ear above water it is almost wholly unpleasant, and has been compared by more than one unfortunate listener to a shriek.*

Potter read it. He read it again. He did not ask her where she had found it, or how, or whether she was sure; he shut the book carefully, with his hand flat on the cover, and sat looking at the hand.

“There are merpeople in the lake,” said Lyra, in case he had not got there.

“I know,” said Potter. He looked up, and he was grinning. “Lyra. That’s brilliant.”

“I know,” said Lyra. “Get your cloak.”

The lake was grey and still, and frozen at the edges in thin plates that cracked if you looked at them. Nobody was down there but the Durmstrang ship, shut up and silent on the water, with smoke going up from one funnel. They went round past the end of the shingle to the rocks below the beech trees, where the Durmstrang boys had dived in November, shouting about the cold; and Potter lay down on his front on the flattest rock, with the egg in both hands, and looked over the edge into the water.

“If I fall in,” he said, “I want it known that this was your idea.”

“You won’t fall in.”

She knelt down beside him on the rock, which was so cold it went straight through her robes, and took hold of the back of his collar with both hands. She took hold of it properly, his robes and the shirt underneath and a good deal of the jumper, and wound it round her fist, and braced her knees.

“Go on.”

He took a breath, and put his head and both arms under the water, and opened the egg.

The shriek did not come. There was nothing but the lake round his head, and the cold, and his shoulders gone rigid under her hands with it; and then, very faintly, from somewhere under the surface, so faint she might have imagined it, she thought she heard something that was not a shriek at all. It went on for some time. She did not let go of his collar. She knelt on the frozen rock with the wind off the lake in her face and both fists wound into his robes, and held on, and counted, and when she got to thirty and he had not moved she pulled.

He came up gasping, with the egg shut in his hands and water pouring off his hair and his glasses gone sideways.

“Well?”

“Hang on—” He coughed, and shook his head like a dog, which she did not appreciate, since most of it went on her. “There’s a song. Singing. Under the water, it’s singing.” He shut his eyes, to get it right. “*Come seek us where our voices sound.* Something about the surface— we can’t sing above it. *An hour long you’ll have to look, to recover what we took.*”

“What you’ll sorely miss,” said Lyra.

He opened his eyes.

“It was the next line,” said Lyra. “I heard it. Just. Through the water.” She sat back on her heels, and let go of his collar at last, and found that her fingers had gone white. “The lake, then. An hour. And they’re going to take something of yours.”

*One of you dies*, said Moody, somewhere at the back of her head, *you or Potter*. She did not say it. She had not said any of it, in four weeks; and she sat on the frozen rock with the words in her mouth and let them stay there, and it was like holding a coal.

Potter rolled over on to his back on the rock and lay there with the egg on his chest and the water running out of his hair into his ears, looking up at the sky.

“I can’t swim, you know,” he said.

He said it to the sky, not to her, in an ordinary voice.

“Don’t be absurd. Everybody can swim.”

“I can’t. Never learned.” He was quiet a moment. “My aunt and uncle used to go to the seaside. Every summer, for a week. My cousin had lessons, at the pool, Saturdays. I didn’t go.”

“Where did you go?”

“Nowhere. I stayed in.” He turned his head on the rock, and looked at the frozen edge of the lake. “There’s a cupboard under the stairs. At theirs. That’s where I slept, till I was eleven. They’d lock it when they went to the seaside, so I wouldn’t touch anything.”

She had known that he had nobody at Christmas. She had known that his letters in the summer came by birds she had never seen before, and that he signed them with an ugly name, and that he never went home at Easter. She had not known this. She sat on her heels on the frozen rock and looked at him lying there, the most famous boy in Britain, with lake-water in his ears, telling the sky about a cupboard; and her hands had shut into fists on her knees.

“I shall curse them,” said Lyra. “Both of them. And the cousin.”

“Don’t.”

“I shall. I know some very good ones. My father taught me one for boils that he says the Ministry has never been able to prove—”

“Don’t,” said Potter again, and sat up. He was not smiling. “He’s still my uncle.”

She stared at him.

He looked back at her steadily, with his hair in his eyes and the egg in his lap. Whatever it was, he had decided it a long time ago, and he was not going to explain it to her, because it was his.

Lyra unwound her scarf from her neck.

It was the cashmere one, grey, the one his owl had once tried to eat in the Owlery, which had cost a great deal more than anything he was wearing. She pulled it off, and put it over his head, and dried his hair with it, hard, rather harder than was necessary, as if his hair had personally offended her. He let her. He sat on the rock with his eyes shut and his head bowed and let her do it, and when she had finished, and his hair was standing up in every direction like a hedgehog’s, she wound the scarf twice round his neck, and tied it, and left it there.

“You’ll catch your death,” said Lyra, “and then where will I be.”

Professor McGonagall came for her on the Thursday night, at a quarter past eight, which was the first time in four years that any Head of House but Professor Snape had set foot in the Slytherin common room.

The room went silent. McGonagall stood just inside the door in her tartan dressing gown, with her hair in its net, and looked round it as though she were counting the exits, and said, “Miss Malfoy. A word, if you please,” and went back out into the corridor without waiting to see whether Lyra would come.

Lyra came.

“The Headmaster has asked me,” said McGonagall, when the stone wall had slid shut behind them, “to ask you a favour. It concerns tomorrow’s task. Each of the champions will be required to recover something from the lake. Something that will be taken from them tonight.” Her lips thinned. “It has been decided that it should be a person. You would be put into an enchanted sleep. You would be perfectly safe; the Headmaster has seen to that himself. You would wake when you were brought out of the water. Mr Potter would be required to—”

“No,” said Lyra.

McGonagall stopped.

“Thank you, Professor,” said Lyra. “It’s very kind of the Headmaster to think of me. But no.”

She had known what it was before McGonagall reached the end of the second sentence. *To recover what we took. What you’ll sorely miss.* She had knelt on a rock in the wind and heard it with her own ears, through the water.

She was not going to do it.

She was not going to spend an hour asleep at the bottom of a lake, tied to something, with weed in her hair and fish looking at her, so that Harry Potter could swim down and fetch her up again in front of three schools and the *Daily Prophet*, like a cup somebody had dropped in a well. She was Lyra Malfoy. She did not get fetched.

That was the reason she gave herself, and it was a perfectly good one, and it was true. There was another one under it, and she did not give herself that one, because she did not have to: she could feel it there, the whole time McGonagall was talking, like a stone in a shoe.

*A sweetheart’s the first thing a Dark wizard goes for. Easiest lever there is.*

Somebody in this castle wanted Potter dead. Somebody had put his name in a cup to get him killed, and would very likely be sitting in the stands tomorrow morning with everybody else, watching the water. And Dumbledore wanted to put her at the bottom of the lake, asleep, tied by the ankle, for that person to look at for an hour, and for Potter to have to come and get.

No.

“I see,” said Professor McGonagall.

She looked at Lyra for a moment longer than she needed to, over the top of her square spectacles, with an expression that Lyra could not read at all; and then, quite suddenly, it changed, and for an instant it looked almost like approval.

“Very well, Miss Malfoy. I shall tell the Headmaster.” She drew her dressing gown round her. “I believe Mr Weasley will do very well instead. He is unlikely to have any objection to an hour’s sleep.”

She went away down the dungeon corridor, with her slippers making no sound at all on the stone.

Lyra stood outside the wall for a while, with her arms folded.

Weasley, then. At the bottom of the lake, for Potter. She did not mind that in the least. Examining it more closely, she was glad. Weasley had been his first. It seemed only right that Weasley should be the one he went down for.

The stands had been put up along the far shore of the lake, in the night, and by half past nine on the Friday morning the whole school was packed into them, in scarves and hats and gloves, stamping its feet, with its breath going up in clouds. The water was grey and flat and gave away nothing. Out at the end of a little wooden jetty stood the four champions, and the judges’ table, draped in gold, with Bagman behind it already on his feet.

Lyra sat in the Slytherin stand between Crabbe and Goyle with her hands in her sleeves. Her neck was cold. She had not got the scarf back, and had not asked for it.

“—*one hour* to recover what has been taken from them,” Bagman was booming, his voice magically magnified over the water. “On my whistle, then! One— two—”

On the whistle, Diggory and Delacour and Krum went into the lake.

Potter did not.

He stood at the end of the jetty by himself, and raised his wand, and Lyra heard it come across the water, thin and clear: “*Accio Firebolt!*” A ripple of laughter went along the stands. Somebody in the Hufflepuff stand shouted that it was a lake, Potter, not a pitch. Then the Firebolt came streaking out over the trees from the direction of the castle, and stopped beside him in the air, and he swung his leg over it and shot away, not out over the water but back the way the broom had come, low and very fast, towards the school.

“Where’s he *going*?” said Goyle.

Lyra did not know. She watched the small dark shape go up the slope and across the grounds towards the great grey bulk of the castle, and rise along its face, and level out; and she saw, a moment before it happened, which window he was making for. She had looked out of that window herself, a year ago almost to the week, with the light going and a werewolf’s potion on the desk behind her.

There was a tiny, distant, glittering burst, like frost knocked off a branch.

Then the sound reached them across the water: a far-off crack, and a long tinkling crash, and a great many people in the stands all saying *what* at once.

“That’s—” Somebody along the Slytherin bench had stood up. “That’s the *dungeons*—that’s Snape’s—”

It was. Harry Potter had flown a Firebolt through the window of the Potions master’s private office in the middle of the second task, in front of the whole school and two foreign ones and the *Daily Prophet*, and he was inside it now.

Lyra sat quite still.

She did not know what she felt. It was the office of her own Head of House, the one room in the castle into which no Slytherin had ever gone uninvited and come out unmarked; it held, to her certain knowledge, a jar of something with eyes in it that she had dreamt about in her first year. He had smashed his way into it on a broomstick. It was the most outrageous thing she had ever seen a student do, and she had grown up hearing about her father’s schooldays.

She was, she realised, very nearly proud of him.

He came out again almost at once, through the same jagged hole, with something stuffed in his mouth that trailed out of it like a grey-green moustache, and dived straight down the face of the castle towards the lake. He went over the stands so low that Lyra felt the wind of him, and hit the water broom and all, flat, at a speed that sent a sheet of spray up over the jetty and the judges; and the lake closed over him, and he was gone.

The stands went mad.

At the end of the staff bench, Professor Snape had not moved. He had watched his window go without a flicker, as he watched cauldrons boil over in the dungeons, as though it were what he had always expected and only a little earlier than he had expected it. Now he turned his head, slowly, the whole length of the stands, past the Gryffindors and the Hufflepuffs and the Ravenclaws, until he found the Slytherin bench, and Lyra on it.

He looked at her for a moment, as he had looked at her once by a lake in the dark, with grey feathers on the front of her robes: as if he were adding something to a bill.

Then he looked back at the water.

⁂

After that there was nothing to look at for an hour.

That was the part nobody had thought about, Lyra decided, when they planned the second task: that the whole school would sit on cold benches above a flat grey lake and watch nothing happen to it. The surface closed over the four of them and lay there. Now and then a bubble came up somewhere and burst, and the stands leaned towards it, and it was a bubble. A giant squid put up one lazy tentacle near the far bank and took it down again. Bagman told jokes into his megaphone. Nobody laughed at them.

At twenty minutes, Fleur Delacour came up, alone, without whatever she had gone down for, and was hauled out on to the jetty by Madame Maxime, and stood there in her bathing costume with a towel round her shoulders, shaking and screaming in French about the *grindylows*. Lyra’s French was very good. It was not, on the whole, a speech she would have translated for Goyle.

The surface closed again.

Lyra could not look at it. Every time she looked at it she thought of a boy who could not swim, gone into it on a broomstick with a mouthful of weed from Snape’s cupboard, and of what she had not told him on a rock a week ago, and her hands began to do things in her sleeves that she did not care for. So she stopped looking at the water, and looked at the judges instead, because they were the only other thing there was.

Dumbledore, at the end of the table, with his hands folded on the gold cloth, watching the lake with an expression of mild and perfect patience. Madame Maxime, back in her seat, wrapping her own fur round her shoulders as though it were she who had been in the water. Karkaroff, beside her, leaning forward with his fists on his knees, watching for Krum. Bagman, on his feet, with the megaphone.

And at the other end, a man she had never seen before.

There was a little card in front of him on the gold cloth, the same as all the others, and it said *B. Crouch, Department of International Magical Co-operation*. So that was Barty Crouch. The man who had sent his son to Azkaban. The man whose son had died there.

He was not what she had expected. She had expected somebody hard and grand and frightening, to go with the story Karkaroff had told and the grave Moody had stood at. He was a thin, elderly, very upright man in a suit of dark Muggle cloth so neat it might have been ironed on him, with a moustache clipped like a toothbrush and a parting in his grey hair so straight it looked drawn with a ruler. He might have been going to a wedding at the Ministry. He sat quite upright, with his hands flat on the table in front of him, one on either side of his card.

He did not move.

That was the thing she noticed, after a while, and once she had noticed it she could not stop noticing it. Everybody else at that table moved. Bagman bounced. Karkaroff fidgeted, and muttered, and twice half rose out of his chair when something stirred out on the water. Even Dumbledore turned his head now and then, and once said something to Madame Maxime that made her laugh. Barty Crouch sat with his hands flat on the cloth and looked straight in front of him, at nothing, and did not turn his head towards the lake at all. Not when Fleur came up screaming. Not when the squid surfaced. Not once.

He looked like a man waiting to be told that he might.

Bagman bent down to him, at forty minutes, and said something with a grin and a jerk of his thumb at the water; and Mr Crouch answered him. Lyra could not hear what he said. She saw his mouth move. But she saw, too, that he answered a beat late, a full second after Bagman had stopped speaking, like a man who has had to wait for the question to arrive from a long way off.

She did not know what it meant. She did not think it meant anything, yet.

She looked at him anyway, for the rest of the hour, because it was better than looking at the lake.

⁂

Diggory came up first, at the end of the hour, with a bubble round his head and Cho Chang in his arms, and the Hufflepuffs nearly brought their stand down. Then Krum, or most of Krum: his body was his own, in a pair of black bathing trunks, but his head was the head of a shark, which he appeared to have tried to change back too early, and he came up through the water with Granger under one arm and his great grey snout pointing at the sky. Granger woke up on the surface and saw what was carrying her and screamed, and then saw who it was and stopped, and Lyra, who had been on her feet since the first bubble, sat down again rather suddenly.

The hour went past. Bagman said so, into his megaphone, sounding worried. Nothing came up.

At an hour and four minutes, the lake broke open in front of the jetty and a Firebolt came out of it.

It came out sideways and very low, streaming water, with a great deal more on it than it had been built for. Potter was flat along the handle, and in front of him, slumped across it with his arms round them, were Weasley, blinking and spluttering, and a small girl with silvery hair whom Lyra had never seen in her life. The broom laboured up to the jetty, and dumped all three of them on it in a heap, and lay down in the water beside them like a dog that has fetched a stick much larger than itself.

Fleur Delacour gave a shriek that could have been heard in Beauxbatons, and ran.

Lyra was down out of the stand and on the bank before she had properly thought about it. She did not run. A Malfoy did not run on to a jetty in front of three schools. She walked, very fast, through the crowd of people in towels and people with blankets and Madam Pomfrey shouting about hypothermia, and stopped a yard from the end of the planks, where Potter was sitting up in a puddle with a blanket round him, coughing, and her own grey scarf, which he had plainly gone into the lake in, wound twice round his neck and hanging off him like weed.

It was cashmere. She decided to say so later.

Bagman was standing over him, beaming, with the megaphone under his arm. “Harry! Harry, my boy! The merpeople tell us you were down there with them the best part of the hour! Waiting for the others—making sure they all came up—a lovely bit of sportsmanship, lovely, the judges will—” He clapped Potter on his wet shoulder. “What a show!”

Potter looked up at him. He was white with cold, and his lips were blue, and there was something in his face that Lyra had seen there only on the bad days.

“It’s not a task,” he said. “It’s a spectacle.”

Bagman’s smile stayed exactly where it was, as smiles do on men who have not heard what was said to them, and he went off to tell the judges.

Then Fleur arrived.

She came down the jetty with her little sister already clutched to her front, the two of them dripping on each other and talking in very fast French, and she stopped in front of Potter and looked down at him with her lovely face all wet. “You saved ’er,” said Fleur. “She was not even yours to save, and you saved ’er—” and she bent down, and took his face in both her hands, and kissed him on the left cheek.

She was turning his face, quite clearly, for the right.

Lyra stepped forward.

She did it beautifully. She had been doing it since she was four years old, at her mother’s parties, to women who wore more diamonds than Fleur Delacour would see in her life. She put one hand lightly on each of Fleur’s shoulders, so that Fleur had to let go of Potter’s face to turn round, and she kissed Fleur on the right cheek, and on the left, and on the right again, as they did in Paris, before Fleur had quite worked out what was happening.

“*Merci*,” said Lyra warmly, in a French so perfect that two people from Beauxbatons turned round, “*de me l’avoir rendu.*” Thank you for giving him back to me.

Fleur looked at her. For a moment she looked very much as though she might be offended; and then she began to laugh, a real laugh, quite helplessly, with her sister still in her arms. She said something in French that Lyra decided to take as a compliment, and went away up the jetty, still laughing, with Gabrielle.

Potter sat in his puddle in his blanket and looked up at Lyra. He did not seem to know where to look; he looked at her, and at Fleur’s back, and at the planks, and at her again.

“You’re wearing my scarf,” said Lyra.

“You put it on me.”

“A week ago.”

“I didn’t want to lose it,” said Potter, and his blue lips went up at one corner, and she turned away from him quickly before it could do anything to her face.

When the marks went up, he was level with Diggory at the top of the table, and the whole of Gryffindor was screaming, and Weasley was being sick into the lake.

Lyra looked for the judges, out of habit, before she went back up to the stand.

They were rising from the gold-draped table, one after another, in the noise: Bagman bouncing, Madame Maxime gathering her furs, Karkaroff hurrying, Dumbledore smiling at everybody. At the far end, Mr Crouch got to his feet with the others, very straight, and buttoned his neat dark coat, and walked away along the bank towards the castle, past the edge of the water, with his eyes fixed in front of him.

He had not looked at the lake in an hour. He did not look at it now.
