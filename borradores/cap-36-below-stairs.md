# Chapter 36: Below Stairs

Mad-Eye Moody passed them on the fifth-floor landing on the first Tuesday in March, coming down as they went up, and did not say a word.

The blue eye came round as he passed and settled on the two of them, Lyra on the inside by the banister and Potter on the outside with his bag over one shoulder, and stayed on them, swivelling slowly backwards in his head as he went on down the stairs, *clunk* and step, until the turn of the staircase took him out of sight. It was the look a man gives two coins he suspects of being forged. Lyra had been getting it twice a week since January, in lessons, and had stopped minding it in February, more or less.

Potter waited until the leg had gone right down to the fourth floor.

“Dumbledore sent for me,” he said. “Last night.”

Lyra did not stop climbing. “What for?”

“Moody. Sort of.” He was quiet for half a flight. The portraits on that stretch of staircase had given up leaning out to watch them by the end of January, which was a mercy. “He wanted to know how sure I’d been. About the trunk. Before I went in.”

“And what did you say?”

“I said completely.”

“And?”

Potter shifted the bag on his shoulder.

“He said, *That is what troubles me.*” He said it in a fair imitation of the voice, gentle and unhurried, and it was not funny. “And then he gave me a lemon drop and sent me to bed.”

Lyra climbed two more steps.

She put it with the rest. It went in beside *I know* and *I was sure* and a dead man said twice, and it did not settle there, it lay on top of the others and would not go down; and she noticed, besides, the thing that was stranger than any of it, which was that he had told her. She had not asked. She had not so much as looked at him in the way that meant *ask me*. He had simply come up the stairs beside her on a Tuesday morning and told her what Dumbledore had said to him in the night, as though it were the most natural thing in the world, as though they did that.

She did not say any of that, in case he stopped.

“Well,” said Lyra. “He’s quite right. It *is* troubling. You’re very rarely completely anything. You’re usually only completely wrong.”

“Thanks.”

“It was a lemon drop, I suppose, and not a Toffee Éclair.”

“It’s always lemon drops.”

“That,” said Lyra, “is the most troubling thing you’ve told me.”

He laughed, and they went on up to the seventh floor, and at the door of the Arithmancy classroom he stopped, as he had stopped every morning since January, and did not go in, and did not quite go away either.

“Divination,” said Lyra. “North Tower. You’ll be late.”

“I’m always late.” He did not move. “Lyra.”

“What?”

“Nothing,” said Potter. “See you at lunch.”

He went off down the corridor with his hands in his pockets, and Lyra went in to Arithmancy and sat down in her usual place by the window, and opened her book at the right page, and looked at the tables of values for some time without reading them.

The Hogsmeade weekend was the second Saturday in March, and it was the first time they had ever walked down the drive together in daylight with nobody under anything.

It was not, Lyra decided, at all what she had imagined. She had imagined it a great many times, in the summer, behind her bed-hangings, and in every version they had been alone. In none of them had Weasley been there, walking on Potter’s other side with his hands in his pockets, complaining about the Chudley Cannons, and Granger behind them reading a book as she walked and somehow never falling into a puddle; and in none of them had half the school been coming down the drive behind them in a long, cheerful, staring procession, with Parkinson somewhere in the middle of it telling anybody who would listen that it was all for her father.

She found she did not mind as much as she would have expected. She minded Weasley, a little.

The Three Broomsticks was as hot and crowded and loud as it had been the first time, in her third year, when she had come in out of the rain and seen Potter across the room and not looked at him for an hour. Today he went to the bar for four butterbeers and came back with them, and sat down next to her at a table in the middle of the room where everybody could see, and the whole pub watched him do it. A table of Ravenclaws by the window had stopped talking altogether. Two Slytherin sixth-years at the bar were looking at her as though she had come in wearing a hat made of cabbages.

Lyra took her butterbeer, and looked at the table of Ravenclaws until they started talking again.

“You paid for these,” she said to Potter.

“Yes.”

“All four.”

“Yes.”

“That puts me in your debt,” said Lyra. “I shall pay for the next ones.”

“You can’t,” said Weasley at once. “It’s Harry’s round. And then it’s Hermione’s round, and then it’s my round, and then it’s yours. That’s how it goes.”

“That is not how it goes. That is a system designed by a Weasley so that a Weasley never has to pay for anything until everybody has gone home.”

Weasley went red. “It is *not*—”

“You have one Sickle in your pocket,” said Lyra, “and you’ve been holding it since the gates, because you think nobody can see you doing it.”

Weasley took his hand out of his pocket, empty. Granger, without looking up from her book, said, “She’s got you there, Ron,” and turned a page.

“Whose side are you on?”

“Nobody’s. I’m reading.”

They argued about it for ten minutes, and then about the Chudley Cannons for another ten, and then about whether Krum would have caught the Snitch at the World Cup if Lynch had not hit the ground first, which was not an argument at all since Lyra had been in the top box and Weasley had been in the row in front of her, and they both knew perfectly well what had happened; and somewhere in the middle of it Lyra realised that she was enjoying herself. It was a peculiar feeling. She had quarrelled with Weasley at intervals for nearly four years, on staircases and in the grounds and on the gravel outside the doors, and every one of those quarrels had been meant. This one was not. They were both doing it because it was what they did, the way two dogs from neighbouring houses go to the fence every morning to bark at each other, and would be lost without it.

She thought of saying so, and decided he would take it badly, and said instead that the Chudley Cannons had not won a match since the reign of Queen Victoria, which he took worse.

Potter did not say much. He sat between them with his butterbeer and looked from one to the other, and every so often he put his head down on his arms on the table and laughed until his shoulders shook, and once, when Weasley said something so stupid about the Wimbourne Wasps that even Granger looked up, Potter’s hand found Lyra’s under the table and held on to it, as though he needed something to keep him upright.

She let him.

When they came out it had begun, very lightly, to rain, the fine silvery rain of March that hardly seemed to fall at all, and the High Street was full of students going from Honeydukes to Zonko’s and back with their collars up. Granger had gone ahead with her book over her head. Weasley had stopped to look in the window of Dervish and Banges.

They were outside the post office. Lyra had stopped to pull her hood up, and Potter had stopped because she had, and he looked at her, in the rain, with drops on his glasses and the High Street going past on either side of them; and then he bent his head and kissed her.

It was very short. It was not for luck, or to stop her saying anything, or because there was a dragon. It was in the middle of Hogsmeade on a wet Saturday afternoon, between the post office and a shop that sold quills, and there was no reason for it at all.

“Oh, *honestly*,” said Weasley, from the window of Dervish and Banges.

Lyra stepped back, and straightened her hood.

“Not,” she said, with great dignity, “in front of the post office.”

“Right,” said Potter. “Sorry.” He did not look sorry. He looked as if somebody had given him something, and he had not expected it, and did not know where to put it. “Where, then?”

Lyra walked on up the street without answering, with her hood up and her face perfectly composed, and did not look back to see whether he was following, because she could hear that he was.
