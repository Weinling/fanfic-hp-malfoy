# Chapter 29: Every Ounce

Weasley was waiting at the foot of the marble staircase on Monday morning with his ears the colour of the Gryffindor banners, and he was plainly not waiting for breakfast.

Lyra saw him from the top of the dungeon steps. She had come up early with Crabbe, Goyle having got as far as one stocking and told them to go on without her, and she stopped where she was. One did not walk into the middle of a thing like that. Besides, the dungeon steps were in shadow and the Entrance Hall was not.

Potter came down the marble staircase with Granger. He was talking, and Granger was answering him over an armful of books, and then he saw who was standing at the bottom and stopped talking, and came down the last few steps more slowly.

Weasley said something. Lyra could not hear it over the noise of people going in to breakfast, and in any case he said it mostly to the floor: a short mumble with a shrug at the end, which was how boys of his sort said anything that had cost them something.

Potter did not let him finish. He came off the bottom step and put both arms round him, hard, as though he had been standing at the top of that staircase for a fortnight waiting to be allowed to. Weasley staggered, and said “Gerroff,” and did not push him off.

Then Granger burst into tears.

She did it all at once, over the top of her books, and then she dropped the books and put an arm round each of them and held on, saying something wet and furious that seemed to be about both of them being idiots. Weasley stood in the middle of it with his hands up at the height of his shoulders, as though somebody had pointed a wand at him, opening and shutting his fingers, with no idea where to put them.

Gryffindors, Lyra reflected, did everything in public. They apologised in Entrance Halls, and wept on staircases, and left their schoolbooks on the flagstones for anybody to tread on, and seemed to feel a great deal better for it. It was a wonder any of them had a scrap of dignity left by the fourth year.

Over Weasley’s shoulder, Granger opened her wet eyes and saw her.

It lasted a moment. Granger looked straight across the Hall to the dungeon steps, to where Lyra stood in the shadow with Crabbe, and there was something in her face that Lyra did not care for at all. It was not quite gratitude; Granger had learned better than to offer Lyra that. It was knowing. Weasley had told them where he had got it from. Of course he had. Weasley could not have kept a secret in a locked trunk.

Lyra examined the ceiling.

“That was you,” said Crabbe, beside her.

“I have no idea what you mean.”

“All right,” said Crabbe, and went on in to breakfast.

Lyra followed more slowly. She was glad. That was the irritating part. For the last week Potter had been cheerful in a loud, careless way that had taken in the entire school, and now he would not have to be; he had them back, both of them, at every meal and in every lesson and in the next bed, and she had done that, and she was glad, and it put her in a temper all through breakfast.

Because she had something for him too. She had been carrying it since last night, since the shingle and the blue eye in the dark: three things off Karkaroff’s table, and nowhere to set them down. Potter was going to spend the whole day inside a knot of Gryffindors, and the next day, and the day after that. She could hardly walk up to him in the Great Hall and say, in front of four Houses and two foreign schools, *Crouch sent his son to Azkaban to die*.

She buttered a piece of toast, and looked at it, and arrived at a decision.

Three crumbs off a man’s table were not worth the risk of handing them over. Her father had never in his life gone into a room holding half a hand, and nor would she. There was a first task in ten days that nobody in the castle was supposed to know anything about. Somebody did. Somebody always did. And by the time she got Potter on his own, she meant to have the whole of it.
