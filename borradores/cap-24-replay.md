# Chapter 24: Replay

“Lyra! *Lyra*, look! It’s Viktor Krum!”

Draco came into the morning room at a run, without knocking, which he had been told about, and unrolled a poster across her Arithmancy before she could stop him. On it, a young man with heavy black eyebrows and a nose like a hawk’s was hurtling straight down at the ground on a broom, faster and faster, until it seemed impossible that he would not go into it head first; then at the last instant he pulled up, the player behind him ploughed into the turf, and the picture swung round to show Krum rising, unhurried, with a Snitch in his fist. Underneath, the words *WRONSKI FEINT* blazed up in red and faded, and the whole thing began again.

“I thought you supported Ireland,” said Lyra.

“Ireland’s Seeker is *boring*. Krum’s *eighteen*, Lyra. He’s still at school and he’s Seeker for the whole of Bulgaria.” Draco was ten that summer, and he said *the whole of Bulgaria* as though he had personally been consulted about it. “Look, he does it again in a minute—”

Lyra leaned in to look. Draco snatched the poster back against his chest.

“No. You can’t see. It’s mine.”

There was a time, not very long ago, when Lyra would simply have taken it off him. But he was ten now, and had found out about stubbornness, and on the subject of stubbornness Lyra knew she was up against the only person in the Manor with more of it than she had. So instead she reached under the table, into the bag she had been meaning to show him anyway, and brought out a pair of brass binoculars covered in little knobs and dials.

Draco’s eyes went perfectly round.

“What are *those*?”

“Omnioculars. Slow motion. Replay. They’ll follow any player you like for the whole match, and keep it, so you can watch it again afterwards.”

“Give me a go!”

“I don’t think I shall,” said Lyra, and turned them so that the light ran up and down the brass. “Unless you’d like to swap.”

It took him nearly a minute, a long time for Draco, to put the poster down on the table. He took the omnioculars with both hands and went to the window with them at once, to look at a peacock.

Lyra unrolled the poster and watched Krum come out of the dive again. He really was extraordinary. He did it with so little visible effort, as though the ground had been a suggestion. It was only the face that let it down: heavy and sullen, and rather a waste of a dive like that. If it had been a different face, somebody’s with untidy hair and glasses, say—

“You *fancy* him!” Draco had the omnioculars trained on her from the window. “Girls are all the same. They see somebody good at Quidditch and they go all stupid.”

“I do not fancy Viktor Krum,” said Lyra, with great clarity. “Not even slightly.”

Draco lowered the omnioculars and looked at her over the top of them. Then he looked at the omnioculars, and turned them over, and looked at the dials for rather longer than she liked.

“Are these the real ones? The Irish ones?”

“Of course they are.”

He looked at her a moment more, with their mother’s face, the one that asked nothing and waited. Lyra held it. Draco said, “All right,” in a voice that did not believe her and did not much mind, and took the poster back off the table. “I’m having this back as well, then. For the cheating. In case.” And he went out with both his treasures held against his chest, calling from the hall that he was going to show the gentleman.

Lyra sat where he had left her, with the real ones in her lap: Irish team issue, made for the squad’s own training, gold leaf on every dial. There were perhaps a dozen pairs in the world. She was nearly sure she had won. She was much less sure what she had won, and she had certainly lost the poster.

The gentleman in the east cottage still lived in the east cottage, then. She had not asked, all summer, for the same reason she had not asked at Christmas, and nobody had said.

She turned the omnioculars over, and thought about whom, exactly, she had bought them to look at.

The ring had gone gold at a quarter past two on a Tuesday morning, and Lyra, who had not been asleep, had sat up to look at it.

She drew the hangings round her bed at nine o’clock every night that summer and put it on in the dark. It was certainly not because somebody had asked her to, under a cloister, in May. The slip of instructions from Zonko’s listed more colours than any reasonable person could want, and in July she had found out that most of them were real. Purple, *on guard*. Gold, *success; something accomplished*. Green, *safe*. Red she had not seen since February, and did not intend to see again. That Tuesday it had been gold for about a minute, then green, and she had lain back on her pillow with the satisfied feeling of somebody who has read the last page of a book first.

A boy whose ring went purple at four in the morning and gold at a quarter past two was plainly not spending the summer weeding his aunt’s garden, and it was very rude of him not to say what he *was* doing. He wrote, sometimes, by birds she had never seen before, a different one every time, and signed himself *D. Dursley*, which was so ugly a name that it could only belong to a real person. The letters told her it was hot. They told her to look after herself. Once, at the bottom, there was a line she read twice and then put inside the cover of a book she did not intend to lend to anybody. She answered twice, by owls hired at the post office in Diagon Alley on the days her mother took her shopping, since anything sent by the family owl went past her father’s study window first.

“He’s telling *me* to be careful,” she had said, to the canopy of her bed. The ring, at that moment, had been purple.

The rings worked both ways. That was the part of the slip she minded most. So when she lay down at night she was careful to think about nothing in particular, her Arithmancy, or the new broom polish, or the Quidditch World Cup, which her father had got them into the top box for. She was not going to lie there thinking about anything that might come out blue.

He would be at the World Cup. Everybody would be at the World Cup.

The Malfoys did not queue.

The Ministry had connected a handful of private fireplaces to the stadium for the use of important persons, and the Minister for Magic had invited Lucius Malfoy to use one, so they stepped out of the Manor’s drawing-room grate and into a panelled anteroom somewhere under the stands without so much as a smut on their robes. Lyra had heard that other people had walked for miles over wet fields at dawn, with a Portkey that looked like an old boot. She was sorry for them, in a general way.

Cornelius Fudge was waiting at the top of the stairs, in a bowler hat and a state of great excitement. He shook her father’s hand for a long time, and kissed her mother’s, and then turned his beaming face on Lyra and Draco.

“And these are the children! At Hogwarts, aren’t you? Both of you?”

“Lyra is,” said her mother. “Draco goes next year.”

“Well, well.” Fudge winked at Lyra, enormously. “Lucky things. You’re in for something rather special this year, you know. Very special indeed. But I mustn’t say.”

Draco, who had decided that this was a deliberate insult to anybody who would not be at Hogwarts until next year, glared at the Minister for Magic with such concentrated loathing that Fudge spent the next three flights of stairs trying to win him round with talk of Bulgarian Chasers.

Lyra hardly heard any of it.

*Especially next year. If students come from other schools.* Potter had said it to her in May, out of breath, with a ring in his hand, and she had been too angry at the time to ask what on earth he meant by it. Now the Minister for Magic was winking at her about something special at Hogwarts that he mustn’t say, and he was the Minister, and it was his business to know. Potter was fourteen, and it was not.

They had reached the top box. It was small and beautifully placed, at the very top of the stands, exactly halfway between the goals, with two rows of purple and gilt chairs; and the front row was full of Weasleys.

Lyra stopped in the doorway.

There were a great many of them, in a row, red-headed and shabby, and Granger at the end in a jumper, and in the middle of them all, with his elbows on the rail and a pair of ordinary brass omnioculars round his neck, was Potter.

She had brought the good omnioculars for the express purpose of finding him somewhere in the cheap seats on the far side of the stadium. He was sitting one row in front of her.

“Arthur,” said Fudge, with great heartiness. “You’ll know Lucius, of course. He’s made a most generous donation to St Mungo’s. I asked him along myself.”

Mr Weasley and her father greeted each other with the expressions of two men who had each been handed something unpleasant to hold. Her father led his family past the Weasleys to their seats, and as Lyra went by, Potter looked round.

He nodded to her. It was a perfectly civil nod, the kind one gave to somebody from school whose name one could just about remember, and then he turned back to the pitch.

That was what they had agreed. Not in front of people. Not anywhere anybody could see. Lyra had told him at the time exactly what she thought of the arrangement, and then walked off with his ring on her finger, which came to the same thing as agreeing. It was quite sensible, and she would have been extremely annoyed if he had done anything else.

She sat down.

It was still a perfectly horrible nod.

Beside her, her mother arranged her robes, and looked at Lyra, and then, for a little longer, at the back of the row in front.

At the far end of their own row a house-elf sat bolt upright on a gilt chair with both hands over its eyes, minding the empty one next to it for a master who was evidently too important to arrive on time. Lyra thought it very poor form to bring an elf to the top box, and worse to leave it there frightened of the drop.

The Bulgarian mascots came out first, and they were veela.

Lyra had read about veela. She had not been prepared for a hundred of them at once: women who were not women, with skin that shone as if the moon were somewhere behind it and hair that moved about their shoulders with no wind to move it. The music started and they began to dance, and within a minute every male in the stadium had lost his mind. In the box below, a Ministry wizard was taking his hat off and putting it on again, very quickly, as though he could not decide which suited him better. In the row in front, Ron Weasley had one leg over the rail.

Lyra looked at all of it with deep and untroubled contempt, and then thought, *wait*, and looked at Potter.

He had his omnioculars up to his eyes. He was leaning forward on the rail, perfectly still, quite absorbed, and the corner of his mouth had gone up, and stayed up. If the music was doing anything to him, it did not show.

Lyra’s mouth went thin.

“Lyra,” said Draco dreamily, from her other side, “when I grow up I’m going to marry a veela.”

She cuffed him round the back of the head. It was not for any reason. Somebody in the family had to keep their wits about them, and the music had stopped.

In the row in front, Potter made a small snorting noise into his omnioculars.

The Irish mascots were leprechauns, a glittering green cloud of them, and they rained gold on the crowd until the stands rang with it. Weasley caught an armful and pressed a fistful into Potter’s hand with a face of shining joy—“For the omnioculars, Harry! *Ten Galleons*—” and Potter put it in his pocket without looking round. He still had the omnioculars up. He was still smiling. Watching the veela again on the replay, Lyra supposed, and took a dislike to the entire Bulgarian nation.

Then the match began, and for a while even Lyra forgot about Potter. She forgot the elf as well, until the first Irish goal, when the empty chair beside it gave a creak, as though somebody had settled into it to watch.

She had never seen flying like it. The Irish Chasers moved like one person in three bodies, and they were ten goals up before the Bulgarians had worked out where they were. Krum alone was worth the price of the box. He did the Wronski Feint for real, once, and the Irish Seeker went into it and the whole stadium stood up. It was not enough. Ireland were a hundred and sixty points ahead when Krum caught the Snitch, and everybody could count: he had caught it knowing it would lose them the match by ten, to finish it himself rather than be beaten slowly.

Lyra, who had once let go of her broom in a thunderstorm rather than win a match the wrong way, understood him perfectly, and her fist closed on the rail.

“Oh, *no*, I missed it—Harry, give us a look—” Weasley had the omnioculars off Potter’s neck before Potter could stop him, and pressed the replay. Then he went quite still. After a moment he handed them back with an extraordinary expression.

“Mate,” he said. “The *whole* match?”

“Krum got the Snitch?” said Potter. “So who won?”

Weasley shook his head slowly at him, as at somebody past help, and went off down the row to Granger. Potter scratched the back of his neck and said nothing at all.

Lyra put it down to Weasley.

Her father did not come back to the tent with them.

He saw the three of them inside it, a tent with four rooms and a chandelier and Pobble’s supper laid out on a table that had certainly never been near a field before, and then he put on his travelling cloak. It was not the cloak he had worn to the match.

“Stay in the campsite,” he said. “All of you. I have some old friends to see.” He kissed Narcissa’s cheek, and Lyra’s forehead, and ruffled Draco’s hair, and went out, and the tent flap fell shut behind him.

Her mother looked at the flap for a moment after it had stopped moving. Then she turned back to the table and asked Draco whether he wanted the chicken or the salmon.

Draco wanted to talk. He was so full of the match that it seemed to be leaking out of him at the edges, and since Lyra was the only other person in the tent who had ever been on a broom, it was mostly leaking at her. By the fiftieth question about the Wronski Feint she was answering in single syllables.

“He sounds,” said her mother, “like a mandrake that has just been pulled up. Go out and get some air, darling. I’ll see to him. Don’t go far. We may be leaving early.”

Outside, this end of the campsite was keeping up appearances. It was where the Ministry put people who mattered, and while fireworks went up over the far fields and somebody a long way off was singing the Irish anthem very badly, here the tents had striped awnings and the celebrating was being done with dignity. Lyra walked a little way along the path between them, out of the light from her own, and took the ring out of her pocket, and put it on.

It went pink.

*In the company of their sweetheart.*

She looked at it. It went on being pink. So that was where Potter had got to: somewhere in this campsite, with somebody, while she stood here in the dark holding a ring that had the effrontery to tell her so. One of the veela, presumably. They had been very much his sort of thing, to judge by the omnioculars.

It had done this once before. In February, on the night of the full moon, it had gone red and then pink and then red again, as if it could not decide, and she had not understood why until afterwards: that there had been two of Potter that night, one lying on the shore and one in the castle, under a cloak, standing beside—

Lyra stopped walking.

“Harry?” she said, to the air.

“Long time,” said the air, very close to her ear, and something cool fell over her head and shoulders, and the world went on exactly as it had been, except that now there was Potter standing in it, an inch away, grinning.

A minute ago she had had a great deal to say to him, all of it sharp. Now that he was actually there, the sharp things had gone somewhere, and what was left was a ridiculous urge to walk forward into him, forehead first, and stay there. Her pride caught it just in time. She folded her arms.

“Idiot. You took your time.”

“Sorry. I followed you under this. I couldn’t exactly come into the tent, so I’ve been out here.” He considered. “For a bit.”

It occurred to Lyra that the one who had been kept waiting was not, strictly speaking, her.

“Draco,” she said, “is a great deal of work.”

“He’s loud, isn’t he.”

Potter’s arm came round her shoulders. She knocked it away with the back of her hand. She was very glad to see him; there was, however, a matter to be settled first.

“You couldn’t take your eyes off the veela. Were they really so very wonderful?”

Potter looked honestly baffled. “I don’t know much about magical creatures,” he said. “I know one with platinum hair who makes men stupid, though. She’s standing right here.”

“Do you really imagine you can get out of it like that?”

And then she saw them, hanging from his wrist by their strap: the omnioculars. With replay.

She had them off him before he could move, and found the dial, and turned it back to the beginning of the match.

“I will not be lied to,” she informed him, with her eye to the lenses. “If you think you can talk your way round me with a lot of pretty—”

She stopped.

Only one setting had been on all afternoon. It was the one that followed a single player round the pitch, whatever happened, and kept them in the middle of the picture.

It had not been following a player.

There was Lyra Malfoy with her arms folded, glaring at the back of somebody’s head. There was Lyra Malfoy cuffing her little brother round the ear, and sighing, and smoothing her robes. There was Lyra Malfoy leaning forward when the match began, with her lips parted and her eyes very bright, and half out of her seat when Krum did the Feint, and with her fist clenched on the rail as he caught the Snitch. Two hours of it. Every second.

They had been made for watching Quidditch at the World Cup, the greatest match of the age, with half the wizarding world in the stands. He had watched her.

Lyra lowered the omnioculars, very slowly. Potter, who had obviously been waiting for exactly this, grinned.

“Accusing people without any evidence,” he said. “That’s not very fair, Malfoy. There are laws about that, you know.”

She looked at the omnioculars in her hands. Then she hung them round her own neck.

“These,” said Lyra, “are evidence. I’m keeping them.”

“Ron paid me ten Galleons for those.”

“Leprechaun gold. It’ll be gone by morning. I saw.” She put a hand flat over them. “And as for the false accusation, you may put it on my account.”

“I’d rather settle now,” said Potter.

His arm came round her shoulders again, and this time she let it stay. He had sat through the World Cup final and not watched a minute of it, and she was so embarrassed about that and so pleased about it that she could not tell the two apart, and she had not been alone with him since May. His hand came up, slowly, to her face, and she found that she had lifted her face to meet it, and shut her eyes.

And behind her shut eyes, for no reason she could have given, was a tent flap falling closed, and a travelling cloak that was not the one he had worn to the match.

Lyra opened her eyes.

Potter had stopped, a breath away, and for a moment looked as though he could not think what he had done wrong.

“Harry,” she said. “My father went out. He changed his cloak first. *Old friends*, he said. Where are they all going?”

He did not make a joke of it. That was how she knew it was a real question. His face changed, under the cloak, in the dark, into the one she did not like, and then he put it away again, and what was left was only tired.

“Go back to your mum,” he said. “Stay with her tonight. Don’t go wandering about.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No,” said Potter. “It isn’t.”

“Lyra?” Her mother’s voice, from the door of the tent. “Your father’s sent word, we— Now where has she got to?”

Lyra was out of his arms in an instant, pushing the omnioculars down inside her robes, putting her hair back. Under a cloak, of course, but even so. *Here*, of all places, a few yards from her mother.

“Too many people about,” murmured Potter, and very nearly made it sound like a joke. “Shame.” He put his hand on her hair, very briefly, and leaned in, and kissed her forehead.

Then the cloak slid off her shoulders and he was gone inside it, a ripple in the dark between two tents.

“See you at school,” said the ripple.

Lyra stood on the path, perfectly visible, with her face burning and the ring on her finger shining pink. She put her hand in her pocket just as her mother came round the corner of the tent.

Her mother stopped. She looked at Lyra’s face for some time in the light from the awning. Then her eyes went down, to the pocket, and stayed there a moment longer, and came back up.

“There you are,” said Narcissa Malfoy. “We’re going home. Your father will follow.”

They went back by the Ministry fireplace, the three of them, with Draco protesting all the way that it was not fair and nobody else was going to bed.

Lyra was the last into the grate. As she stepped in, with the Floo powder in her hand, she heard the noise from the far fields change. There was still shouting, but it was not singing any more, and it was not cheering, and somewhere under it a great many people had begun to run. In her pocket, under her fingers, the ring was growing warm.

Her mother did not look round.
