# Chapter 60: Furniture

“You will go to Professor Snape,” said Dumbledore, “on Tuesdays.”

Harry stopped with a lemon drop halfway to his mouth.

It was the Saturday after Valentine’s Day. He had come up the spiral stair in a good mood, which was a thing he kept forgetting to hide; he had a piece of the second-best paper folded in his back pocket, with a date on it and a time. Fawkes was asleep. The little silver instruments went on puffing and ticking on their spindly tables. The Headmaster had his fingers together and he looked like somebody who had not been to bed.

“What for?”

“Occlumency. He will explain it to you, I expect, with his usual warmth.” Dumbledore looked at the tips of his fingers. “Until now, Harry, you have been seeing what Voldemort sees. You told me about a long room by the sea, and people on their knees. He has settled somewhere now, I think. He has leisure. He has had his attention drawn to you. I believe it’s only a matter of time before it occurs to him that a door opens both ways.”

Harry put the lemon drop down on the desk. He knew what Occlumency was. He had done it before, with the same man, in the same office, in another life. It had lasted a few weeks, and it had ended with him looking into the wrong Pensieve and being thrown across the room by the arm; and a girl with a lot of red hair had been in it, and a good man had died of what he hadn’t learnt.

“I can’t,” said Harry.

“I’m afraid I must insist.”

“No, I mean I *can’t*. Not him. Sir, he’ll go in. That’s what it is. He’ll go in and walk about in my head, and it’s all *in* there.” He had got up without noticing. “There’s the Shack. There’s the—he’ll see the end of it. He’ll see the floor, and the snake, and he’ll see himself. He’ll see what I saw when I looked in that basin. You can’t send me to Snape. You might as well hand him the bottle.”

Dumbledore did not answer at once. He took off his half-moon spectacles, which was a thing Harry had seen him do perhaps four times in two lives, and he held them up to the window and looked through them at the snow.

“Yes,” he said.

It took a moment.

“You want him to,” said Harry.

“I have known for some three years what is going to be asked of Severus Snape,” said Dumbledore, “and how he will be repaid. I have sat across this desk from him every week. I have let him go on thinking that he kept his secret from everyone but me. Each time, I told myself that I should find a better moment.” He put the spectacles back on. They made his eyes look very blue and very old. “I don’t think there is one. I have been a coward about it, Harry. I find I would rather you knew that, before you go down there, than that you thought I had a plan.”

There did not seem to be anything to say to this. It had never once occurred to him, in either life, that Albus Dumbledore could be got to say that sort of thing out loud. It made him feel extremely unsafe.

“Do try to keep him out,” said Dumbledore, more gently. “He will know if you don’t.”

It was a Tuesday, and it was raining on the snow.

He knew the way. He knew the stairs, and the smell of the passage, and the third flagstone from the door, which rocked. He had been coming down here since he was eleven, and for a good while now he had been the best in the year but one. Her hand had been on this door on Thursday nights since September. She had told him that much. He had not been able to think of any way to tell her where he was tonight, and he had not tried very hard.

“Sit,” said Snape.

He had not looked up. He was marking. It was the same office: the same jars, with their soft dead things in coloured water, and the fire that gave no heat, and the desk. There was a window that had been mended with slightly greener glass. Harry sat. He had worked out how he was going to do this. He had thought about it for three days. He had looked for a way of walking in there and saying something; and he had not found one, and he got out his wand and held it on his knee.

Snape signed something, blotted it, and laid down his pen.

He looked at Harry then. He had been looking at him in the same way for four years, with no expression at all, like somebody reading a thermometer. It was the look that had started on a night in a winter corridor when a boy of eleven had said *You killed my mum and dad*. It had not changed by a hair. He had never once mentioned it. In all the years since, he had marked Harry’s essays with a fairness you could have cut yourself on.

“I am told,” said Snape, “that you have a mind. I am instructed to teach you to close it. You will appreciate that I undertake this in the same spirit in which one might undertake to teach a sieve.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You will empty it. You will not succeed. You will attempt to resist me by whatever means you have, and I expect that I shall be bored. Wand out. On your feet.”

He got up. He stood on the worn place on the flagstones. Snape came round the desk, without any hurry, with his long sallow hands hanging at his sides, and raised his wand a few inches, and there was a moment when you could have heard the rain.

“*Legilimens*.”

The office went away.

There was a cupboard. He knew it was a mistake, and he tried to get in front of it, and it was gone. There was Dudley; there was a fat boy’s foot and a bicycle; there was a green stool in a robe shop, and a girl with her chin up. He tried to hold on to something. He had forgotten how fast it went. There was the sixth floor, and a wrist. *No.* There was a rug, somewhat bald, and an armchair; somebody was laughing with her forehead down on the arm of it, and there was a sheet of paper with a time on it, and he felt the thing that was looking stop, and bend closer, and for one instant he was so frightened for her that he did something he had not known he could do. He shut it.

It was not what you were supposed to do. He knew that as he did it. He got hold of the whole of that room, with her in it, and he shoved it behind him, with all his strength, and he stood with his back against it; and the cold attentive presence in his head turned, quite slowly, away from the place where it had been stopped, and looked round to see what else in there was shut.

There was only one other thing.

He had never gone in. He had built that door himself, a long time ago, and he walked past it the way that you walk past a room in a house where somebody has died. He felt the presence come up to it. *No*, said Harry, or somebody did. *No, don’t, sir, not that, you don’t want—*

It opened.

There was a dusty floor with a hole in it. The boards were grey, and the shutters were nailed up, and a little dirty light came in. There was a thin man in black, lying against a wall. He had both of his hands up at his neck. There was a great deal of blood. It was going over his fingers and down inside his collar. It made a noise. He was looking at something that had just gone out of the room, slithering, in a sphere of stars; and then he turned his head, and it took him a long time. He saw who was kneeling in front of him. His lips moved. Something was coming out of the corners of his eyes that was not tears. It was silver and it would not stop.

*Take it.*

*Take … it.*

There was a flask, in a hand that shook. The hand was Harry’s.

*Look … at … me.*

The black eyes found the green ones. He had got his fingers into the front of a boy’s robes, and he held on to them, and whatever had been behind his eyes went out, a long way away, and left them fixed and blank. The hand fell. He made no more noise after that.

It did not stop. Harry had thought that it would stop. It went on going; he could not have stopped it now; the flask, a stair, a basin, and a red-haired girl, on a swing; a small sallow boy behind a bush, looking at her. It ran down and down. *Always*, somebody said. There was a boy of seventeen going downstairs. It was early, and getting light. There were a lot of dark trees. He had a stone in his hand, and there were some people walking beside him, and he was so tired. There was a clearing with a fire in it, and a tall thin figure with long white fingers lifting a wand. He looked at it. He was not afraid. He looked at it, and he thought of nothing; there was a flash of green—

There wasn’t anything.

It simply ended. It ended as a floor ends. It had a clean edge. There was the green, and then you were standing at the edge of where there was no more of anything, with your foot out; and there was nobody there. He felt the cold thing that was holding him stand at the end of it, and look over.

He was on his hands and knees. His wand was on the other side of the room. He had been sick, a little, on the flagstones. He could hear somebody breathing as if they had run a long way, and after a while he found that it was himself.

Nobody spoke.

He got one foot under him, and got up. It took two goes. Snape was standing where he had been standing. He had not moved. He had let his wand arm fall to his side, and with the other hand, with the tips of two long fingers, without appearing to know that he was doing it, he was touching the left side of his own throat.

There was no colour in his face. There never had been a lot. He stood there for what might have been a minute. The rain ran down the green glass. Then he walked across to the door, slowly and very upright, and he pointed his wand at it, and Harry heard the bolts go across, one after another, and a low humming come up in the stone.

He went back to the desk. He did not go round it. He put out one hand and felt for the edge, and he sat down on the visitor’s chair, the hard one, and he looked at the wall.

“How long have you known?” said Snape.

It was a perfectly ordinary voice. It was only rather dry.

“Since I was eleven,” said Harry.

“The corridor.”

“A bit after that. It came in bits. I’d got most of it by Easter.”

“Most.”

“All of it, sir,” said Harry. “That was the last thing. I got that in the summer after second year. Before that I thought—I thought what I said.”

There was a long silence. A coal fell in the grate.

“In September you were insolent,” said Snape, to the wall. “In the February you looked at me as though you had seen me fail, and were waiting for me to do it again. I told the Headmaster so. I said that when I looked at you I thought of the Dark Lord.” Nothing moved in his face. “He told me that I was tired. How long has he known?”

“Since the end of second year.”

“I see.”

He sat with that for a moment. He seemed to be looking at something on the wall a long way behind the jars.

“He sent you to me tonight,” said Snape. “Knowing what I would find.”

“Yes, sir. He said he’d been a coward about it. He said he wanted me to tell you that.” This was not quite what he had said. It would do. “He said there wasn’t going to be a good moment.”

Snape’s mouth moved, very slightly. It was not a smile. He had seen him do that once, on a hill, in another life, and it had not been a smile then.

“Then you have sat in my classroom for four years,” said Severus Snape, “knowing what I did. To her. Who took it to him. And what I did afterwards, and for how long, and why.” He turned his head at last. His eyes were quite black, and there was nothing in them that you could get a hold of. “And you have seen that. All of that. What was in the flask.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And?”

Harry opened his mouth.

He had had a long time to think about this. He had thought about it on a good many nights, in two lives; he had thought of things that you could say. He had once told a small boy on a railway platform something about that man. He stood in the middle of the office in a cold sweat, with his knees not working and sick on his sleeve. He looked at the long yellow face and the greasy hair, and the fingers that had come to rest against the side of the neck, and every one of those things went straight out of his head.

“I don’t hate you,” said Harry. “I did. For seven years. I said a lot of things. I’m not—I can’t think of anything else. Sorry.”

Snape looked at him for some time.

“No,” he said. “I shouldn’t think that you can.”

He took his hand away from his throat. He looked at it, as if he had found it in somebody else’s pocket, and laid it on his knee.

“It’s a snake,” he said. “Not a curse.”

“Yes, sir.”

“When?”

“The second of May. Ninety-eight. In the other one. It won’t be the same, I don’t—it’s all different now. He’s got a house. There’s things that have moved. I don’t know what’s going to happen any more.”

“Evidently.” There was another of those silences. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, and he did not seem to be speaking to Harry at all. “And it served? What I gave you. You took it down that stair, and into those trees. I saw that. You had it in your hand.” He stopped. “I saw where it ends. Did it serve?”

The rain had got heavier. Harry looked at the flagstones. He could still feel that edge. He had been walking past it for four years, and he had never before stood on it with anybody else.

“I don’t know,” said Harry. “It stops.”

Snape waited.

“That’s all there is. I went in there, and he did it. I never found out. I don’t know if it was any good. I don’t know if I—I don’t know what happened to anybody.” His voice did something, and he got it back. “I’ve never told anyone that. She asked me how it ended, and I said I couldn’t. It’s not that I won’t. I don’t *know*, sir. It just stops.”

For the first time since he had known him, in any life, Severus Snape looked at him as you look at a person. It lasted about as long as it takes to breathe in.

Then it was gone. He stood up. He crossed the room and picked up Harry’s wand from where it had rolled, between two fingers, as one picks up something that a cat has left, and held it out. He went round the desk. He sat down in his own chair, and drew a stack of Third Year essays towards him, and he uncapped his pen.

“You will never,” said Snape, “speak of what was in that flask. To anybody. Not to the Headmaster. Not to Miss Granger, whose name I have seen. Not to the person in the chair.”

Harry’s stomach dropped through the floor.

“I don’t ask about the chair,” said Snape. He did not look up. “I did not see it. I saw a boy shut a door, very badly, with his entire body, as one would hold a door against a flood. I have seen one person in twenty years do that with any talent, and she has been coming here on Thursdays. You will be pleased to hear that you did not do it as well.” He made a small mark on an essay. “It is the worst thing you could have done. You have shown me exactly where you keep what you want. So will you show him. You have, in that head of yours, one room with something living in it, and you’ve put a sign on the door. Sit down.”

“Sir—”

“I said that I would teach you, Potter. I don’t recall saying that anything had changed.” He raised his eyes. They were as they had always been. There was a great deal of dislike in them, and it was the most comforting thing that Harry had seen in months. “You have the mental discipline of a Hufflepuff in a sweetshop. Pick up your wand. Clean that floor; by hand. Then we shall begin again, and this time you will try to give me something dull.”

“How was your evening?” said Malfoy.

He had got there after she had. It was not on the timetable. He had not been able to think where else to go; he had gone up seven flights with his legs shaking, and walked three times past a wall. She was in the chair with her feet tucked up, and a letter in her hand, and she folded it in half as he came in and put it into her book. She looked at him over the top of it. She looked tired. He supposed it was Umbridge; she’d had tea with her again. She said that it was like being slowly embalmed.

There was a place on the arm of the chair. He sat down on it. He took her hand, and turned it over, and looked at it, because it was something that was alive.

*Not to the person in the chair.*

He thought about it. He thought about telling her that he had seen a man die today, the second time. That the man had got up and marked some essays. He thought of her Thursdays, and of that long yellow face turning towards her in the green light, knowing what it knew. *Nothing moves without asking*, she had said to him on a hill. He’d said *All right*.

“He’s foul,” said Harry.

“Who is?”

“Snape. I’ve got detention. Tuesdays. I said something.”

She looked at him a little longer. She was the cleverest person he had ever met. He saw her eyes go over his face from one side to the other, down the column, and stop; and he saw her decide. It went by like a bird past a window. He had no idea what it meant. She laid her book on the fender.

“How very like you,” said Malfoy. She took him by the front of his jumper, and kissed him, quite kindly, and rested her forehead for a moment against his. “You smell of disinfectant. Go to bed, Potter. You look like something that has been left out.”

It was only when he was on the stairs that he thought about the letter. She had not said who it was from. He had not asked. It was one more thing, he supposed, that they weren’t going to mention, and it struck him that it had been a remarkably short time.

He was sitting on something hard, and it was not his.

He knew the room. He had never been in it. It was a long room, with a lot of cold light in it, and pale panelling, and at the far end a window that looked out over a lawn with snow on it. Somebody had laid a fire. He had been told about this room, in a voice that did not shake, on a hill. He knew the table. There was a scorched place on the carpet that somebody had tried to get out. His hands were lying along the arms of the chair, and they were long and white and they had too many joints in them, and he felt perfectly well.

There was a man on the floor.

He was a small man, and he was on his knees. He had got a good deal smaller since a night by a lake. His hair had gone in patches. He was wearing what they had given him in Azkaban, and somebody’s old coat. He kept wringing his hands together, and one of them had a finger missing. Behind him the heavy-lidded woman was leaning against the mantel, cleaning her nails with something, and she was watching him as you would watch a bluebottle on a window.

“Again,” said Harry, in a high, light, courteous voice. “Take your time, Wormtail. Nobody is angry.”

“I—it was nothing, my lord, I only thought—it’s only the way he *looked* at me. That’s all. At the lake. I’d been in that dormitory for two and a half years. I slept on the Weasley boy’s pillow. And he’d look at me, my lord, the Potter boy, at night, when the others had gone off. He’d lie there on his side with his chin on his hand. He used to give me crusts. I thought nothing of it. I was a rat. And then he came out of those trees, and he’d got the same face on. Thirteen years old. He wasn’t surprised. I’ve thought about it, I’ve had two years in the dark to think. He looked at me as you’d look at somebody you’d known a long time, and were tired of. He said *You were in my dormitory*, no, I said— He *knew*, my lord. Not that night. He’d always known. He was eleven years old, and he knew what I was, and he fed me.”

The fire whispered.

The thing that was Harry sat quite still. Something was happening to him, and it was happening in the place under the ribs. It began small. It was warm. It rose, and it spread out along his arms; it was the first time that he had felt it in a very long time; it was like the sun coming up on an empty country that was all his. He had been a little bored. He had been bored, he now understood, for fifty years.

“The boy knew,” said the Dark Lord softly.

He had got up. He was at the window, with his back to the room. Something long and heavy moved over his foot and went on across the carpet. There was the lawn, and the black yews, and a thin line of smoke going up from a cottage at the end of the path. He laid one long finger against the cold glass.

*He knew.*

*The Chamber. The basilisk. The crown, on the seventh floor. Not because someone told him. He has been walking ahead of me all this time, this child. He looked at Peter as I would look at Peter. How does a boy of thirteen come by a look like that?*

*What have you got in there, Harry Potter?*

*Whatever it is, I don’t think that we’ll break it, Bella. I think we shall want it in one piece.*

He smiled. He had no lips to do it with. Harry felt it happen, on the front of his own skull.

He woke with his hand over his mouth.

It was a quarter past three. The rain had stopped. There were the hangings, and the dormitory, and Ron turning over and saying something about the Chudley Cannons. His scar was splitting. It was a dull steady sawing pain, right through to the back of his eyes; and he lay and looked at the dark, and shook, and waited for it to go. Nobody was coming. He knew that. He had a thing that he was supposed to be doing. *Give me something dull.*

He made an effort. He tried to think of a room. There was a table in the other life, he supposed, with Aunt Petunia’s doilies on it. You could put in a Herbology essay, and some boots. He got as far as a wall. It was cold, and it took a lot of doing, and it did not feel as if it had anyone in it.

There was nothing to put in the middle of the floor. He looked for a long time. He only had the one good thing. It was big and sagging and it had no particular colour; and he knew what Snape would say. He knew exactly what would happen to anything that he left in there, with the door open, for those long white fingers to pick up.

He took it out again. He carried it, in the dark, as far back as he could get, and he stood in front of it with his arms spread out, and he did not go to sleep.
