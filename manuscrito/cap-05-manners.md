# Chapter 5: Manners

There were a great many people Lyra Malfoy looked down on, but if she had been asked to pick only one, the name at the top of the list would have been Rubeus Hagrid.

She had a whole system for it. People without breeding she merely disliked. People who stopped trying and settled for what they had she despised. And people who had fallen behind and then gone on living off the kindness of others, as though that were a perfectly respectable way to get through life, she came closer to hating than she generally bothered to hate anybody. Hagrid managed all three without apparent effort. He was the size of a small shed, he dressed like a haystack that had been out in the rain, he had never finished his own schooling, and he had spent the fifty years since living in a hut at the bottom of the grounds on whatever Dumbledore saw fit to give him.

And now he was a teacher.

Lyra had nothing against connections. Connections were the whole point. But a connection only opened the door; it was up to you to walk through it in a manner that did the person who had opened it some credit. If you could not, then every week you stood in that doorway cost them a little more, until the day somebody cleverer came along and pointed out how much. Influence was something you spent. People who had never had any always seemed to think it grew back on its own.

She had decided, walking down the lawn to Hagrid’s hut that Friday afternoon, that this would be his last chance. If a man who loved animals as much as Hagrid apparently did could not even manage to teach Care of Magical Creatures, then what exactly was he for?

“Right, then!” Hagrid boomed, beaming down at the two Houses gathered by the paddock fence, Slytherins on one side, Gryffindors on the other. “First thing yeh want ter do is open yer books.”

“How?” said Lyra.

She held hers up. It was bound shut with two belts and a length of her Spellotape and was still grinding its pages at her through the gaps.

“I tried to read it on the train for something to do, and it very nearly had my finger off. I don’t think it’s at all responsible to make students buy a book like that without so much as a warning on the cover.”

Hagrid looked crestfallen. “Yeh’ve just got ter stroke ’em,” he said, and took the book out of Neville Longbottom’s hands, unfastened it, and ran one huge finger down its spine. The book shivered all over, then fell open in his palm and lay there quietly.

“Oh, you have to *stroke* it,” said Lyra. “How terribly amusing.”

“I— well, I thought they was funny,” said Hagrid uncertainly.

“I was being sarcastic, Professor. Do you enjoy watching your students get bitten by their homework? I’d have thought practical jokes on other people were something one grew out of when one left school. Oh, unless…” She let her eyes travel over him, slowly. “Unless one never quite…”

She had the rest of it ready, smooth and cold and sharp enough to stay with him for a week. She did not get to say it.

“Shh, Malfoy.” Potter had wandered over from the Gryffindor side and was holding up his own copy of the book, which was open and perfectly placid. “Are you sulking because you couldn’t work out how to read it?”

“Sulking?”

It was a fair complaint about a ridiculous book. Every Slytherin in the year had had trouble with it, and from the plasters on the Gryffindors’ fingers they had fared no better. If Potter meant to take Hagrid’s side simply because they were friends, he was only going to make her angrier. A boy’s reputation was a small thing to spend, and Potter did not have nearly enough of it to buy Hagrid out of trouble.

She drew breath, and Potter opened his book wide under her nose.

“I worked out how to open it ages ago,” he said, “so I’ve already read the first chapter. And the preface. Edwardus Lima, the bloke who wrote it, says he put the biting charm on deliberately. Magical creatures are alive, see, and you have to approach them gently or you get nowhere, so he made the book teach you that before it teaches you anything else.” He shut it with a loud clap. “So opening it’s the first test, really. Isn’t it?”

Lyra made a small sound that she would not have liked to hear described.

Hagrid had certainly not chosen that book for any such reason. Hagrid had chosen it because he thought it was funny; he had more or less said so. But Potter had worked out how to open it and she had not, and so long as that was true, nothing she said about it would sound like anything but sour grapes. She had shut the wretched thing in her trunk with two belts on it and never once thought of touching it. She had lost, in front of both Houses, before the lesson had properly begun.

She turned her glare from Potter to Hagrid, who was hovering anxiously in the hope of somebody telling him what to do.

“Well? What are you waiting for?” she said. “Get on with the lesson. *Professor* Hagrid.”

“Right! Yes! You lot wait here, I’ll go an’ fetch ’em!”

Hagrid gave Potter an enormous thumbs-up and strode off into the trees. Lyra unfastened the belts on her book with some dignity and ran a finger, experimentally, down its spine. The book growled, shivered, and then went limp in her hands with an air of complete surrender, as if it had been waiting all summer for somebody to think of it. She sniffed and flicked through to the front.

The preface was two paragraphs long. The first thanked the author’s mother. The second was entirely about the publisher’s liability for lost fingers.

Lyra looked up from it, very slowly, at the back of Potter’s untidy head. She could hardly accuse him of lying about a preface now, after the fact, without sounding even more like a bad loser. He had known that. He had probably known it before he opened his mouth.

As if he had felt her looking, Potter turned round and wandered back over, hands in his pockets.

“What’s the matter with your face? You’ve got your lip stuck out a mile.” He considered her. “Want to kiss something?”

Lyra actually stumbled. It was only half a step, on perfectly level grass, and she recovered from it at once, but she would be furious about it for days.

“K-kiss? Don’t be revolting!”

“Why not? Bet if you’d given it a kiss it would’ve opened right up for you.”

“As if one kiss would make any difference!”

“To the *book*, Malfoy,” said Potter innocently. “What did you think I was talking about?”

“You—”

She never found out what she had been going to call him, because Panther Parkinson chose that moment to arrive between them like a wardrobe being moved into a small room.

“Don’t talk to her, Potter.” He folded his arms, which made him even wider. “Coward.”

“Ooh.” Potter looked delighted. “That’s a new one.”

“You fainted on the train. Everyone knows. Took one look at a Dementor and keeled over like a girl.”

The story of Potter collapsing on the Hogwarts Express had gone round the entire castle in two days, and nobody had enjoyed it more than the Slytherins, who had spent the previous two years on the receiving end of Potter’s idea of fair play. They had decided, almost as one, that they had finally found his weak spot. Lyra did not agree with them, exactly. She had been there, and whatever had happened to Potter on that floor, it had not looked like cowardice. But she did not stop them, either. He had fainted. That much was simply true.

“Amazing you managed to open the book, then, a coward like you,” said Parkinson, warming up. “How long did you have to work up to it?”

“Wow,” said Potter. “It got that far, did it? Have any of the other stories got round? Like what happened just before the Dementor could get its—”

Lyra looked at him.

She put everything into it. Every ounce of her upbringing, every portrait on every landing of Malfoy Manor, and a perfectly sincere promise that if he finished that sentence she would find a way to kill him and make it look like a Quidditch accident. Potter met the look, held it for a second, and grinned, and Lyra was certain, absolutely certain, that he was going to say it anyway.

Then he turned back to Parkinson.

“No, it’s just that I scare easily around really ugly things,” he said. “Which is why you’re honestly a lot worse than the Dementor, Parkinson. I’ve seen under one of their hoods, and it’s an improvement.”

“What?”

Lyra sighed. It had always been this way. Whatever the Slytherins came up with, however inventive, Potter could always come up with something worse, faster, and say it in a more carrying voice. Parkinson had already gone the colour of a beetroot, and Potter showed no sign of stopping until it burst.

“And could you not breathe in my direction? It’s like standing next to a drain. Honestly, I’d rather have the Dementor. Look, even Malfoy’s making a face.”

Lyra was making a face, but not for the reason Potter meant.

“You little—”

Parkinson swung at him. It was a big, slow, honest punch, and Potter had plainly seen it coming from the moment it was a thought. He stepped neatly inside it and, almost in the same movement, clapped his open Monster Book against Parkinson’s forearm. The book snapped shut on it.

“AAAARGH! My arm! It’s got my *arm*!”

Hagrid came thundering out of the trees at a run. “What’s goin’ on? What’s happened?”

“The book! It’s eating my arm!”

“It can’t be,” said Hagrid, baffled. “Once yeh’ve opened one o’ them books, all the teeth go soft.”

Parkinson stopped screaming and looked down. The Monster Book was certainly clamped round his forearm, and was chewing at it with every sign of enthusiasm, but it had left no marks whatsoever. It had not so much as creased his sleeve. He took hold of it with his other hand, and it let go with a small, contented sigh.

His face went from beetroot to something darker.

“Potter…!”

“Yep, that’s me. Thanks for the reminder, I’d nearly forgotten,” said Potter. “Cheers, Gorilla.”

Even Parkinson could not hit somebody in front of a teacher. He stood there and burned, and Potter strolled away from him towards Hagrid as though nothing had happened.

Hagrid had brought a dozen creatures out of the trees on long chains, and for a moment even Lyra forgot to be scornful. They had the heads, wings and front legs of enormous eagles, steel-grey and bronze and storm-blue, and the bodies and hind legs of horses, and their eyes were a fierce, flat orange that seemed to take in everything and approve of none of it.

“Those are hippogriffs, aren’t they, Professor?” said Potter.

“They are!” Hagrid looked as though he might burst with pride. “Beauties, aren’t they?”

“I’ve read up on how you’re supposed to approach them. Can I show everyone?”

“Would yeh, Harry? Good lad!”

Potter walked straight out into the paddock towards a great grey hippogriff at the end of the line, stopped a few yards from it, and waited for it to look at him. When it did, he held its eye without blinking and swept it a long, low, beautiful bow, the kind Lyra’s dancing master would have made her practise for a week.

“Hello,” he said. “That’s a very handsome beak.”

The hippogriff considered Potter for a long moment, and then, slowly, it bent its scaly front knees and sank into a bow of its own.

Potter walked up to it as easily as if he had been doing it all his life and stroked its neck. The hippogriff closed its eyes and leaned into his hand like a cat. Lyra watched this with considerable irritation. She had been hoping, quietly, that it would take his fingers off. Clearly the creatures had no judgement of character whatsoever.

“Mind if I get on? Thanks.”

He swung himself up onto its back behind the wings, and the hippogriff gave one great cry and launched itself into the air.

Lyra had seen Potter fly a hundred times, mostly from the wrong end of a Quidditch pitch, and she had never once allowed herself to enjoy it. This was different. The hippogriff beat its way up over the paddock in long, powerful strokes, and Potter sat it as though he had been born there, with his knees tucked in and one hand knotted in its feathers and his face turned up into the wind, and for a few seconds he looked like something out of the oldest kind of story, the kind with dragons in it and a rider at the end. He took it once round the paddock in a wide circle and brought it down to land with barely a jolt.

There was no hesitation in any of it. He did the whole thing as if it were obvious, as if anyone could, and Lyra was annoyed to find that, for just a moment, she had thought he looked rather splendid. Crabbe and Goyle were clapping. Lyra looked at them, and they stopped and became very interested in the fence.

*It’s only because he read the book first,* she told herself. That was the whole difference between them. He had known how and she had not, and knowledge could be learned. Once she knew the trick she would do it every bit as well.

Potter slid down, gave the hippogriff a last scratch under its beak, and leaned in towards it with his lips pursed, quite plainly about to plant a kiss right on the end of its nose.

Lyra’s eyes narrowed. *Does he go round kissing everything?* There were boys in this school who would have refused to wash their hand for a month if Lyra Malfoy had so much as let them touch it, and here was Potter handing out kisses to farm animals in the middle of a lesson. She found herself watching the gap between his mouth and the beak with fierce and quite unaccountable attention.

Potter looked up and caught her doing it.

He grinned. And then, without taking his eyes off hers, he only bumped the end of his nose very lightly against the hippogriff’s and stepped away.

Lyra felt exactly as though she had been led in a circle by a hand she had not noticed taking hers. She turned her head away so sharply that her hair whipped Goyle in the face.

“Brilliant!” roared Hagrid, applauding. “Ten points ter Gryffindor! Yeh all saw that? Hippogriffs are proud. Easily offended. Yeh never insult one, ’cause it might be the last thing yeh do. Wait for him ter move first, give him a bow, an’ if he bows back, yeh’re all right. Go on, then, all of yeh!”

Encouraged by Potter’s success, the rest of the class began to edge out into the paddock in twos and threes. Neville Longbottom’s hippogriff refused to bow at all and he had to keep running away from it. Weasley and Granger were working on a chestnut one with great caution and a good deal of whispering.

Lyra, with Crabbe and Goyle, inherited Potter’s grey.

“Buckbeak,” she read off the collar, and wrinkled her nose. “What a ridiculous name.”

She had no intention of bowing to an animal. A Malfoy did not put her head below a farm creature’s, however proud it was said to be, and she would sooner have gone without the whole lesson. On the other hand she was not going to stand there doing nothing while Potter watched. So she compromised. She took the sides of her robes in her fingers and sank into a small, correct curtsey, the kind one gave an elderly aunt one did not particularly like.

Even that stung. Buckbeak looked at her for a long time with its hard orange eyes, long enough that she began to feel the back of her neck go warm, and then it folded its front legs and bowed in return.

*Well, naturally,* thought Lyra, with a relief she did not care to examine. A creature that had let Potter on its back could hardly turn down a Malfoy.

She stepped up beside it, and found that close to, it really was a fine animal. The feathers were sleek and dense and ran from its crown down into its grey coat without a seam, and the beak was hooked and hard as slate, and the whole thing smelled of cold air and bracken. She reached out and laid her hand on its neck, and her eye went, without any permission from her, to the end of its nose. The exact spot where Potter had been about to put his mouth.

All at once she was in a filthy temper.

“Honestly,” she said to it, “simpering over Potter like somebody’s pet pony. You’re nothing but a stupid great animal after all, aren’t you?”

It happened before she had finished the sentence. There was a flash of steel-grey, so fast that she never saw it start, and something went past her arm.

She found she was sitting on the grass. She had not decided to sit down; her legs had simply stopped holding her. For a strange, suspended moment nothing hurt at all, and she looked at her right sleeve with mild surprise, at the long rip in it, and at the dark stain spreading out from the rip and down towards her wrist. Then the pain arrived, all of it at once and a whole second late, like heat from an oven door. Her arm was on fire from the elbow to the shoulder, and there was blood, a great deal of it, soaking through her robes and running hot between her fingers, and the arm would not move when she told it to.

“Ah— ah—”

The worst injury of Lyra Malfoy’s life, until that moment, had been a grazed knee at the age of five, after which her father had had the offending gravel path relaid. Nothing had prepared her for this. She could not even scream. She opened and shut her mouth, and the tears came on their own, and then the sobs, ugly, hiccupping, babyish sobs that she would have died rather than let anyone hear on any other day, and today she could not stop them. She could not think. She could only hate. She hated the animal that had done this, and herself for sitting on the ground crying in front of two whole Houses, and Hagrid for bringing it out of the forest in the first place, and every single person standing there looking at her.

Hagrid had Buckbeak by the chain and was talking to it, low and steady, the way one talks to a frightened horse, and the hippogriff, which had reared to come at her again, was slowly coming down. Somebody was shrieking. Everybody else seemed to have frozen where they stood.

“Grit your teeth.”

The voice was close to her ear and quite calm, and Lyra did as it said without thinking, because nobody else was telling her to do anything. Someone knelt in the grass beside her, pulled off a school cloak, and pressed it hard against the wound and bound it tight round her arm, so tight that she gasped through her teeth. Then whoever it was tugged the rest of the cloak up and over her head, so that it fell down around her face like a hood, and the paddock and the staring faces disappeared.

Under the cloak it was dark and close and smelled strongly of flowery Muggle washing powder. She knew that smell. Nobody could see her now. Safe in the dark, she let her face do whatever it liked, and cried.

Arms went under her knees and behind her back, and she was lifted off the grass, and then whoever it was began to run.

She never knew how long it took. Under the cloak there was only the jolting, and the rasp of someone breathing harder and harder above her, and the slap of shoes on grass and then on stone steps, and then on more stone steps, and at one point a muttered word she was fairly sure she was not supposed to hear. Whoever was carrying her stumbled once on the stairs, caught himself against the wall with his shoulder rather than let go, and kept climbing.

Then a door banged open, and Potter’s voice, so out of breath it cracked in the middle, shouted, “Madam Pomfrey! Patient! Hippogriff, it’s got her arm—”

There was a cry of horror and a rush of footsteps, and the cloak was pulled away from her face, and the hospital wing’s bright white light came pouring in.

After that everything happened very quickly and a good deal of it hurt. Madam Pomfrey cut away the sleeve of Lyra’s robes without asking, said things about Hagrid under her breath that she would certainly never have said in front of a student on any other day, and cleaned the wound with something that stung so badly Lyra bit her lower lip until it bled. Then came a thick, cold, foul-smelling paste, and a bandage wound round and round her arm from elbow to shoulder, and a potion for the pain that tasted of old pennies. The bleeding stopped. The burning faded to a dull, deep, throbbing ache. Then Madam Pomfrey swept off to her office to send for Professor McGonagall, with strict instructions that Lyra was not to move.

The crying did not stop.

Lyra sat on the edge of the hospital bed with her bandaged arm in her lap and tears pouring down her face and hiccupped. She could not seem to do anything about it. It was as though some door had been left open inside her and everything was falling out of it.

“That— that *animal*— I’ll have it— I’ll have it killed! A filthy, stupid beast, and it dared— it *dared* lay a claw on me! I’ve never— I’ve never bled like this in my whole life—”

“All right,” said Potter. He was standing by the bed with his hair stuck to his forehead and his shirt smeared red down the front, still breathing hard. “Calm down a bit. Here, wipe your face.”

“Calm down? *Calm down?*”

Potter shut his mouth very quickly.

Lyra scrubbed her face with the handkerchief he had given her and smeared tears and dust and somebody’s blood across both cheeks. “I’m going to write to my father,” she said thickly. “And he’ll have it executed. That’s the law. Any magical creature that attacks a witch or wizard gets put down.”

“That’s a bit much, isn’t it?” said Potter. “Executing it. Over a cut.”

“Oh, so that beast matters more than my *arm*, does it?”

“No. That’s not— no.”

He did not seem to have anything else to say. For once in his life, Potter had run out of clever answers. He gave a small sigh, picked a cloth off Madam Pomfrey’s trolley, wetted it in the basin, and wrung it out.

“Right,” he muttered, half to himself. “Let’s just get you fixed up first. You can decide what to do about it when you’ve got your head back.”

He took her hand, the one on the end of the bandaged arm, and began to wipe the dried blood off her fingers, very carefully, one at a time.

Lyra let him. She did not say anything. She sat with her face still wet and watched the cloth go round each knuckle, and listened to the sound of the basin, and to Potter breathing more slowly now, and to the ache in her arm going on and on underneath it all.

It occurred to her, through the ache and the tears and the smell of Muggle washing powder that still clung to her hair, that this was not the first time Potter had caught her when she fell. She had spent the best part of a year making sure nobody remembered the first time, herself included. She put the thought away at once, in the same drawer as everything else, before it could finish itself.
