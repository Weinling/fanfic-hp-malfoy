# Chapter 4: Riddikulus

Lyra spent the start-of-term feast not looking at the Gryffindor table, which was harder than it sounded, because the Gryffindor table was directly across the hall and everyone else at the Slytherin table was looking at nothing else.

The story had come up from the carriages ahead of them: Potter had fainted on the train. By the time the first-years had been Sorted it had grown in the retelling, the way stories did in Slytherin, until Potter had screamed for his mummy, wet himself and been carried off at Hogsmeade on a stretcher.

Most of the growing had been done by Panther Parkinson, who had the shoulders of a Beater and roughly the conversational range of one, and who had been following Lyra about since first year with the persistence of a moth that has found a particularly good lamp. He was now doing an impression of Potter keeling over, with sound effects, for the benefit of the fourth-years.

Lyra neither joined in nor told him to stop. She cut her roast potatoes into smaller and smaller pieces and did not look at the Gryffindor table, where Potter had come in late with Granger, straight from McGonagall’s office, and was now eating his dinner with every appearance of not caring what anyone said. Lyra knew this because she was not looking.

“Malfoy,” said Crabbe, beside her, “your face has gone all red.”

“It’s the Dementors. They leave one feverish.”

Crabbe accepted this, as she accepted most things Lyra told her. Lyra had not said a word to her or to Goyle about what had happened after Lupin’s *Enervate*, and she had no intention of ever saying one.

There was nothing to say. It had lasted less than a second. The only witness was a teacher, and she had scrubbed her mouth with the back of her hand four times in the carriage on the way up to the castle, and as far as she was concerned that was the end of it.

At one point she was almost sure that Potter looked across the hall at her. She was not looking, so she could not be certain.

When the plates had cleared themselves, Dumbledore rose. He welcomed them back in his usual way, as if the whole school were one enormous and slightly disappointing grandchild, and then his voice changed.

The Dementors of Azkaban, he told them, were stationed at every entrance to the grounds, at the Ministry’s request, and would remain there until further notice. Nobody was to leave the school without permission. The Dementors could not be fooled by tricks or disguises, and it was not in their nature to understand pleading or excuses. He would ask the prefects, and the new Head Boy and Girl, to make sure no student gave them cause to do anyone harm.

It was all perfectly sensible, and Lyra had heard Dumbledore say a great many sensible things without believing any of them. But there was something in his voice when he said the word *Dementors* that she had never heard in it before, something cold and flat and very tired, and for a moment the old man looked less like a headmaster than like someone describing a neighbour he had long ago stopped speaking to.

Dumbledore gave away almost nothing he did not mean to. Lyra kept hold of the moment and wondered what the Dementors had ever done to him.

Then, more cheerfully, came the new staff. Professor Lupin, who had kindly agreed to take the post of Defence Against the Dark Arts teacher, stood up in the same patched robes he had worn on the train, to scattered applause that came mostly from the Gryffindors who had shared his compartment.

Lyra noticed that Professor Snape was not applauding. He was looking at Lupin down the length of the staff table with an expression she had only ever seen him turn on Potter, and she filed that away as well. Then Hagrid, the gamekeeper, was named the new Care of Magical Creatures teacher, and the Gryffindor table erupted.

Lyra clapped three times, slowly, for form’s sake.

It was quite a collection: a giant who could not keep his own boots clean, now let loose on a subject with teeth, and a Defence teacher who looked as if he had been dressed out of a charity box. Slytherin did not have Hagrid until the following Friday, with the Gryffindors, and Lyra was already composing what she would say to him.

Professor Trelawney, at least, was not new, merely invisible. She had been prophesying doom from the top of the North Tower for years without Lyra ever having to notice her, and now that Lyra had taken Divination as well as Arithmancy, on the theory that one of the two ought to be easy, she supposed she would have to start.

If it had not been for what Lupin had done on the train, she would have written off all three. As it was, she was prepared to reserve judgement on one of them, for about a week.

The week lasted until Thursday afternoon, and by twenty past two on Thursday Panther Parkinson’s worst fear was sitting on the staff-room carpet, tied up in a bow.

It had been a giant octopus, grey and glistening, coiling its way out of the wardrobe towards his shoes, and Parkinson had made a noise like a kettle and said “*R-Riddikulus!*”, and the tentacles had tangled, and knotted, and tied themselves into an enormous floppy bow. Now the octopus sat there trussed like a birthday present, blinking, and the class was laughing, and Parkinson was telling everyone he had known it would be the octopus.

Lyra, at the back of the line, was still reserving judgement.

They had gone up to Defence in a loud, cheerful herd, most of them in the mood to be entertained. “That’s the poor one, isn’t it?” somebody had said, and Parkinson had said he doubted the man could afford a wand, let alone new robes, and one of the Warrington cousins had said Dumbledore collected strays and this one had plainly come with fleas. Ordinarily Lyra would have added something to finish it off. She had walked along without a word. She had sat four doors from a Dementor not a week ago and seen the silver flare in the corridor when this man sent it on its way; whatever else he could not afford, he could afford that. Besides, after Quirrell and Lockhart, the bar was lying on the floor.

Professor Lupin had met them at the classroom door with a battered briefcase, said “Wands, and nothing else,” and walked them straight back out again, round two corners in a bewildered crocodile and into the staff room, where a tall wardrobe stood on its own at the far end, banging against the wall.

“A boggart,” Lyra had said to Crabbe, not troubling to lower her voice, because Hufflepuffs and Gryffindors put their hands up and Malfoys merely happened to know. “It lives in dark places, wardrobes, under beds, the backs of desks, and it turns into whatever frightens you most. I should think everybody knows that.”

“Exactly right, Miss Malfoy,” Lupin had said, without turning round, and gone on from there as though she had been reading out his notes for him. A boggart facing several people at once could not decide what to become; he had once seen one try to be half a headless corpse and half a giant slug at the same time, and the result had been more pitiful than frightening. On one’s own, however, the answer was the charm *Riddikulus*, and the trick of it was laughter. Picture the thing you feared most. Then picture it made absurd.

Greer Goyle was next. The boggart spun, and became a fairy, a small and spiteful one with sharp teeth and a sting in its fingers, which rose buzzing into the air and came straight at her face. Goyle yelped and jabbed with her wand, and its wings turned to tissue paper. The fairy went spinning down like a sycamore seed and rolled away under a chair, swearing in a tiny voice.

Then it was Vanessa Crabbe’s turn, and the boggart spun again and became Crabbe herself.

It was the Crabbe of two years ago, the Crabbe who had sat next to Lyra at the Sorting: heavier than she was now, round-faced, in first-year robes that pulled at the buttons, with her hair in the two plaits her mother had made her wear until the end of that year. It stood there and looked at her. It did not even do anything. It did not need to.

The room went quiet, and then somebody at the back of the line began to snigger, and Lyra turned her head and looked at him until he stopped.

Crabbe had gone white. Her wand arm had dropped to her side, and she was staring at the boggart with an expression Lyra had never seen on her face, as though the last two years had been a dream and she had just woken up from it.

“Back you come, Vanessa!” said Lupin briskly, and moved to draw her away.

Lyra had known that girl. She had shared a Chocolate Frog with her on the train in their first week and never once thought her anything to be ashamed of, and she found now that she was furious. She was not angry with Crabbe in the slightest. She was angry with the thing in front of the wardrobe, for daring to pick her out in front of everybody and use her like that.

So while Lupin steered Crabbe back into the line, Lyra stepped past them both, to give the boggart something else to look at.

She took her place in front of the wardrobe and raised her wand.

She had no idea what would come out, and she was rather curious. Lyra Malfoy was not afraid of anything. She had thought this through quite carefully over the years, usually in the bath, and had never found a single exception. Whatever the boggart turned into, it would find nothing in her to grip, and she would deal with it in front of the whole class without a hair out of place. She was almost looking forward to it.

The heavy, first-year Crabbe turned once in the air, like a coat on a hook, and became a wizard in a long dark hooded cloak.

Only that: a tall, thin figure, the hood drawn so far forward that she could see nothing of the face inside it, standing quite still in front of the wardrobe. Lyra looked at it and felt mostly puzzled. She had never seen anybody like it in her life, not in a book, not in a nightmare.

It was so vague, so entirely generic, that she supposed it was what a boggart produced when it rummaged through somebody and came up empty-handed, and she was faintly flattered.

The only difficulty was what to turn it into. A cloak was not very funny. She stood there with her wand raised, considering. A lampshade, perhaps. A tea cosy, with the hood for a spout. That would do. She lifted her wand a little higher.

The hood tipped back, only as far as it needed to for the person standing directly in front of it, and she saw the face.

It was white, whiter than any living face ought to be, and perfectly still, and it was smiling at her. It was the smile of someone looking at an insect he had not yet decided whether to bother stepping on, cold and patient and quite without hurry, and under the shadow of the hood, round the eyes and the shape of the mouth, it looked like Potter.

Lyra did not move. She meant to. She could feel the wand in her fingers, and the word was right there behind her teeth, and none of it would go anywhere. The staff room seemed to have got very quiet and very far away, and there was only that face, and the smile, and the eyes behind it.

“Stand aside, Miss Malfoy!”

Lupin was in front of her. The boggart swung round to him, and for an instant it was something else entirely, a silvery-white orb hanging in the air like the moon seen through cloud, and then Lupin said “*Riddikulus*” almost lazily and the orb went soft, like a balloon with the knot undone, and shot round and round the ceiling making a long, rude noise, and the class cheered.

Under cover of the noise, Lupin drove the thing back into the wardrobe with three brisk flicks of his wand and shut the door on it.

“Splendid. That’ll do for today. Five points apiece to anybody who stood in front of that wardrobe.”

Lyra looked at her wand hand. It was not shaking, at least.

She had not moved. She, who had stood her ground against a Dementor barely a week ago without so much as a tremor anyone could see, had stood in front of a boggart in a cloak and not been able to lift her wand an inch.

And it had been Potter. Or something with his face. She turned that over and could find no sense in it.

Was it the diary? Was it the blackmail? Potter had threatened her, certainly. She had agreed to help him, certainly. But that was a business arrangement, nothing more, and the idea that the eldest child of the House of Malfoy could be frozen to the spot by a business arrangement was simply absurd.

Behind her the class was already chattering about octopuses and fairies. From the back of the line, as Parkinson would explain at length over dinner, the boggart had only been some bloke in a cloak, and Malfoy had been about to sort it out when Lupin jumped in to show off. Lyra decided she would let that version stand.

“Right, everyone. A very good lesson. A foot of parchment on boggarts, please, for Monday. Off you go.”

The others filed out, chattering about octopuses. Crabbe and Goyle hung back by the door, plainly meaning to wait, and Lyra waved them on. On her way past, Crabbe caught the edge of Lyra’s sleeve between two fingers and held it for a moment, without saying anything, and let go. Lyra pretended not to have noticed. It seemed kinder for both of them.

When the door had shut, Lupin looked up from his briefcase and found her still there.

“I’d rather not have those five points, Professor,” said Lyra. “I didn’t earn them.”

“I’d say you earned them before it ever turned round,” said Lupin mildly. “Vanessa wasn’t standing in front of that wardrobe on her own for very long, was she?”

It was a kind answer and she disliked it intensely.

“I should like another go,” she said. “Now. Before anybody else gets a turn at it.”

Lupin looked at her for a moment over the briefcase. Then he set it down and went over to the wardrobe.

“I was going to suggest it,” he said. “I’d like to see it beaten before I let you leave.”

“It won’t take a minute.”

And it was nothing, really, now that she thought about it properly. That was a nursery sort of fear, a thing in a cloak; the kind that lived under beds and went away when somebody lit a lamp. She had been startled the first time, that was all. Now she knew exactly what was in there, and there was nothing to be startled by.

She smoothed her robes and took up her position with her wand raised.

“Ready? Then… now.”

Lupin turned the key. The hooded figure stepped out, and under the hood Potter’s face turned towards her and smiled that cold, unhurried smile.

“*Riddikulus!*”

The heavy cloak vanished. So did the glasses, which spun off across the room and disappeared. In their place Potter was wearing full evening dress: a black tailcoat with silver buttons, a waistcoat, a stiff white shirt and a bow tie, like a guest at the sort of ball Lyra’s mother gave at Christmas. It did not suit him in the least. His hair was as hopeless as ever above it.

Lyra lowered her wand and sat down in the nearest armchair with great satisfaction.

“Well. One could almost take you somewhere— *oh!*”

The boggart in the tailcoat had stepped smartly forward, bowed low over her hand, and kissed the back of it.

Lyra snatched it away as if the boggart had bitten her. Potter, or the thing wearing Potter, looked faintly wounded, bowed again even lower, recovered her hand with great courtesy and kissed it once more, with tremendous seriousness, as though the first had only been a draft.

Lyra understood, with horror, what she had done. She had put the thing in evening dress, and it had taken the evening dress at its word. The train came back to her all at once, in vivid and unwelcome detail, and her face went hot to the roots of her hair.

“*Riddikulus!*”

The tailcoat became a butler’s black jacket and striped trousers, and the boggart, which now had a napkin folded over one arm, bowed so deeply its nose nearly touched the floorboards. Lyra let out a long breath. A servant: a Potter who knew his place. Much more like it.

The butler straightened, whisked a silver tray out of nowhere and set it down at her elbow. On it were a teapot, a cup, a plate of thinly sliced cake and a small silver bell, presumably in case she should want anything further. He poured her a cup of tea. He added milk without asking how she took it, and got it exactly right, which was somehow the most annoying part.

Then he took the napkin off his arm and, before she could stop him, tucked it into the neck of her robes, cut a neat square of cake, held it up on a fork in front of her face and waited, attentively, for her to open her mouth.

“No,” said Lyra. “No, I don’t— put that *down*—”

The butler, with an air of infinite patience, produced a little ivory fan and began to fan her with one hand while holding up the cake with the other. When she leaned away, he followed. When she ducked to one side, he was already there, wearing the earnest, devoted face of somebody whose only purpose in life was to see her fed.

Lupin, by the wardrobe, made no move whatever to help. He seemed to be enjoying himself.

So Lyra stood up, which brought the butler’s face and the fork and the cake very close together, and said it a third time, through her teeth.

“*Riddikulus.*”

The butler took one devoted step after her, caught his foot on nothing whatever, and went headlong. The tray went up. The teapot went up. The cake went up and came down again, cream side first, squarely on top of his head, and he sat there on the floorboards with jam running down his nose and an expression of such profound and Potter-like injury that Lyra laughed out loud before she could stop herself.

The boggart burst like a soap bubble into a thin grey smoke and went back through the keyhole. Lyra kicked the wardrobe door shut after it.

There was a short silence.

“So it isn’t Harry,” said Lupin, from beside the wardrobe. “It’s Harry with the upper hand. Put him in a waistcoat and give him a tray, and you’re not frightened in the least. Only rather embarrassed.” He tilted his head. “Has he ever given you a really bad fright, at some point?”

“Professor,” said Lyra, “if you have *quite* finished with my psychology—”

“I’ll admit it’s a weight off my mind,” said Lupin, in the voice people use to settle a nervous horse. “I had been asking myself whether Harry ought to sit somewhere else in future. But manners can be mended. That’s his job, not your fear.”

He pulled two of the shabby armchairs round to face each other and sat down in one, and Lyra, because it would have looked like running away not to, sat down in the other as though she had been the one to offer it. The napkin was still in her collar. She did not notice it until much later.

“The face under the hood,” he said. “The first time. It was Harry’s.”

It was not a question, so she did not treat it as one. “It was. I’m not frightened of Potter, Professor. I’m *bored* of him. There’s a difference.”

It was quite true, as far as it went. A girl who was frightened of somebody did not go down a pitch-dark train towards the sound of him screaming, and she had. The diary made him awkward, and she disliked him more than anyone else in the school, but she had never once been frightened of him.

“It must have been a defective boggart,” she said.

“Possibly. Harry’s never owned a cloak that long in his life; his robes are halfway up his shins.” He paused. “But I’ll be honest with you, Miss Malfoy, and I hope you won’t take it the wrong way. When I saw it, before it turned towards me, it didn’t put me in mind of Harry at all. It put me in mind of the man people still won’t name.”

Lyra had been ready for almost anything but that, and least of all now, sitting there with cake on her shoe. She felt herself go still, the way her father went still at dinner when somebody mentioned the war.

There was exactly one person, alive or not quite alive, who could walk into the Manor and help himself to whatever he liked: the house, the name, her father, her own mind if he cared to, and she had heard enough over the years, in half-sentences and lowered voices, to know he had helped himself to other people’s. Potter too, for all Potter seemed to think himself untouchable.

She supposed a reasonable person might be afraid of that. She supposed it might even be the sensible thing to be afraid of.

“Of a man who disappeared while I was still in a cot?” she said. “I couldn’t pick him out of a crowd.”

“Which may be the point. You can’t be frightened of a face you’ve never seen, so it went and fetched one. And after last year, I can see where it might have looked first.”

Last year. The Chamber, and the writing on the wall, and Potter hissing at a snake in the Duelling Club in front of half the school. Half of Hogwarts had decided he was the Heir of Slytherin, Lyra included, for about a week, until Potter made his famous announcement about kneeling and the whole business collapsed into farce.

And then, at the end of the year, in a cold classroom with the moon in the window, she had learned what had really been going on.

“Has Harry ever said anything to you,” said Lupin gently, “about You-Know-Who?”

She knew a good deal more than he did, she suspected. She knew about the diary.

And she had worked out the rest for herself, sitting on her window seat in August with the light on in the east cottage: one piece of seven gone, which left six, hidden somewhere.

A boy who knew that number, and who had asked her about her family’s houses, including the one up north that no register in the country had ever recorded, was not asking out of idle curiosity.

Potter was looking for the others.

She was nearly sure of it. She had not told a soul. And she was certainly not going to start with a man in patched robes whom she had known for a little over a week, who knew her name without being told it and who would, for all she knew, be in Dumbledore’s office within the hour repeating every word.

Whatever she knew, it was the only thing standing between her family and the worst kind of trouble. She was not going to hand it to anybody she could not be sure of.

“Not a word,” she said, and folded her hands in her lap so they would have nothing to do. “I expect it’s just as you say. I find him so tiresome that it gave the thing ideas.”

“Do you?” said Lupin. He looked at her for a moment, without any particular expression, and then nodded as though she had confirmed something, though she could not have said what. His eyes had gone back to the wardrobe door, and Lyra could have sworn that he had not finished thinking about it at all.

Lyra came out of the staff room with her robes in disarray, a napkin still tucked into her collar and a face that could have lit a fire, and stalked off down the corridor towards Arithmancy without looking at anybody.

This was Potter’s doing. All of it. The boggart, the butler, the cake, the train. She could not have said how, precisely, but she was entirely certain of it, and she put it on his account.

It was only on the stairs, when her face had cooled and her heart had slowed down, that the first face came back to her, the one under the hood. She had seen Potter angry. She had seen him smug and bored and spiteful and, once, on the floor of a train, frightened. She had never once seen him smile like that, cold and patient and quite without hurry, as though he had all the time in the world and already knew how everything would end.

She hoped very much that she never would.
