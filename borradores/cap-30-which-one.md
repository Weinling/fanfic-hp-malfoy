# Chapter 30: Which One

By breakfast on Thursday, Crabbe had asked three times.

She had asked in the dormitory, with one stocking on, and on the stairs, and in the common room while Lyra was putting the last pin in her hair; and now she was asking over the porridge, in the low, reasonable voice she kept for questions she meant to go on asking until somebody answered them.

“You do know, though. Don’t you.”

The whole of the Great Hall wanted to know. The school had been up since six in the giddy, unsettled way of a school that has been told something tremendous will happen after lunch and nothing else, and every table was guessing. Lyra had heard sphinxes, and a maze, and a Hufflepuff whose cousin was quite certain it would be Inferi.

She considered. Krum would know by now; Karkaroff was hardly going to keep it from his own champion. The Delacour girl would know. The task was in five hours, and one more person knowing was not going to make a dragon any hotter.

She touched her napkin to her mouth, and behind it said, very low, “Dragons.”

Crabbe’s face went through astonishment and arrived at delight. “*Really?*”

“Hush. It’s a secret. I can’t explain how I know.”

“Dragons,” breathed Crabbe. Then her face changed again. “But—won’t the champions get hurt?”

Lyra had spent four days not thinking about that, with some success. She thought about it now. A dragon’s hide turned spells the way a roof turned rain. It took half a dozen grown wizards to bring one down, and you could go for the eyes, the library said, if you wanted it angrier. And the Horntail had a tail. She had looked at the picture until Madam Pince came round with the snuffer: a tail like a mace, studded with bronze spikes, and a note underneath in small print about its reach.

The toast had gone cold in her fingers. She put it down.

“Dumbledore will have arranged something,” said Crabbe, rather quickly. “Won’t he? He’d never just—”

“He had better have.” Lyra heard her own voice go up, and did not bring it down. “If he means to stand fourteen-year-olds in front of— If there aren’t wizards on hand to stop it, I shall write to the Board myself. I shall write to the *Minister*. A man who calls himself the greatest wizard of the age, and can’t keep one boy out of—”

She stopped.

Crabbe was looking at her with an expression of deep and tender pity.

“You’re not even pretending any more,” said Crabbe. “Are you?”

“Pretending what?”

Crabbe shook her head slowly and went back to her porridge, and did not explain; and Lyra was left with the uncomfortable sense of having lost an argument she had not known she was in.

Goyle arrived then, late and outraged, with a copy of the *Daily Prophet* folded open in both hands.

“Have you *seen* this? Rita Skeeter. The champions. Look what they’ve done to Professor Snape!”

It was an interview with the youngest Triwizard champion, and something had gone wrong with it between the champion’s mouth and the page. Wherever a reader might have expected the name of Harry Potter, it said instead *the sleek-haired bachelor Professor Snape*.

*The sleek-haired bachelor Professor Snape*, Lyra read, *wipes away a tear as he speaks of his parents. “I still cry about them at night,” he admits. “I’m not ashamed of it. I know they’re watching over me.”*

And further down: *Love, too, has found the sleek-haired bachelor Professor Snape at Hogwarts. His close friend Colin Creevey says that he is rarely seen without one Hermione Granger, a stunningly pretty Muggle-born girl who, like Professor Snape, is one of the top students in the school.*

Lyra breathed in through her nose, and held it.

It was the funniest thing she had ever read. But Professor Snape had marked her up for three years, and had once looked at a set of grey feathers on the front of her robes and said nothing about them, and he was sitting at the staff table at this moment with a copy of the same paper beside his plate. Lyra Malfoy did not laugh. She held her breath until her eyes watered, and folded the paper along its creases, and handed it back.

“Disgraceful,” she said. “Somebody ought to write to the editor.”

Up at the staff table, Professor McGonagall had her napkin pressed to her mouth and her shoulders were going. Professor Snape was not reading his paper. He was looking down the length of the Hall at the Gryffindor table, at Potter, with the face of a man choosing between poisons.

Somebody had Confunded that reporter’s quill. Lyra could think of exactly one person in the castle who would do such a thing to Rita Skeeter and to Professor Snape in a single stroke, and he was sitting at the Gryffindor table eating eggs with every appearance of peace.

She took the paper back off Goyle, found the paragraph about Granger, and read it again. It was nonsense. She had sat across a library table from Granger on Sunday night, with Viktor Krum two tables along gazing at the back of Granger’s head, and she knew precisely how much nonsense it was. All the same.

She looked across the Hall at Potter for some time, coldly.

Potter, who had borne the whole weight of Professor Snape’s regard without turning a hair, looked up, met her eye, and shivered.

Lessons stopped at noon. The champions would be sent for after lunch, which left lunch.

Lyra did not eat it. She stood outside the doors of the Great Hall with her back to the wall and her bag over her arm, as though she were waiting for somebody of no particular importance, and when people going in looked at her, she looked back until they stopped.

The Gryffindors came along the corridor in a crowd, the way they went everywhere, with Potter in the middle of it: Weasley talking at one ear and Granger at the other, and a second-year walking backwards in front of him asking whether he was scared.

Lyra pushed herself off the wall as they came level, put her hair back over her shoulder, and walked past him the other way, as anybody leaving the Hall might, near enough that he could not possibly miss her.

He did not look at her. He went on through the doors in the middle of the crowd, and they swung shut behind him.

Lyra stopped in the corridor.

“Very well,” she said to the doors.

Four days she had spent not thinking about Horntails. She had not asked him for a speech. She had not even asked him for a word. One look would have done, and she was not going to stand about in a corridor waiting for it like a parlourmaid hoping for a tip—

She was already walking, fast, towards the dungeon stairs, when feet came running behind her. Then Potter was beside her, out of breath, and something cool and slippery was being bundled into her arms.

“Wear this,” he said, “and come and find me.”

Then he was gone again, back through the doors of the Great Hall before she had got her mouth open, and Lyra was standing in the corridor holding a great armful of nothing, which slid about against her robes as though it would rather be somewhere else.

She looked down at it. Then she stuffed it into her bag, very quickly, before anybody came by and saw a girl standing in a corridor holding air.

Crabbe had told everybody.

By the time the school was packed into the stands round the enclosure, the word was going along the Slytherin benches like a draught, and when the first dragon was led out, roaring, at the far end, and the Hufflepuffs screamed, the whole of Slytherin House looked at it with the bored, knowing faces of an audience that has read the end of the play. Parkinson was explaining to a third-year that he had always rather expected dragons. Crabbe, beside Lyra, sat with her hands folded in her lap, looking like a girl who had never told anybody anything in her life.

The champions’ tent stood at the edge of the enclosure, a great striped thing with its flap tied back. Mad-Eye Moody stood outside it, leaning on his staff, with the blue eye going round and round.

At the judges’ table, Ludo Bagman had got to his feet with a purple megaphone and was explaining the rules: a nest, and a clutch of eggs, and among them one egg of gold. While every face in the stands was turned towards him, Lyra stood up.

Crabbe moved along the bench into the space she had left, without a word, and folded her hands again.

Under the cloak, behind the stand, the world went very quiet. Lyra walked down the slope with her heart going, and stopped a few yards from the tent, and from Moody.

The blue eye went round. Once it went past her, and on, and did not stop. Perhaps it had not seen her. Perhaps it had seen her and did not care. She did not wait to find out which. The tent flap moved, and Cedric Diggory came out of it, grey to the lips, with his wand in his hand, and she slipped in through the gap behind him before it fell shut.

It was dim inside and smelt of canvas and crushed grass. Viktor Krum was sitting hunched on a stool with his elbows on his knees, staring at the ground between his feet as though it owed him money. Fleur Delacour was walking round and round in a tight circle, very pale, with her silver hair tied back. A fat glossy beetle was blundering about against the canvas near the roof, and nobody minded it.

Potter was on a bench at the back. He was sitting quite upright, with both hands gripping the edge of the bench on either side of him, and his knuckles had gone white.

Lyra went round Fleur, carefully, and sat down beside him, and laid her hand over his.

He did not turn his head.

“You took your time,” he said, very low.

Out in the enclosure something roared, and the crowd made a noise like a wave going over, and Bagman’s voice came through the canvas: “—oh, *nasty*! Nasty, nasty—”

“Have you got a plan?” whispered Lyra.

“Yes.”

“Is it a sensible one?”

“It’s a broom.”

“You haven’t *got* a broom.”

“Ah,” said Potter.

Lyra thought of the school shed, and the Shooting Stars with their twigs every which way, and a Cleansweep with a crack in the handle mended with Spellotape, and a black dragon with a tail like a mace, and went cold to the ends of her fingers.

Outside, the crowd went up all at once in a roar, and then a long cheer. Diggory had got his egg. A whistle went. Fleur stopped walking, and stood very straight for a moment with her eyes shut, and went out.

Under Lyra’s hand, Potter’s let go of the bench. It turned over and took hold of hers instead, hard. His forehead was damp. He was not looking at her, since he could not see her, but he had turned his face very slightly towards where she was.

“It’s a dragon, Lyra,” he said.

It was only that. He said it quite quietly, the way you might say *it’s raining*, and she understood that it was the most frightened thing she had ever heard him say.

“I know what it is,” she said. “I told you what it is.”

“Yeah. Thanks for that.”

“If you get hurt—”

“You’ll kill me. I know.”

The roars went on for a long time. Then cheering again, and Bagman shouting about singed skirts, and the whistle. Krum got up off his stool without a word to anybody and went out, ducking under the flap; and then there were only the two of them in the tent, and the beetle.

Potter let out a long breath. When he spoke again it was nearer his ordinary voice.

“About this morning. The paper. I didn’t say any of that, about Hermione.”

“I know. Professor Snape did.”

He made a noise that was nearly a laugh. “I’m a model of good behaviour. You’ll see.”

“Prove it.”

“I will.” He tipped his head back against the canvas. Then he shut his eyes, and turned his face up towards where she was, quite shamelessly, and waited. “Do I get anything? For luck?”

Lyra looked at him for a moment, with his eyes shut and his face tipped up, waiting, as though there were not a dragon anywhere in Scotland.

Then she put her hand in her pocket.

“You get this,” she said, and pressed it into his palm.

He opened his eyes and looked at it. It was a sugar quill in a twist of Honeydukes paper, gone soft at the folds and grey at the edges, and a little bent in the middle where it had been sat on, once, at a Quidditch match.

“What’s this?”

“A sugar quill. You eat it afterwards. If you’ve been sensible.”

He turned it over in his fingers. “It’s bent.”

“It’s been in a pocket.”

“How long?”

“Since Hallowe’en.”

He looked down at the paper, where the Honeydukes crest had worn nearly away.

“…Which one?” said Potter.

Lyra did not answer, and he did not ask again. He looked at the sugar quill for a long time, and then he put it away, carefully, in the pocket where he kept his wand, as though it might break.

“*Harry Potter!*”

Bagman’s voice, through the megaphone, from outside.

Potter stood up. Lyra stood up with him.

She was invisible. He could not see her face; nobody in the world could see her face. That was the whole point of the cloak, and she had never in her life been so grateful to a piece of cloth. She put her hands on his arms, and stood up on her toes, and kissed him.

It lasted a second. There was the cloak between them, cool and thin, and his mouth on the other side of it; and his eyes, which she could see and which could not see hers, gone perfectly round.

Then she let go, and stepped back. She meant to go straight out.

She did not go. Potter was standing alone in the middle of the tent with nobody in front of him at all, and for a moment he did not move. Then he put the back of his hand against his mouth, hard, as though there were something there he had to keep in, and the colour came up out of his collar and went on coming, and he began to laugh. He made no sound doing it. It shook his shoulders and would not stop, and he looked at the empty air where she had been, and then a little to the left of it, and then to the right, like somebody who has no idea which way to look and no hope of finding out.

“Lyra—” he said, to nobody.

She had seen him angry, and smug, and frightened on the floor of a train. She had seen him cold enough to stop a library. She had never once seen him like this. Nobody had. She had done it in a second, with a cloak in the way, and she stood a yard off and could not move for looking.

“*Harry Potter!*”

He took his hand away from his mouth. The laugh went, mostly. The smile did not; he could not get it off his face. He picked up his wand from the bench and walked out of the tent with it still there, towards a Hungarian Horntail, grinning like a fool.

The fear came back all at once, so hard it took her breath. She had sent him out to a dragon with *that* on his face.

Lyra ran. Something fat and droning went past her ear on the way out, and she did not stop for it.

Crabbe moved along the bench without a word, and Lyra sat down in her own place with the cloak bundled into her bag on her knees. She could feel her face. Nobody looked at it. Everybody was looking at the enclosure.

The Horntail was black, and scaled like a lizard, and much larger than the picture. It crouched over its clutch of eggs with its wings half open and its yellow eyes on the boy in the middle of the grass, and its tail went to and fro behind it, scoring long furrows in the ground with every swing: a great black whip of a tail, studded down its length with bronze spikes. Among the grey eggs under its belly, one shone gold.

Potter walked out to the middle of the enclosure on his own. If he was still grinning, she was too far off to see it, and was glad.

He raised his wand.

“*Accio Firebolt!*”

His voice carried all the way round the stands. Lyra sat quite rigid. There was no Firebolt in the castle to summon except hers, in its case in the dungeons, where she had put it back on Tuesday evening with every twig brushed straight—

Something came streaking out over the edge of the Forest from the direction of the castle, low and very fast, and it was a broom, and it was a Firebolt, and it stopped dead in the air beside him as though it had been waiting all its life to be asked.

The Slytherin stand drew a single breath.

“That’s— isn’t that *Malfoy’s*?”

Parkinson had turned round on his bench to stare at her. So had half the row in front.

“Did you lend him yours?” said Goyle.

“No,” said Lyra.

*No need*, he had said. In the Great Hall, in the spring, with the whole of Slytherin round her new broom, when she had offered to let him have a go on it if he asked her very nicely. *Very kind. But no need.*

He had gone and bought one. Of all the brooms in the world, with all the money his father had left him, he had gone and bought the same one as hers.

She could not decide whether it was the stupidest thing she had ever heard of or the vainest, and the corners of her mouth, without consulting her, had begun to go up.

Potter went up too. He did not go for the dragon. He took the Firebolt round the enclosure in a long, low, lazy lap, past every stand in turn, so that everybody could have a good look at the broom, and the stands went mad. He came round past Slytherin at exactly the height of the top row, and for a second he was ten yards off and looking straight at her.

She looked back at him with the whole weight of nine hundred years of Malfoys. *Get on with it.*

He grinned, and peeled away.

What came after happened too fast for most of the school, and not nearly fast enough for Lyra. He went up high, and the Horntail rose on its hind legs to follow him with its head, and its jaws opened. He dived, and it reared, and the fire went over him by a yard. He went into the dive again, lower, and the tail came round to meet him.

She saw the spike catch his shoulder. She saw his robes tear, and something dark come out through the tear, and she was on her feet with the bag sliding off her knees, and could not afterwards remember getting up.

He did not fall. He rolled out of it, and came round again, and the Horntail spread its wings wide to come up after him and left, for one second, its belly and its eggs uncovered; and in that second Potter went in underneath, low, flat along the Firebolt, and came out the other side with the golden egg under his good arm, and was away, up over the stands, into the sky.

The stands came down. The noise was enormous: screaming, and stamping, and Bagman bellowing through his megaphone that he had never seen anything like it. In the Slytherin stand even Parkinson was on his feet.

Lyra did not clap. She had forgotten how. She stood with one hand pressed flat against her ribs and watched the dark patch on his shoulder go up into the sky with the golden egg, and it came to her, quite simply, that she did not care in the least whether he had won.

He had come back. She had told him at Hallowe’en what she would do to him if he got hurt, and he had gone and got hurt, and she did not want to kill him at all. Last Christmas, on the end of her bed at the Manor, she had sworn to be first on her knees if the Dark Lord ever came back, even if it meant turning on Potter. Standing in the Slytherin stand with her hand against her ribs, she could not have turned on anything.

By dinner the whole castle knew the marks. Potter and Krum were joint first; Delacour and Diggory were a point apart behind them, and Delacour was said to be furious about it in French.

In the Slytherin common room the argument about the Firebolt went on all evening. Parkinson held that Potter had stolen it. Montague held that Dumbledore had bought it for him out of school funds, which was worse. One of the Warrington cousins put it about that it was Lyra’s, lent for a bet, until Lyra looked at him. She let the rest of them argue. She sat in her chair by the fire with her Arithmancy open on her knee and her face perfectly composed, and did not tell any of them that somewhere above their heads, in a tower, Potter was very probably eating a sugar quill a year and three weeks old.

Then a prefect came in and pinned a new notice to the board by the door, and the whole common room got up to read it.

> *THE YULE BALL*
>
> *Christmas Day, at eight o’clock, in the Great Hall.*
>
> *Open to the fourth year and above. Younger students may attend if invited by an older student.*
>
> *Dress robes will be worn.*
>
> *The champions and their partners will open the dancing.*

Lyra read it twice.

All round her, the fourth-year girls had begun, very casually, to look about the room, and the fourth-year boys had begun to look at the floor. Somebody at the back said *dress robes* in tones of despair.

*The champions and their partners.* In the middle of the Great Hall, on Christmas night, in front of the whole school and Durmstrang and Beauxbatons and anybody from the *Daily Prophet* who cared to come: Potter, with somebody on his arm, opening the dancing. *It’s better, for now, if nobody can see it*, he had said under the cloister in May. *Officially.*

She was still looking at the last line when, behind her, Panther Parkinson cleared his throat.
