# Chapter 88: Still Bored

The voice came on the Saturday night, at about one o’clock, while he was awake.

That was what he couldn’t get over, afterwards. There had always been a dream: a corridor, or a tall man on some steps; you got a bit of warning, and you could tell yourself you had been asleep. He was lying on his back in the dark with his glasses on, looking at the top of his four-poster, as he had been since about ten, while Seamus snored. A bar of moon lay on the floor, the bed next to his had its blanket pulled straight, and his right hand lay on his chest with a cold blue ring on it. He thought about a knife going along a piece of toast. He had not stopped thinking about it for fifteen hours, and he didn’t feel any better.

His forehead gave a small, polite, single throb, like someone clearing his throat.

“Good evening, Mr Potter,” it said.

He stopped breathing.

It wasn’t in his ears. It was very quiet and very close, and it seemed to come from a place about an inch inside the scar. He had heard it say *Come and see*, in a pit, with a dog lying on the stone. It was light and cultivated and rather tired, the voice of a man who has been reading late, and has put down his book for a moment, and has thought of someone.

“I hope I don’t disturb you. I’ve been meaning to say how much I enjoyed that. *Please come.* One seldom gets to see anything really well made, nowadays.”

He had got up on his elbows. He could feel his heart going, in his throat. He put up his hand, and pressed, and it made no difference. There was a door. He’d been told about that. He knew just where it was, he had been down to a dungeon every Tuesday for nine months, he had built a room with a bad table in it. He went for it. He got it shut; he stood there in the dark, in his head, with his whole body against the wood; and the voice went on, without the least alteration, from the other side.

“Oh, very nice. You’ve been having lessons. You needn’t trouble. I’m not coming in; I only want a word. I looked round once, you’ll recall, in June, for a moment. I don’t suppose it took as long as it takes to sneeze. Such a lot of furniture. It’s all very sad, and it’s so badly kept. All those people. A table with one leg. And right at the back, in the only room with a fire in it, there’s a tent.”

He couldn’t move.

“Canvas, I think. There’s a smell of crushed grass. There’s a dragon somewhere, and a hand. I must say I thought it rather touching. I hadn’t known that one could live on so little. I have been looking at that, on and off. I thought you’d like to know how the account stands.”

There was a pause. He seemed to be turning something over, by the stem.

“You sent her back to me on the eighth of January, Mr Potter, with a bag of scraps. You sat on a rug and you wrote them out. You thought that was clever. She has sat at my table every holiday since, and written out my reports. I was short of a clerk, and you lent me one. I only wanted to say how very much obliged I am.”

It went away as a person does, who has looked in at a door, and has nodded. His forehead gave one more small throb, and was quiet. Seamus turned over and muttered something about West Ham.

He lay there. He was very cold. After a while he noticed he had bitten through the inside of his cheek.

The post came in at eight, on the Sunday.

He’d gone down early, for no reason he could have given. Hermione was at the far end of the table, by herself, with a book. Ron and Ginny had sat down across from each other, about a dozen places along, and Ron was cutting up a sausage with his head bent, as you’d cut something for an invalid. It was a raw grey morning, with the windows running. The owls came in as they always came, in a long soft untidy clatter, all down the length of the Hall, and things began to drop among the toast. He didn’t look up. He got very little post.

Something hit the table in front of him.

It hit it hard. The plates jumped. There was a crack, and a spray of milk, and somebody down the bench said “Oi!” and started to laugh.

He looked at it.

It was lying in the toast rack, on its side, and it was white. There was a very great deal of white, more than you would have thought. One wing had gone out across the butter dish, and it hung there, with the long feathers fanned, and the barred tips of them had got into the marmalade. He knew those feathers. He’d have known them in the dark. Her head had turned right round on her neck, and it was resting on her back, looking at the ceiling. Her eyes were open. They were very round and very yellow, with a film on them, and they looked mildly surprised.

He didn’t do anything.

She had gone out on the Thursday night, by the dormitory window. She went hunting on Thursdays. She’d looked back at him from the sill, over her shoulder, as she did, and clicked her beak. He had been thinking about something else.

There was a bit of string round her leg.

It was a neat, small, rather clever knot. He had got quite good at that. He had done it every morning for twelve days, in June, with his tongue between his teeth, at a dormitory window. He had got so that he could do it with one hand. There was a scrap of parchment tied there, folded in four.

His fingers seemed to be a long way off. They went out across the table, by themselves, past the wet white feathers, and she was still warm. He undid it the first time, and unfolded the paper. It was very clean, good, cream-coloured paper, with two words on it and no name, in the hand of a boy who has won prizes for that.

*Still bored?*

It had gone quiet. He noticed it spreading, out from where he sat, along the bench and across to Hufflepuff. Someone had stopped laughing in the middle, and a girl somewhere said, “Oh, *no*.” He could feel them all turning, in the grey light, six hundred people who had seen him do most things. They had seen him come out of a maze. He’d come in at those doors with a split lip, and with a bandage, and with Sirius dead. Nobody had ever seen him cry at that table. He had been rather careful about that, for five and a half years, in two lives. There was a rule.

He couldn’t remember the last thing that he had said to her. He tried.

It came up from the bottom of him, and he couldn’t do anything about it at all.

It made no noise. He didn’t put his hands over his face. He just sat there, with his arms on either side of the toast rack, looking down, and it ran out from under his glasses and dropped off the end of his nose; it was going on to the feathers. His mouth had come open a little. He had been given that owl by a man with an umbrella, when he was eleven. He’d been in a cupboard, and then he’d had a white owl. She had been the first thing anybody had ever given him, and she’d been the only one that he’d had in both.

Somebody had got up.

He heard the bench, and a plate going down, and large feet coming along behind the row of backs, not fast. They stopped. He didn’t look round. He could see a bit of a sleeve, out of the corner of his eye, and the end of a big freckled hand with a graze across the knuckles, and it didn’t touch him. Ron Weasley had come up the length of the Gryffindor table, in front of the whole school, for the first time in six months, and he stood there beside him, with his arms hanging and his ears very red, facing the Hall.

He stayed there. He didn’t say a word. He looked at Seamus, and Seamus looked down. He looked at a table of Ravenclaws, and they found they’d got things to do. He stood like a man holding a door. After a while the talk started up again, in a low uneasy way, along the benches, and he went on standing.

There was a very small dry sound beside the toast rack. Hermione had come round the other way. She’d got a clean napkin, a big one. She didn’t say anything either. She unfolded that, and she laid it over the wing, and tucked it in, with her thin quick careful fingers. It took her some time, because she couldn’t see very well.

They buried her behind Hagrid’s cabin, at the edge of the pumpkin patch, in the rain.

Hermione had brought a spade from Professor Sprout, and he dug it himself, and wouldn’t let anybody do it by magic. It was hard going, in the cold black earth, with the rain getting down his neck, while she stood by with the napkin in her arms. Hagrid had come out in his great moleskin coat, with Fang pressed against his leg. He looked at the bundle, and at Harry, and his beetle eyes crinkled up and filled, and he put one vast hand on top of the fence. *Yeh said yeh’d come,* he said, without reproach. *Yeh said at the start o’ term. I’d got some rock cakes in. Oh, Harry. Oh, she was a beauty. I got her off a feller in Eeylops, she bit him, she bit him clean through his glove—* He couldn’t go on. He stood there with his shoulders heaving, and he cried for the owl, and for Harry, in great hoarse wet gasps, like a bellows; somebody had to, and Harry had stopped. He felt very clear. He laid her down and filled it in, and stood on the place, and scraped the spade on the edge of his boot.

“Harry,” said Hermione.

It was the first time she had said his name since June. She was looking at his face. She had rain on her eyelashes. He saw her look; he saw her go down the column, and stop, and a very small line came between her brows.

“Thanks,” said Harry. “That was good of you.”

He gave her the spade.

He went up to bed at ten. He cleaned his teeth. He waited until Dean had stopped reading, and Seamus had started to snore. Then he lay down on his back on the top of the blankets, in his clothes, and he took off his glasses, and put them on the locker, and folded his hands.

*You will keep that door shut*, somebody had told him, in a green underwater light, about four hundred times. *You’ll keep it shut if he comes to you singing. You will keep it shut if you’re on fire. There’s only one thing that you can do that is more stupid than leaving it open, and it’s to go and open it yourself.*

He looked at the table with the short leg. He picked it up, and moved it out of the way. He went along the passage. He took hold of the handle.

It was very easy. It had always wanted to be open.

It was dark, and very large, and it smelt of cold stone and something clean. He had no eyes. He was sitting, he thought, in an upright chair, in a long room with a shuttered window, in the middle of the night, with a fire somewhere and something heavy moving slowly across his feet. A book lay on his knee, and a long white hand lay on the page, with the nails very clean. He felt it stop. He felt the whole of that enormous cool attention lift, and turn. It was like being looked at by the sea.

“Hello, Tom,” said Harry.

He hadn’t got a voice. It didn’t need one. He found he’d been thinking about this for some time. He’d thought about it with a spade in his hand, and he knew exactly how he was going to do it.

“I got your note. I thought I’d answer. It’s only polite.”

Nothing. It was waiting; it was rather amused. He could feel that, along his arms.

“I’ll tell you what I know. I know you’re frightened. I don’t mean of me. I know what you’re frightened of. I’ve always known. You saw your mum’s ring and you saw her father, you saw that place with the iron beds, you had your first look at a dead person when you were eleven and you’ve never got over it. I’ve seen your Boggart. It’s you. It’s a corpse on the ground. It’s just you, and nobody coming. You made seven. *Seven*. Who needs seven? Abraxas Malfoy has been laughing at that for fifty years. You did it to the thing you’re proudest of, you tore it up like a Muggle tearing up a ten-bob note, and you hid the bits in a lot of old cups, because you’re so scared. You’re the most frightened person who’s ever lived. It’s all you are. You’ve got a very nice voice and it’s got nothing behind it but a boy in a blanket.”

The hand had not moved.

He could feel his own heart, a long way away, in a bed. It was going slowly. He was enjoying himself. He’d had no idea. It was the first thing that had tasted of anything since a morning in June.

“You’ve got no one. D’you know that? I’ve got a table with a short leg. I’ve got a boy who came up the Great Hall this morning and stood next to me. He doesn’t even like me. You’ve got Wormtail. Bellatrix doesn’t love you; she loves the *noise*. You’ve been dead once, and d’you know who did that? I was fifteen months old. I couldn’t walk. You came to a house in the middle of the night, with your great big wand, the greatest wizard in the history of the world; and you got beaten by a *baby*. In a *cot*. You got beaten by a nappy, Tom. You’ve been running away from that for fifteen years. And you’re still *bored*, are you?”

It was very quiet.

He had thought there would be something. He’d thought it would be rage, and he had been looking forward to that. It wasn’t. For the length of a dropped cup, nothing happened at all.

And then the voice wasn’t there.

Nothing had changed, and the fire burnt. Only the courtesy had gone. It had been lifted off, as you’d lift off a lid, a very thin lid that had been on for seventy years; and he looked down, with his whole mind, into what was underneath. It had no words, and no face. It was a white, flat, freezing, screaming *want*, that had never been anything else, looking straight up at him with its mouth open, and it went down for ever.

He had faced that man in a graveyard, in another life, and on the Astronomy Tower, and in a forest. He had stood and waited for the green. He had not been frightened as he was now. He hadn’t known that you could be. He heard somebody make a thin high noise, in a bed. He wanted his mother. He tried to let go of the handle, and his fingers wouldn’t open.

It lasted a second. It might have been less.

The lid went back on.

He felt it settle. He felt the long fingers relax, and turn over the page. Something brushed by, across his ankles, and went on. When it came, it was perfectly pleasant. It had a slight smile in it, the voice of someone who has been told the most amusing thing, and intends to have it framed.

“Well, now,” it said. “You’ll want to see what that costs.”

The door shut in his face.

He was in the Great Hall at ten past eight.

He hadn’t slept. His forehead felt as if someone had been at it with a chisel. It was a bright hard Monday, with frost on the windows. He’d got some porridge in front of him, and he was holding a spoon. Neville had sat down next to him. Neville did that, these days, without appearing to think about it, and he’d brought the pot, and was feeding it something out of a paper bag. There wasn’t any owl on the sill of the dormitory. He kept looking for that. He felt all right. He felt tired and light and rather pleased; he’d have liked to have somebody to tell. *A nappy, Tom.* There was a good clean ache behind his eyes.

He looked along the Hall, because he always did. She had come down.

She was sitting where she sat, with her back to the wall, between Goyle and a gap. Nobody had said much to her since Saturday. She had a piece of toast on her plate, and a dish of butter, and she’d picked up her knife. She was in her long sleeves, with her cuffs buttoned, and the winter sun was coming in at the high windows and lying along the table, and she looked as neat as a new pin.

His scar went white-hot.

It was so sudden that he dropped the spoon. It hurt a very great deal, but that wasn’t what it was. Something had come through the door without opening it, something warm, spreading up the back of his skull and across, and down the insides of his arms. He’d felt that on a staircase, with his fingers on his forehead. *He’s happy.* It was a slow, contented kind of warmth, like a man’s after a good dinner. Somebody sitting in a long room, four hundred miles away, had finished his coffee, and turned back his cuff, and laid the tip of one long white finger on the inside of his own left arm, quite lightly. He was smiling.

*Watch the toast*, it said.

He raised his head.

He saw her stop.

Nobody else would have noticed. She had brought the knife down to the edge of the bread, and it stayed there for perhaps the space of a heartbeat, and every bit of colour went out of her face, like water out of a basin, and left her lips the same as the rest. Her left arm was lying along the table, beside the plate. He saw the fingers of that hand open, all at once, as wide as they would go, and scrabble at the cloth, and stop. She pressed them flat. The whole of the arm had gone rigid, and it had started to tremble, a very fine fast tremor, like a wire in a gale. God help him, he could feel it, from the other end. It was going in at the wrist, like a red-hot needle, slowly, along the vein, and it was so *interesting*.

She moved the knife.

She moved it along the toast. It went about an inch. Her back had not altered by a hair. She drew a very small breath through her nose. There was a muscle jumping in her jaw, and a thin bright line had appeared on her lower lip, where she had got it between her teeth, on the inside; he saw it, he saw a bead come up at the corner, and it was gone. She looked straight in front of her. She took some butter on the end of the knife, and she spread it. She took some more.

Five seconds. Ten.

He had seen people held under that. He’d seen it done, in the other life, on a drawing-room floor. He had seen grown men on their knees, with their nails coming off. There wasn’t a sound. There were six hundred people in the Great Hall, and they were eating. Dean Thomas was laughing at something. She buttered that piece of toast out to the left-hand corner. She turned it, by a few degrees, with one finger, and she started on the crust.

It’s on her arm, he thought. *She’s got his Mark on her*. He had known that for three and a half months, since a chalk ridge and a sock, and he’d written it down in a black book and underlined it. *Long sleeves at Quidditch.*

Twenty seconds. Thirty.

Goyle had looked up. She had a bun in her hand. Her small slow eyes went to the side of Lyra’s face, and stayed there. She didn’t say anything. She put down the bun. She reached out, without any hurry, with one big arm, and took hold of the teapot; she filled a cup, and she moved that along the table, an inch at a time, until it was standing by the plate, and she left her hand there, on the cloth, beside it, and she went on looking at nothing.

*Every time*, said the warmth, very softly. It was all through him now, and it had got into his teeth. *Every time, Mr Potter. Do you see? I had thought you ought to have a receipt. A word from you, and this. Whatever you do. Wherever you go. Anything that you do to me, ever, I’ll have it from her. I find it’s so much more instructive than the other thing. She’s very well brought up. She doesn’t get that from Lucius.*

His lips were moving.

He could feel them. He couldn’t stop it. It wasn’t him, there was nothing funny, he was going to be sick. They had started to draw back from his teeth. He could feel the corners of his mouth going up, by themselves, a little at a time, with a slow, enormous satisfaction. He was smiling at her. He was sitting in the Great Hall with the tears starting to run out of his left eye, and looking down forty feet of table at a girl with a butter knife, and it felt like winning.

Fifty.

His right arm had come up.

It came off the table, very smooth, with no weight in it at all, and it seemed to know exactly where it was going, past the Slytherin table and away over the frost, for four hundred miles, to a long room with a fire. It had the wand in its hand, and he hadn’t known that. It was perfectly steady, and there was a word on his tongue, with a taste like a coin. *Sec*—

Something took hold of his sleeve.

It wasn’t hard. It was a big warm slightly earthy hand, with a thick blunt thumb and a bit of bonemeal under the nail, and it closed on the cloth just above his wrist. It didn’t pull. It was just there.

He couldn’t turn his head. He could see it from the tail of his eye: a round mild face, with a crooked jaw. It wasn’t looking at him but along the table, at the same thing he was, and it had gone a dull bad colour. Neville had got a spoonful of something halfway to a plant; and he’d laid that down. He sat there, holding on to Harry Potter’s cuff, as you would hold on to a child at the edge of a road, and he didn’t say one word.

Sixty.

It stopped.

It went out of him all at once. It was like being dropped. He felt it go, the whole of that warmth; it drew back along his arms and up his neck, and it went away, unhurried, through a door that it had no need to close. *There*, it said. He heard the small click of a cup going down on a saucer. His arm fell on the table. His wand rolled off into the porridge. He got his hand over his mouth, and he swallowed, and swallowed.

She had finished. There wasn’t a spot on that piece of toast that hadn’t got butter on it. It went out to the edges, in all four directions, perfectly even, as if it had been done by a machine. She laid the knife on her plate. Her hand was shaking so much that it rang. She looked at the toast for a moment. She took her hand away and she put it in her lap, the right one; she put that over the left; and she drew one long breath, and her head came up.

She looked along the Hall.

It was only for a second, and he didn’t think she knew she was doing it. Her eyes were perfectly black. They came across the benches and across the sun, past Hufflepuff, and they stopped on him. She looked like a person who has been a long way under water, and has come up, and doesn’t yet know where she is; and she looks for the shore. There wasn’t anything on her face at all.

Then her eyes went down. She reached out, slowly, with her right hand. She took up Goyle’s cup, and she drank.

He got as far as the second-floor landing.

There was a window there, with a stone seat and a cold white view of the lake, that nobody much used. He sat down, with porridge on his cuff, and held his hands out in front of him with the fingers spread. They were as steady as the hands of a clock.

He’d got it now. It was all quite simple. He wondered that he had been so slow.

He’d thought it was a war. He had thought there was a list, and that you crossed things off; you found the cup and the snake, and at the end of it there’d be a stone path and a green light, and you stood and took that. He’d been ready. He had been ready for four and a half years, it was the one thing that he had always been good at. He hadn’t been afraid of dying for a very long time.

*Anything that you do to me, ever.*

So that was what it had been for. All those Sundays, and the second-best paper. All that she’d been doing, with her nice clear voice and her long sleeves. He didn’t know what she was doing. He didn’t care. She could stand up and tell him that she had sold him to Lucius for a hairpin box. He’d been holding on to that like a stone, for three days: his hurt feelings. He’d stood at the end of a table, and he had taken the one thing that she had ever given him, and he had put it through her in front of the entire school; and she had sat there and buttered her toast. Two days later she had sat there and buttered it again.

Someone had sat down on the seat beside him. He hadn’t heard the feet. There was a smell of compost. The pot was put down on the stone, carefully, between them, and it moved one of its stalks a little way towards the light.

Neville didn’t ask. He looked out of the window, with his bag on his knee and his crooked jaw working slowly. He’d seen the whole thing, Harry thought: an arm coming up, and a smile. He hadn’t known what he was getting hold of. He was the only person in the world who could have, and he’d simply reached across, as he had once bent down to put his bag against a wall.

Neither of them said anything for a long time. The frost had started to go from the top of the pane.

It came up in him very quietly, without any heat, and that was what was so restful. He had hated people before; he’d hated Snape, and Dudley, and a woman with a lace collar, and it had been a hot, stupid, exhausting thing that made his hands shake. This was as cool and clear as water in a glass, and it had no bottom to it. It was the first thing he had felt in five months that he knew what to do with. He knew what you could do to a man who was afraid of dying. He had got a very great deal of information.

A little behind his right ear, something stirred, and made itself comfortable.

He’d never heard it sound so like him.

“I’m going to kill him,” said Harry.

It was a perfectly ordinary voice. He might have been saying that he’d go down to lunch. He went on looking at the lake.

“I don’t mean the prophecy. I don’t mean because I’ve got to. I want it to take a long time.”

There was a pause.

He waited. He knew what came next. It had been coming since June, outside an examination hall and at the door of a long room, and it had never failed. He turned his head.

Neville had stopped looking at the window. He looked at Harry’s hands for about a second and a half, with his mild, anxious, slightly protuberant eyes, and then at Harry’s face, at whatever was on it. His own had got very tired. It looked, of all things, sorry. He had been going to St Mungo’s since he was two, on Sundays, with his gran, to a ward with a lot of curtains.

He didn’t say *All right*.

“I know what a long time looks like,” said Neville.

He picked up his plant, and held it on his knee, and he stayed where he was.
