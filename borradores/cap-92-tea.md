# Chapter 98: Tea

By the end of January she had burnt a hundred and ninety pieces of paper, and marked every one of them first.

Nobody had planned it. It had begun on the Monday after the armchair, when he came in at a quarter past nine from Professor McGonagall’s study with ink to the wrist, and sat down on the rug by her knee, and put into her hand a strip torn from the bottom of a Transfiguration essay. *Eggs for breakfast. Ron sat. No potato.* She had looked at it for some time. Then she had taken the red pencil out of her book of rounds, where it had lain unused since June, and written *4/10. See me* in the margin, and given it back; and he had made a noise into the horsehair that she covered, for whoever might be listening, with a remark about Darius.

After that there was no stopping it.

One read aloud. That was the method. She had Herodotus in her right hand and the pleasant voice going, clear and unhurried, about the customs of the Scythians, and under it, on the arm of the chair, the small dry traffic of paper. He was not allowed more than a line. He had no notion of how to stop, otherwise. *McG made me do my Charms twice. She watched my hands the whole time. I think she counts them.* *Neville found one behind a pot. 41.* *Your hair’s coming down on the left.* She marked them for spelling and for sense, and wrote back when there was something to say, in the smallest hand that she could do; and at ten, before her rounds, the whole evening’s work went into the fire a strip at a time, and she watched each one go black, and curl, and lift.

Nothing else that she had been given since the summer had come without a bill. She knew that. She found that she had begun to save things up for it during the day: what Zabini had said about Montague’s cravat, and that the square-necked grey had been copied by a fourth-year in Ravenclaw, with nothing to show.

She gave him the other thing in the second week, and did not try to make it short.

*He has given me a day. Saturday 22 March, when I go home for Easter. I am to bring you with me. If I come by myself it falls due: mine, and my mother’s. He said it to my face, on the hearthrug, with Draco holding the tray.*

She went on with the Scythians while he read it. She heard him stop breathing, a little below her elbow, and begin again.

*Then I’ll come,* wrote Harry.

*You will not. He’ll be behind the door. It’s what the whole of this is for; he told me so in June. He wants you to find the way in, and he wants to be there when you do.*

*I can’t find it anyway. I’ve looked. There’s a field.*

*You find it if I tell you. Nine words. And I can only say them. He made it so; it won’t go on paper. I have tried.* She had, in August, on the window-seat, and watched the nib go across the sheet and leave nothing there at all. *He will hear me telling you. He’ll know the hour.*

She read him a paragraph about the burial of kings. He had the pencil in his fist. She could feel him looking at her left arm, where it lay in its black silk on the other side of the chair, with the small flat head on the wrist. He wrote four words, and held the strip where she could see it.

*It was asleep once.*

She read that to the end of the page. She turned over. Her voice did not alter by a hair, and nobody a room’s length off, or four hundred miles, could have told that she had stopped seeing the print.

She burnt it with the others. She found, on her rounds, that she had brought it away with her all the same.

The pork came up from the kitchens on a Thursday, in a cloth, by itself.

She found it on the fender when she came in, the better part of a shoulder, with a note pinned to the rind in a round careful hand that was not an elf’s. *Off the Friday order. Mind the grain.* Somebody had copied that out of a fat black book. She stood and looked at it rather longer than a joint of meat required.

“You’ve brought something that can’t complain,” said Lyra. “How thoughtful.”

He had got the old root-board out of somewhere. He laid the meat on it, on the rug, and knelt down on the other side, and took out his wand; and then he did nothing at all. He knelt there with it in his hand. He had gone the colour of the fat.

She understood, a moment later than she should have. Snape had made the cuts, on Tuesdays. There was no Snape here.

Paper was no use; he would not have been able to hold the pencil. She said it aloud, in the nice clear voice, as one might ask for the window to be opened.

“I can’t very well shut what isn’t open, Potter.”

He looked up at her. He looked at the line that ran down from under her ear, and then at his own right hand.

It did not go up by itself. She had been ready for that. She had seen that arm rise on a landing, and in a pink room, and across a wet floor, as smooth as a thing on a wire, and she watched for it now with her back against the chair and her wand loose in her sleeve; and what she saw was a boy lift his arm from the shoulder as though it weighed a hundredweight, with the sweat coming out on his lip, and point it at a piece of meat. His mouth moved. He got it all out, under his breath, to the last syllable. She had never heard the end of it before.

The rind opened in a long clean line, as a letter is opened.

He put the wand down on the rug. He was shaking a good deal. She thought it was very likely the first time that he had ever done it on purpose, and that he knew it, and that there was nothing to be said about that which would not make it either better or worse than it was.

“Well?” said Lyra.

He sang.

He had got better. That was the first thing. Somebody over Christmas had taught him where to put his breath, and he held the four notes level, an inch above the meat, against the grain, and the edges crept together behind his wand and stayed. It left a raised pale seam. She knew what that looked like. She had one.

The second thing was what she had been waiting for, and it did not come. She had lain on a floor under that sound, with her eyes going dark, and thought that if it was the last thing she heard it would do. She had it still. She could have told anybody where it had cracked. She sat now with her hands in her lap and listened to it as one listens to a hymn in a language learnt at school; and it was a boy, kneeling on a rug, doing something difficult rather well. A long white finger had turned that over on a Sunday in December and set it down a little to the left.

She was glad that he had his eyes on the pork.

“Again,” said Lyra. “Do another. I want to try it.”

She was not good at it.

She needed some time to believe this. She had been top in every subject that could be learnt from a book since she was eleven, and in two that could not. She knelt down, and held her wand as she had seen it held, and opened her mouth; and there came out of it a thin, correct, careful noise, a full quarter of a tone under the note, like a governess at a piano. The cut closed for the width of a finger and came open again with a small wet sound.

“You’re flat,” said Harry.

“I am nothing of the kind.”

“You are, though. You were flat at the feast.” The corner of his mouth had gone. He had it back directly, and then, as she watched, he seemed to decide that he need not. “It’s not your ear. It’s your breath. You sing like someone who’s waiting to be told to stop. Down here. Not up there.”

“I beg your pardon?”

“That’s what Lupin said. He had me against a dresser. Stand up. Feet apart.”

She stood up. She had never in her life been told to put her feet apart by anybody. She did it, in the square-necked grey, on a bald rug, with her bad arm in its sling and her chin up; and he walked round her once, frowning, exactly like a small tired man in a brown jumper, and told her, as though she were a first-year with a recorder, to give him her note.

It was a quarter of a tone under, at ten o’clock, and again at half past. He sat on the fender with his elbows on his knees and beat time with a toasting fork; and at some point she found that she was so angry that she had forgotten to be anything else, and that he was grinning at her quite openly, with his terrible lopsided mouth, as he had not since a gale in November.

“Six weeks,” said Harry. “You said.”

“I shall take ten points from Gryffindor for every week over.”

“You can’t. I’m the one teaching.”

“That has never stopped Professor Snape.”

She had it for two inches by the middle of February. It never did come right. It closed, and it held, and it sounded like a kettle; and he said that the meat couldn’t tell the difference, and that nobody who was bleeding would be likely to complain.

Weasley’s sister came for her on the first Tuesday of the month, between Charms and lunch, in the long corridor on the third floor, with forty people in it.

She did not raise her voice. She stepped out from the wall in her red-and-gold scarf, with her hair tied back and a split in her lip from somebody’s elbow, and stood in the way. She had been made Seeker; she had got harder about the mouth.

“Malfoy,” said Ginny Weasley. “You’ve got something of mine. You’ve had it since November. I’d like it back.”

The corridor had begun to slow down. Lyra felt it, as one feels a current. It lay on her wrist with its eye open; and there had been a passage, once, with a tall grey window at the end, and a mouth with a split in it beginning to say a thing that would have had her mother dead by Christmas.

She looked for the pleasant voice, and it was where it always was.

“I can’t think what you—”

“No,” said Ginny. “I didn’t suppose that you could.”

She held out her hand, with a pencil in it, rather chewed, and a sheet of parchment folded in four.

*He told her*, thought Lyra. And then: *no. He told her that he wouldn’t.* She did not know how she knew that. It was in the angle of the pencil.

She took it. She set the parchment against the wall, between a suit of armour and a second-year, and she wrote with her right hand, standing up, in front of forty people, in the best hand in Slytherin. She was not long about it; she had had it by heart since the ninth of November.

*You had watched me come in from the pitch. You said that I had begun to listen before I spoke, and that you had done the same for a year. You asked me who it was, and said that I need only nod. Then you looked at my left sleeve and said: It’s him. You’ve got him on your— I took the hour before you had finished the sentence.*

*You were right. He hears whatever is said near that arm. If he had heard you say it, my mother would have paid for it within the week. That is the reason. I do not offer it as an excuse. I put your collar straight afterwards; I thought you ought to know that I had the time.*

*L. Malfoy.*

She folded it once, and held it out.

The girl read it where she stood. She read it as she had learnt to read everything, twice, with the pencil going under the words; and Lyra watched the colour leave her face a freckle at a time, and saw her get to the collar, and stop there.

She did not burn it. She put it inside her robes, on the left.

Then she took out her wand.

It was very quick, and extremely well done. Something made a sound like a wet sheet being shaken, and the corridor went dark; and something the size of a kitten, with wings, came out of each of Lyra’s nostrils and set about her head. Somebody screamed. More came, and more. They got into her hair, and they got into the square neck, and one of them fastened on the lobe of her left ear, just above where the line began; and she stood in the middle of the third-floor corridor, in front of the whole of her Arithmancy class, and beat at her own face with a Herodotus.

When it was over she had a pin left. Her nose was running. Somewhere behind her a second-year was being sick with laughter into a helmet.

“That’s for the hour,” said Ginny Weasley. She had not moved. Her voice was quite level, and it carried. “I haven’t started on the rest.”

Lyra had a prefect’s badge on the front of her robes. She had taken ten points from that House for less, in her time; she had taken five for breathing. She stood with her hair down one side and a scratch across the bridge of her nose, and she found that she had put her hand up to her collar, from habit, to see if it was straight.

“Your follow-through is too wide,” said Lyra. “I should see to it.”

She went in to lunch as she was. It appeared to be the only thing to do. Zabini looked up from his soup as she sat down, and took in the hair, and the nose, and felt behind his ear.

“Eight,” said Zabini. “I should have said nine, but she let you keep the pin.”

On the Saturday, against Ravenclaw, the Weasley girl took the Snitch in eleven minutes from under Cho Chang’s broom, upside down, with one hand. Lyra watched it from the Slytherin stand. She had held that record herself since her third year. She did not suppose that anything had been paid, on either side; she had done the sum. It was only that for the first time since November she could look at the back of that red head without seeing it turned towards a window.

She did it as a column, in the end, on the window-seat of the dormitory, with nothing written down.

If she brought him in at the front door, on her arm, he was dead before the soup. If she came alone, then she was, and her mother with her; and Draco would go on pouring tea for whoever was left. That was two, and neither of them would do.

There was a third. She had been walking round it since a strip of paper with four words on it. He could not come in at the door if he was expected. Very well: then he must not be expected. He must have the way in, and nobody must know that he had it. It had to be said, and it had to be said aloud, and there was exactly one condition on which the thing on her wrist had ever been known not to hear.

*It appears to sleep when you are very nearly gone. I shall have to take care that you are not often so ill.*

She looked at that for a long while. She had been afraid of a good many things since she was eleven. She discovered, with some interest, that she was more afraid of this than of all of them, and that it made no difference whatever to the arithmetic.

It would want something that could be measured. A wound could not be measured; she had tried one. She knew of a thing that could. She had read the label, in July, in her father’s study, with the blinds half down. She knew what was in the centres, and in what strength, and for what weight of man; and she sat with a pencil that she did not use and worked it out for a girl of seven stone who had eaten half a slice of cake since Christmas. A third part. It might be a quarter. Between that and too much there was about the width of a fingernail, and on the other side of too little there was nothing at all but a girl being sick in a shop.

A jar of bezoars stood on the third shelf. She knew that as well. It had been six minutes away.

She got up, and went to her trunk, and took out from under the winter things a phial about the size of a tooth. She held it to the window. It had been meant for a Friday, and it turned over in the light, slow and heavy and gold, taking no notice of her; twelve hours in which one would know which foot. It promised nothing. She had read that too.

Then she wrote two lines, in her own hand, and left them on a pillow by the wall, on a long black book.

*I need the shop for an hour on Saturday the 15th. I shall be the one eating. Potter will have what Greer hadn’t.*

It was gone in the morning. On her bedside table, where for six months there had been something in greaseproof paper, lay an iron key as long as her hand. No note had come with it, and nothing in greaseproof paper.

Professor Snape set Amortentia on the Thursday, with contempt.

He did it every year in the week of the fourteenth, he said, in order that the sixth form might understand what they were being sold. It was the most dangerous thing on the syllabus. It did not make anybody love anybody. It made a person unable to think of anything else, which was quite another matter, and it would do that to any fool who drank it, whatever he had been feeling beforehand; there was no constitution on earth that was proof against it, and they might spare him the essays arguing that there was. They would know it by the steam, which rose in spirals, and by the smell, which was different for everyone, and nobody’s business.

Hers came out the colour of mother-of-pearl, and the best in the room. She had known that it would be.

She had not been prepared for the other.

It came up off the cauldron in the green dark, and she went on stirring, with great composure, clockwise. It had hot horsehair in it, and dust, and a fire; a boy’s collar that had been out in the rain; and under those, quite distinct, so that her hand stopped for the length of one turn, there was cold bacon, between two pieces of thick white bread, in greaseproof paper.

Nobody had told her that it could be that. She had thought it was a thing for people in love.

She filled a phial with the rest, when his back was turned. It took about as long as picking up a glove.

She would have said, if anybody had asked her, that she had reasoned it out. She was going to eat a third part of a poisoned sweet in front of a person whose right arm had laid her open, and to lie on a floor, and to say *not yet* to him, and be obeyed. He had not obeyed her in June. He had put her hands straight and smiled. She wanted something that would hold him. One was entitled to a security; any Malfoy would have said so. Not until she was on the seventh floor, with the phial in her sleeve and her heart going, did she know what it was that she was doing, and by then she had done it.

The room had given them tea. It did, sometimes: a brown pot and two cups, on the fender. She poured. She had her back to him. It needed three drops.

“You’ve learnt to make it properly, I hope,” said Lyra, and held out the cup.

He took it. He was on the rug, with his back against the chair and a strip of paper half written on his knee. He said something about the pot. He lifted the cup, as he had lifted a thousand, and she watched it go up, with the steam coming off it; and it stopped, about four inches from his mouth.

He did not put it down. He held it there. She saw his nostrils move.

He had been taught, she remembered, to stand over a thing and breathe in and say what it was. He had been taught it in the same room that she had.

“You’ve put Amortentia in my tea,” said Harry.

She might have said a great many things, and had most of them ready. She looked at the back of his neck, and found that there was only one that she could get out.

“Yes,” said Lyra.

He turned round. He looked up at her, with the cup in both hands. She waited for it: for the white quiet, and the hands going into the pockets. She had earned it. She thought that she would even be glad of it.

His mouth was doing something else.

He set the cup on his knee. He took up the pencil, and the strip that he had been writing, and turned it over; and he wrote for longer than a line, with his arm round it, and held it up to her. She had to bend to read it.

*In the other one it was treacle tart, a broom handle & something at the Burrow. Sixth year, first week. I thought about it for months.*

He put his nose over the cup again, with his eyes shut, like a man at a wine tasting.

“It’s two out of three,” said Harry.

She could not speak for a moment.

“*Which* two?”

“The tart. The broom.”

“And the third?”

He sniffed. He took his time over it.

“Burnt paper,” said Harry. “Second-best. I’d know it anywhere.”

She stared at him; and he began to laugh.

This was not the one from the wind. It came up from much further down, with his head back against the horsehair, and the tea slopping over his fingers; he laughed until he had to set the cup on the fender, and wipe his eyes on his cuff, and it was the first time that she had heard it since a night in June when a chair had given way. She stood over him with the pot in her hand. She could not think what to do with her face.

“You were going to *drug* me,” said Harry. “With that. Me. I’ve been in love with you since I was eleven, I’ve got a notebook, I flew to Wiltshire in a blizzard with a road atlas— What did you think it was going to *do?*”

“Hold you,” said Lyra.

It came out very small. He stopped laughing.

He looked at the cup, on the fender, full to the brim, with the steam going up from it in spirals. He did not pour it away. He left it where it stood, between them. Then he raised his eyes, and they were quite steady, and there was no kindness in them, and she was obscurely grateful for that.

“Nothing moves without asking,” said Harry. “You, Draco, anybody. That’s what you said. On a hill.” He picked up the pencil. “You don’t put me anywhere.”

She heard it. She heard the whole of it, in her own voice, with snow on it. She had stood on a chalk ridge and made that the price of coming back to him; she had turned down a Vow on the strength of it, in a hospital bed, with headings. And she had come up seven floors tonight with three drops in her sleeve to arrange a boy for his own good.

She sat down. Her knees had rather settled it.

“I wanted to be sure of you,” she said.

“You can’t be. Nor can I.” He tore the strip in half, and gave her the clean end. “That’s why you’ve got a pencil.”

They wrote it the next night. It took four sheets, and the whole of the Persian retreat.

She did the heads. He would not have known how. She set them down one under another in her own hand, on the second-best paper, with the red pencil for the numbers, and passed each across the arm of the chair as it was done; and he read it, and sometimes he wrote a word in the margin, and sometimes he only nodded, and the fire went down.

*1. Saturday the 15th, Hogsmeade, at half past two. The empty shop between the cobbler’s and the cauldrons. V.C. has given the key.*

*2. One, from the box on the Headmaster’s desk. H. to ask for it. He may show this paper.*

*3. I take a third part. I have done the figures; they are below. I may be wrong.*

*4. Before it, I drink what H. left in my bag in December. It is mine to spend.*

*5. H. keeps hold of my left wrist from the beginning. He will feel it go.*

*6. Until it goes, we say only what follows. It knows now when it sleeps and why; it must be given a reason. So: a girl has gone to her dead friend’s shop with one of her dead friend’s sweets, because she is tired; and a boy has followed her, and is too late to stop her. He will like that. He will think that it brings you nearer. The lines are on the fourth sheet. Learn them.*

*7. When it goes, I talk. H. does not interrupt.*

*8. The stone is not to be used until I say so. Not if I am sick. Not if I ask for my mother. Not until I say.*

She read him a page and a half about the Hellespont while he had that one. He was a long time over it.

When the sheet came back there was a ninth, in pencil, in a cramped upright hand that she would have known in the dark.

*9. If you cant say, I decide. H.P.*

She looked at it. She took the red pencil and ruled a line through it, from one end to the other, quite straight, and handed it across.

He did not argue. He could not have, aloud; and he did not write *please*, or *Lyra*, or anything else that she had been ready for. He took the sheet on his knee, and under the line she had drawn he wrote the same seven words again, larger, and pressed so hard on the last of them that the point went through.

He held it up.

She sat with the book open on her lap. She had told him, on Thursday, that she wanted to be sure of him; and here was the answer, in his own writing, twice. He was not going to promise. He was going to stand in a shop on Saturday with a stone in his hand and do as she said for precisely as long as he could bear it, and after that he would do what he thought right, and she would be in no condition to take points.

It was the thing she had been afraid of since June. She looked at it lying on a sheet of second-best paper with a hole in it; and it was also, as far as she could see, the only clause on four sheets that was true.

She took the pencil. She put an apostrophe in *can’t*.

Then she signed, at the foot, and dated it, and gave it to him to do the same; and it was only when she saw the two names one above the other that she noticed what day it was.
