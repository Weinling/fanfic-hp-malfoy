# Chapter 8: Hogsmeade

“Sugar quills,” said Goyle, with the reverence other people kept for chapel. “A whole box. I’m going to keep one up my sleeve in History of Magic and suck it all through the goblin rebellions, and nobody will ever know.”

“Binns wouldn’t know if you ate the desk,” said Crabbe.

“That’s not the point. The point is I’ll know.”

The notice had gone up on the common-room board a fortnight earlier, *First Hogsmeade Weekend: Hallowe’en*, and the third-years had talked of very little else since. Lyra had let them. She had no great interest in a village of shops for ordinary people, and she had heard enough at home about the Three Broomsticks to know that it was mostly full of farmers and Ministry clerks on their afternoon off.

But it seemed unkind to say so to Goyle, who had been planning her route round Honeydukes since the first week of term and had drawn a map, so Lyra had agreed that it all sounded tremendous fun, and, rather to her surprise, half meant it.

The Great Hall smelled of pumpkin. It had smelled of pumpkin since the day before, when Hagrid had dragged in the great orange monsters from his patch, and by Hallowe’en morning the whole castle seemed to be holding its breath for the feast.

There was still nothing from her father. Nineteen days, and a second letter, shorter, sent the Monday after the first, which had not been answered either. Draco had not written back about the cards, which was not like him; Draco answered letters before he had finished reading them.

And Lyra had not answered her mother. She had begun a reply to the postscript three times, and three times stopped, not knowing what she was allowed to say, and put it on the fire.

Every Malfoy she had was being silent at once, herself included, and she had stopped looking for the eagle owl when the post came in, or at any rate had stopped being seen to look.

All down the long tables, third-years were getting up and going to fetch their cloaks. At the Gryffindor table Weasley and Granger rose together, Granger already winding a scarf round her neck, Weasley still eating a piece of toast, and went out through the doors talking with their heads close together. Lyra’s eyes followed them out, from pure habit, and then went back to look for the third head, which was usually between them and generally the loudest.

It was not between them. It was at the far end of the Gryffindor table, on its own.

Potter sat with his elbows among the plates and a cherry tart in one hand, and ate it without any hurry while the third-years streamed out past him. Nobody stopped. He was the most talked-about boy in the school, the one every corridor turned towards, and for once he looked like nothing more than a boy who had been left behind, which Lyra would not have thought possible.

There were people who could have everything, and people who could not even manage a trip to a sweetshop. It was nice, for once, to see which of them was which. Lyra let herself enjoy it for a moment longer than she meant to, and then took Crabbe and Goyle to fetch their cloaks.

The queue for Filch went right across the Entrance Hall. He stood at the great doors with his list and his lamp-eyed cat, checking every name against it twice, with the slow suspicion of a man who believed that every child in the school was smuggling something, and Lyra was nine names from the front when Potter came out of the Great Hall behind her, wiping cherry off his fingers onto his robes.

He did not look at the queue. He did not turn towards the Gryffindor stairs either, which was where a boy with no permission and nothing to do all day ought to have been going. He went up the marble staircase with his hands in his pockets, quite unhurried, in the direction of the third floor, where there was nothing at the weekend but a lot of empty classrooms and a statue of a one-eyed witch.

Lyra stepped out of the queue and went after him as far as the bottom step.

“Potter.”

He stopped two steps up, which put his face level with hers. “Malfoy.”

“You’re going anyway,” she said, very low.

Potter pointed at her, delighted, like a quizmaster. “And the prize goes to the girl in green.”

“You haven’t got permission. The whole school knows you haven’t—”

“My aunt and uncle wouldn’t sign the form. After the business with Aunt Marge.” He shook his head at the injustice of it. “So I did Uncle Vernon’s signature myself. It’s a good one, I’ve had practice. And the form folded itself up into a little paper bird and flew straight into the fire.” He shrugged. “I did ask. Nobody can say I didn’t ask.”

“You’re mad.”

“That’s been said.”

“By people with *sense*. Filch is ten yards away with a list, Potter—”

At the door, Filch looked up at the sound of his own name. Lyra smiled at him until he looked down again.

“Filch has been trying to catch me since I was eleven,” said Potter. “He’s got further with Mrs Norris.”

“This is not a midnight stroll to the kitchens.” She kept it to a hiss, because the cat was looking at her. “Sirius Black is looking for you. Do you understand that? You, specifically. And you want to go and wander about a village outside the grounds?”

“Malfoy, in two years I’ve had a teacher with Voldemort on the back of his head and a snake the size of a train. They both had a go. I’m still here.” Lyra flinched at the name, as she always did, and was too angry to care whether he saw. “I’m not going to sit in a tower and be frightened of one rat of a traitor.”

“This isn’t a monster in a cellar!” It came out louder than she meant, and three people in the queue turned round, and she brought it down again. “Monsters want whatever monsters want, and you happen to be standing there. Black has spent twelve years thinking about *you*. He’s out of Azkaban to kill you, you idiot, and you’re walking out of the front gate to make it easier for him!”

Potter blinked at her. Then his face changed, very slowly, into something delighted.

“You’re worried about me.”

“I’m worried about my afternoon. I’ve been looking forward to it for a fortnight, and you are about to ruin it by getting yourself murdered in the same village.”

“Ta,” said Potter. “Honestly. But it’s not exactly safe in here, is it?” He looked past her for a moment, across the hall towards the windows, and something went over his face that was not delighted at all, something cool and quite far away. “Old castle. Hundreds of holes in the walls. Things can live in a place like this for years, and nobody ever sees them.”

“Hogwarts has the strongest protective enchantments in Britain. It has Dementors on every gate.”

“Azkaban’s got Dementors on every door,” said Potter. “Ask Black how that went.”

She had run out of sensible things to say, so she said a cutting one. “Shall I bring you back a sugar mouse? Something to suck on in your tower while you mope?”

“Don’t trouble yourself,” said Potter. “I’ll pick my own.” He had gone two steps further up before he looked back. “Pink, if they’ve got one.”

He went on up, round the turn of the stair, and was gone.

“Get yourself *killed*, then,” Lyra told the empty staircase, under her breath.

When she got back to her place, Crabbe and Goyle looked at her face and then at each other, and did not ask. Filch was four names away. He was a yard off now, and his cat was looking straight at her, and all Lyra had to do was open her mouth.

She pulled on her gloves instead, one finger at a time. She had tried to stop Potter doing things before. Two years of it had taught her that it could not be done, and that the only reward for trying was to look foolish while he did it anyway.

Filch found her name on the list with a speed that surprised them both.

There was another consideration, and she turned it over on the walk down the lane. If Black really meant to get into the castle, he would have been working out how ever since July. A man who had walked out of Azkaban past a hundred Dementors would hardly be put off by a few charms on the gates. Whereas a boy nobody knew was in Hogsmeade, keeping out of sight in a crowded village full of witches and wizards, might very well be safer than a boy everyone knew was sitting on his own in an empty tower.

It was a perfectly sensible piece of reasoning, and she did not care for the shape of it, so she put it away and informed Crabbe and Goyle that if either of them mentioned that hooligan again before supper, she would leave them in the Shrieking Shack.

Hogsmeade turned out to be a single crooked high street of thatched and slated cottages, with a post office and a great many chimneys, under a sky the colour of pewter, and it was a good deal nicer than Lyra had been led to believe.

She looked for him in Honeydukes first. She did not mean to. Honeydukes was also the first surprise. She had grown up on sweets from her mother’s confectioner in Diagon Alley, who made sugared violets to order and would not sell you anything without first enquiring after your family, and she had expected a village sweetshop to be a poor relation. It was nothing of the kind. It was a long, low, steamy room crammed to the ceiling with things she had never seen, and Goyle went round it with the face of a pilgrim.

Lyra bought a box of Chocolate Frogs because it would have been rude not to, and then, while Goyle was deciding between sugar quills in three sizes, she was standing at the counter with a single sugar quill in her hand that she did not remember picking up. There were sugar mice in the next jar, pink ones. She did not look at them.

She paid for it anyway, and put it in her pocket, and did not ask herself why.

Weasley and Granger were over by the window, filling an enormous paper bag from every jar in reach and ticking things off a list. Granger looked up as Lyra passed, and looked at her for exactly as long as it took to recognise her, and then turned back to the jars as if Lyra were a shelf of liquorice wands. Weasley did not look up.

Lyra had been cut by experts, at her mother’s parties, by women who had been doing it for forty years, and it was absurd that a Mudblood with a paper bag should manage it so well.

She looked for him in the Three Broomsticks, from a seat by the window where she could see the street. It was the second surprise. It was hot and crowded and very loud, full of farmers and clerks on their afternoon off, exactly as she had been told, and she liked it at once, which was not what she had been told. Madam Rosmerta, who ran it, had a great many curls and a laugh that carried across the room, and she brought them three foaming tankards of butterbeer without being asked twice.

Lyra had been warned that butterbeer was a children’s drink. It was hot and sweet and tasted of butterscotch and something warmer underneath, and it went down to the very ends of her fingers and toes, and she had finished the first tankard before Goyle had finished telling them about the sugar quills.

She would have liked a second, and a Malfoy did not walk down a high street with a bottle in her hand. So she did the only reasonable thing, which was to buy two bottles from the bar, take them down to the post office, and have an owl fly them up to the castle addressed to herself; and she stood on the post-office step for a good minute afterwards while Goyle bought stamps, watching the crowd going up and down the high street for an untidy dark head without a hat.

The postmistress looked at her as though she were mad. Lyra paid her twice the rate and did not explain. It was extravagant, certainly, but she had never understood what money was for if not for not having to carry things.

Zonko’s Joke Shop was as stupid as she had expected, full of Dungbombs and Hiccough Sweets and Nose-Biting Teacups, a velvet box of silver rings that claimed to warm when the other’s wearer was in trouble, and a crowd of fourth-years buying things to throw at each other. Lyra stood by the door looking bored while Crabbe and Goyle went through the Dungbombs. In her experience Zonko’s sold nothing that worked properly.

There were dozens of untidy dark heads. None of them was his. By the middle of the afternoon she had looked into every shop on the street and both of the side lanes, and had not found him anywhere, and she began to feel, absurdly, as though she ought to be cross about it.

Either he had lost his nerve and stayed in the tower like a sensible person, which she did not for one moment believe, or he was keeping out of sight, which was what she had told herself he ought to do. It was strange how little better that made her feel.

The light went early, as it did at the end of October. The bell in the little church tower rang the hour and shopkeepers began to put their heads out of their doors to remind the students that it was time to walk back up to the castle. Lyra counted her money on the walk back, and stopped, and counted it again.

She had spent two Galleons and a handful of silver. It was not the sum that troubled her; she had spent more than that on a hair ribbon. It was that she had spent it a Knut here and a Sickle there, in a village, on sweets and butterbeer and a quill she had not wanted, without once noticing, like any ordinary girl on an ordinary weekend, and that she had enjoyed it.

She put the purse away and said nothing about it to anybody.

The Great Hall had been done up for the feast while they were out. Hundreds of hollowed pumpkins floated under the enchanted ceiling with candles burning inside them, grinning down at the tables, and live bats swooped in clouds between the beams, and the ceiling itself had been made to match the weather outside, so that above it all the clouds were going by fast and dark and ragged with a half moon behind them.

The food was extraordinary even by Hogwarts standards. Crabbe and Goyle, who had eaten their way down the entire high street, still found room for seconds.

Her parcels came in with the pudding. The school owls brought them down in a long flapping procession, a large box from Honeydukes she had sent up to save carrying it, the butterbeer, and a bag of Dungbombs that Crabbe had not been able to resist and had then not dared to carry past Filch. Several people at the Slytherin table looked impressed. Lyra unwrapped the butterbeer without seeming to notice them.

And she looked, from under her lashes, at the Gryffindor table.

He was there. Of course he was. He was in the very middle of the Gryffindors, with his tie undone and a crowd of people round him, and he was talking at the top of his voice and waving a chicken leg for emphasis, and the people round him were laughing.

Lyra could not hear most of it over the noise of the hall. She caught *Honeydukes* once, and *cellar*, and *nearly sat on Snape*, and something about the Shrieking Shack, none of which made much sense, and the way his friends were laughing told her the rest.

He had been out. He had been out all afternoon, in the same village, among the same crowds, and she had not seen one hair of his head.

Something in her chest that had been clenched since breakfast let go, which was irritating, and she went back to her butterbeer.

Well. He had got away with it. That was the end of the matter.

Back in the common room afterwards, she found the younger ones sitting in a gloomy cluster by the fire, the way children sit who have spent all day hearing about a treat they are not old enough for. Lyra put the Honeydukes box down in the middle of them and told them to share it properly or she would know.

They fell on it as if they had not just eaten a feast, and one of the second-years told her she was the best Slytherin in the history of the school, and Lyra said that was very probably true, and sat down in her chair by the fire with a great sense of having been gracious.

She could not manage the butterbeer after all. She had eaten too much. She put a Preserving Charm on both bottles, which she was rather proud of being able to do, and set them on the mantelpiece for another day, and listened to Parkinson telling the fourth-years about a Fanged Frisbee he had nearly bought.

The sugar quill was still in her pocket. She left it there.

The candles in the Great Hall had been dimmed almost to nothing, and Lyra lay in a squashy purple sleeping bag near the wall, in her dressing gown, with her back to the panelling and the whole room in front of her.

Ten minutes after the sugar quill, Professor Snape had come into the common room with his face quite white and quite still, and not raised his voice, which was worse. Everyone to the Great Hall. Now. In what they were wearing; no, Parkinson, not even shoes. They had gone up through the dungeons in a long silent line, the first-years clutching each other’s sleeves, and found the Hufflepuffs arriving in their dressing gowns and the Ravenclaws coming down the marble staircase looking frightened, and above it all, somewhere high in the castle, a poltergeist cackling.

The Fat Lady was gone. Somebody had slashed the portrait that guarded Gryffindor Tower to ribbons, during the feast, while every soul in the castle was downstairs, and she had fled into another painting on the second floor and would not come out. Peeves had seen who did it, and was delighted to tell everybody. He had a nasty temper, that Sirius Black.

Then Dumbledore had told them the teachers would search the castle from top to bottom, and they would sleep here until it was done, with the doors sealed and the prefects on guard; and wished them goodnight as calmly as if it were the end of an ordinary feast, and gone out, and the great doors had closed behind him with a sound like the end of a sentence.

She looked for Potter. It was not hard. He was in the middle of the hall among the Gryffindors, lying on his back in a purple sleeping bag between Weasley and Granger, with his hands behind his head. She expected him to look frightened. Anyone else would have; she could hear Colin Creevey crying quietly somewhere nearby, and even Weasley’s face, turned sideways on the pillow, was the colour of porridge.

Potter did not look frightened. He was staring up at the ceiling, where the clouds had cleared and the stars were going over, and his face had gone set and quite still, and the only thing on it was something Lyra recognised from a dozen Quidditch matches, from the faces of Seekers who had reached for the Snitch and closed their hands on air. It was the look of a plan that had gone wrong.

His lips moved. He did not make a sound. The candles were low and he was a long way off, and she could not have sworn to it in front of anybody. But she had spent two years watching that mouth say things she would rather it had not, and she was nearly sure of the words.

*Just missed him.*

“Lucky it was during the feast,” said someone near her, a fourth-year from her own House, in the half-whisper people used once the candles had been dimmed. “Everyone was down here. If he’d come in the night—”

Lyra was no longer at all sure it had been lucky.

*Just missed him*: as though Potter had gone looking, and known where, and when, and only got the hour wrong. And if Potter could know where Black would be, somebody could know where Potter would be. She lay with her eyes on the ceiling and went through it as coldly as she knew how.

There were Dementors on every gate and every path into the grounds, and enchantments on the walls older than the Ministry, and Black had walked past all of them as if they were not there, on the one night of the year when every soul in the castle, students and staff and Filch and the ghosts, was shut in a single room eating pudding. And then he had gone up through the empty castle to one portrait in particular.

Lyra did not know where the entrance to Gryffindor Tower was. She had been at Hogwarts for more than two years and had never needed to, and she doubted one Slytherin in ten could have found it. Black had found it in the dark, at the first attempt.

Either he remembered this castle better than anybody had a right to after twelve years in Azkaban, or someone had told him the way. And the way past the Dementors. And the hour when nobody would be watching the stairs.

Somebody on the inside. Somebody who knew the castle and could open its doors. Somebody, at the very least, who sat at the staff table.

She thought of the Fat Lady’s canvas hanging in ribbons. Black had not come for the Great Hall, where Potter had been sitting in plain view among four hundred people. He had gone up to the tower while it stood empty, to wait. She thought, much more clearly than she would have liked, about a dark dormitory, and a boy coming up to bed after the feast with his mouth full of treacle tart.

Lyra turned her head on the pillow and looked along the hall at the doors. Snape had come back in and was standing just inside them, talking to Dumbledore in a voice too low to carry. She could not hear a word.

She saw Dumbledore shake his head, once, and saw Snape’s mouth go thin, and saw Snape’s eyes go, just for a moment, up towards the empty staff table, to the place at the end where the new Defence teacher usually sat. Then Dumbledore said something short, and Snape turned on his heel and went out.

Her father would read about all this in the morning. Perhaps this, at least, would make him write.
