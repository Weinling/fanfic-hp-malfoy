# Year One, Part 5: The Forest

“Not all of you,” said Professor McGonagall. “The Headmaster has persuaded me that an example is better made of two than of nine. The two who began it.”

She looked at Harry over her square spectacles, in the corridor outside Transfiguration, with the rest of the class going past them in a wide careful curve.

“Tonight, Potter. Nine o’clock. The front doors. Hagrid will meet you there.” Her mouth thinned. “And Miss Malfoy.”

“Yes, Professor.”

“And if there is a next time, there will not be a detention. There will be a train.”

She went into her classroom. Harry stood in the corridor with his bag on his shoulder and thought, quite calmly, about the Forest.

He had known since McGonagall’s first sentence where they were going. He did not know how he knew. That was the way of it, this winter: things came up in him like bubbles in a pond, one at a time, from somewhere very deep, and broke on the surface, and he knew them, and had always known them, and could not remember learning any of them. Hagrid, with a lantern. A dog that was a coward. A path through the trees, and a smell of blood. And, from further down than any of it, a boy’s voice screaming, high and thin, going away between the trees as fast as it could go.

He did not know whose voice it was. He thought he would, by nine.

Malfoy came at five past, alone, across the dark lawn from the castle, with her cloak held tight round her at the throat and her chin very high. She stopped a good way off. Not from Hagrid, who was standing at the edge of the light with a crossbow over his shoulder and Fang leaning on his leg. From Harry.

It had been eight days since the corridor. She had not looked at him once in those eight days, in any lesson, at any meal, which in a castle that size took work. He had seen her doing the work. Every time he came into a room she found something on the other side of it to need.

He knew what she was not looking at. He had seen it himself, on the floor of the corridor with blood in his eye: his own face, from the outside, in hers.

“Righ’,” said Hagrid, “we’re off, then. Into the Forest. Stick by me, an’ yeh’ll be fine.”

“The *Forest*.” Malfoy’s voice did not quite work on the first word. She made it work on the second. “At night. That’s—my father will hear of this. They can’t send students into the Forbidden Forest at night, it’s—”

“Can an’ have,” said Hagrid, who did not seem to think much of her father. “Come on.”

They went down the slope in the lantern-light, the three of them and the dog, and in under the trees.

It was very dark under there, and very full: of the smell of leaf-mould and old wet bark, of things moving off in the undergrowth just out of the light, of the sound of something a long way off that might have been an owl. Harry walked at Hagrid’s left hand. Malfoy walked at Hagrid’s right. She kept Hagrid exactly between them, the whole way, as if he were a wall; and every time the path bent and Hagrid stepped round a root and the wall moved, she moved too, quickly, to keep it there.

He watched her do it for ten minutes. It made him feel worse than the Forest.

Then Hagrid stopped, and swung the lantern low over the path, and they all saw it: a splash of something on the dead leaves, silver, shining like spilt moonlight.

“Unicorn,” said Hagrid heavily. “Hurt bad. That’s the second this month.” He straightened up, and looked at the two of them, and at the dark. “I’m goin’ after it. It’ll not get far. You two stay right here, on the path, with Fang. Anythin’ happens, red sparks. I’ll come.”

“You’re *leaving* us?” said Malfoy.

“Five minutes,” said Hagrid, and was gone, enormous and quiet, into the trees, and the light went with him.

Harry lit his wand.

⁂

They stood on the path in the little circle of light from his wand, with Fang pressed whining against Harry’s knees, and Malfoy on the very edge of the light, as far from him as she could get without going out of it.

Her hands were shaking. He could see them in the wandlight. She had them knotted together in front of her robes and they were shaking all the same.

“You’re scared,” said Harry.

“I am not.” It came out much too high.

“You are. You’ve been scared since the gate.” He looked at her. “It isn’t the Forest, is it.”

She did not answer that. She looked at the trees, and the dark, and the dog, and anywhere at all but at him.

He knew how it felt. He had found that out this winter too, along with everything else: what it was to have a room go quiet when you came into it, and to know why. In the other life, the one that came up in bubbles, there had been a man whose name made people go grey, and Harry had hated him more than anybody in the world. He did not know what to do with being the one people went grey at. He did not want it. He would have given a great deal, standing on that path, for her to look at him the way she had looked at him in the autumn, across the flying lawn, with her chin up and her eyes on the Remembrall, wanting to win.

So he gave her something to win.

“Funny,” he said. “I always thought you lot were meant to be brave. Pure blood and all that. Turns out a Malfoy’s scared of the dark.”

That got her chin up.

“*Scared*,” said Malfoy. “Of *this*? I have walked through the woods at the Manor at midnight since I was six. We have *wolves*, Potter. Proper ones.” She drew herself up to her full height, which was an inch more than his, and looked at him for the first time in eight days, and he saw it go into her: not the fear, but the fury, pushing the fear out of the way. “You’re the one who stands about in his little circle of light. You think because you hit one troll with Weasley—”

“Hermione, mostly,” said Harry. “And it was a club.”

“Trolls,” said Malfoy, “have no sense of balance whatsoever. Everybody knows that. You could put one on its back with a well-placed rug.”

He laughed. He could not help it. It came out of him in a short surprised bark that frightened the dog, and Malfoy glared at him with such outrage, with her whole face, that he laughed again; and by the time he stopped she had come two steps into the light without noticing she had done it, and was standing near enough to argue with, which was where he wanted her.

They argued about trolls for a while. Then about brooms, which she knew more about than he did. Then about whether Hagrid was coming back, which neither of them knew, and Fang lay down across Harry’s feet and put his nose on his paws and sighed.

⁂

Fang heard it first.

He came up off Harry’s feet with his hackles up and a low terrible whine in his throat, and backed into Harry’s legs, and would not stop backing. And then Harry heard it too: something dragging, a little way off the path, on the left, among the trees, where the wandlight did not reach. And a smell, coming through the leaf-mould, cold and bright and wrong, like a coin held on the tongue.

“Stay here,” said Harry.

He went off the path. He should not have done. He knew, even as he did it, that he had done it before, and that it had gone badly, and he could not have said what had gone badly or why he kept walking. Behind him he heard Malfoy say “Potter—” and then her feet in the leaves, following.

There was a clearing. The wandlight reached across it, thin and silver, and found the unicorn.

It was lying on its side on the dead leaves. He had never seen one. He had seen one. It was so white that it hurt to look at, and its legs were bent in ways that legs did not go, and its mane spread over the ground like something spilt; and it was dead, and the silver all round it on the leaves was its blood.

And something was bent over it, drinking.

A hooded shape, on its hands and knees at the creature’s side, with its face pressed to the wound. As the light touched it, it stopped. It raised its head.

His scar split open.

It was the corridor again, the white-hot line of it across his forehead, so bad that the clearing went red at the edges and his knees went; and through it, he knew. He knew whose blood that was, and why somebody would drink it, and whose mouth was under that hood, and whose face was on the back of the head under it, and that he was eleven, and could not do one thing about any of it.

The hooded shape stood up. Silver ran down the front of it. It came across the clearing towards them, not walking, gliding, like a stain spreading.

“Run,” said Harry. His voice was not working. “Malfoy. *Run.*”

And from deep down, from the bottom of the pond, the bubble came up and broke, and he heard it again: that high thin scream, and feet crashing away between the trees, and the dog going after them, yelping. A boy with white-blond hair, running. A boy with her name.

He waited to hear her run.

She did not run.

She had come up beside him. Not behind; beside, at his shoulder, so close that her sleeve was against his. She had her wand out. It was shaking so badly that the end of it drew little circles in the air, and her face in the wandlight was as white as the unicorn, and her mouth was open and nothing was coming out of it. But she had her wand up, pointed at the thing that was coming, and her feet were planted in the leaves, and she was not going anywhere.

He did not know what she thought she was going to do with it. He did not think she knew either.

The hooded thing was very close. The pain in his head was enormous. He put his free arm out in front of her, which was a stupid thing to do and the only one he could think of—

Hooves. Behind them, coming fast, a great drumming in the ground; and something went over their heads in a leap, pale gold in the wandlight, and came down between them and the hooded shape, rearing, with its forelegs striking. The shape fell back. It went away from them across the clearing as a shadow goes when you move the lamp, and the trees took it, and it was gone.

The pain in his scar went out like a tide, and left him on his knees in the leaves with his wand still lit.

The centaur stood over them. He was a man to the waist, with white-gold hair and very blue eyes, and below the waist a palomino horse, and he looked down at Harry for a long time; at the scar.

“Harry Potter,” he said. “You should not be in this Forest. Not tonight, of all the nights there are.” His eyes moved to Malfoy, and stayed on her a moment, with an expression Harry could not read. “Nor she. Go back to Hagrid. He is on the path.”

Then he turned, and went away into the trees, and was gone as completely as the other one.

Malfoy lowered her wand. It took her two goes.

“Why,” said Harry, getting up, “didn’t you *run*?”

She looked at him. Her face was still white, but there was colour coming back into it, high on the cheekbones, and it was not fear.

“I beg your pardon?”

“I told you to run! I said *run*, and you just stood there—that thing would have—you should’ve gone back to the path and sent up sparks, that’s what you should’ve—”

“And left you there?” said Malfoy. “With *that*? So that Hagrid could come back and find you drunk out of like a—like a—” She had no word for it. She did not need one. “And you put your arm out. In front of *me*. As if I needed you to. As if *I* were the one on her knees.”

“You were shaking!”

“So were *you*!”

They stood in the clearing and shouted at each other, with the dead unicorn white on the leaves beside them and Fang howling somewhere back on the path, until Hagrid came crashing through the trees with the lantern held high and his crossbow up and an expression of such terror on his great bearded face that they both stopped at once.

He took them back to the castle at a pace that had Harry running to keep up. Nobody said anything. At the edge of the trees, where the lawn began and the lights of the castle came into view, Malfoy stopped, and turned round, and looked back into the dark.

Then she looked at Harry.

It was the look from the autumn. The flying lawn, and the Remembrall, and a girl with her chin up who wanted, more than she wanted anything, to beat him at something.

“Next time,” said Malfoy, “I go first.”

She went up the lawn ahead of him, very straight, with her cloak held tight at the throat and her wand still in her hand, and did not look back.

Harry stood at the edge of the Forest with the dog leaning on his legs and watched her go.

In the other life the boy with her name had run. He knew that now. He knew it the way he knew the rest of it, as a thing that had always been at the bottom of the pond: a scream going away between the trees, and the dog after it, and the light. He had never thought about it much, in that life. He had thought it was what Malfoys did.

She had stood next to him with her wand shaking and her mouth open and nothing coming out, and not gone anywhere.

It was the first thing, since the corridor, that he had not known was coming.
