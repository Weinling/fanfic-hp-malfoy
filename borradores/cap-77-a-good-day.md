# Chapter 83: A Good Day

There had been a second waistcoat. Nobody had known about that.

It was mustard-coloured, with a brown stripe, and it was hanging on the back of a kitchen chair at Number Twelve, Grimmauld Place, with one button loose. Harry found it on the second of August, when he came in with his trunk. He didn’t move it. Nor did Lupin. For the next three weeks they ate their meals on either side of a chair that nobody sat in, and the dog with the torn ear lay down underneath it, on the first evening, with a sigh, and put its splint on the stone.

It was his house, he’d been told. He owned the stairs, and the elves’ heads on the wall, and the long dim hall with the curtains that screamed, and a smell of cats and cold soot. He went from room to room in the first few days and looked at what he had. In the bedroom at the top somebody had left a tumbler by the bed, with a dead fly in it, and a Muggle motorbike, cut out of a magazine, on the wall. He had stood in this room at seventeen, in the other life, and found a bit of a letter; he remembered how his hands had felt. He shut the door.

Kreacher came on the fourth day.

He had been in the boiler cupboard. He came creeping up to the kitchen door at about teatime, in his filthy pillowcase, and stood looking at the floor. He’d grown very small. Harry had thought about this. He had been thinking about it, on and off, for two lives; and he got down the brown pot, and warmed it, as Hermione had shown him, and he made a cup of tea, the proper way, and put in two sugars and set it down on the bottom step in a saucer.

“I thought you might like that,” said Harry.

Kreacher looked at the cup. His great bat’s ears went slowly back against his head. He looked up at Harry, and at the pot in Harry’s hand, and his bloodshot eyes began to fill with an expression of pure and unbelieving horror, such as you might see on a bishop who has been offered a bun in the middle of a funeral.

“Master has made *tea*,” Kreacher whispered. “With his hands. For a *servant*. Oh, if my poor Mistress could see it, what the blood traitor’s brat has brought to the house of Black— He has warmed the pot! He has put the saucer on the *stair*—”

It went on for some time. Harry drank the tea himself. It was rather good.

When it was over the elf stayed where he was, twisting his long fingers in his rag. He didn’t seem to be able to go. He had something in his mouth. He got it out at last, in a hoarse small voice, to the second step.

“Master Regulus’s paper.”

Harry put down the cup.

“The bad master came through the kitchen, the last day, running. He had a letter from Mistress’s niece. He said it was found. He said that Kreacher should see it, after, he said so, he laughed at Kreacher, he said *your little master could write, you know*. Kreacher has looked. Kreacher has been in all the pockets.” He looked up. It was the first time he had ever met Harry’s eye, in either life, and there was nothing in his face but a sort of terrible hope. “Where has he put it?”

There had been a corner of old paper sticking out of a pocket. Harry had seen it. He’d been standing about a yard away.

“He had it on him,” said Harry.

He watched that go in. It took a little while. He’d told a lot of people a lot of things lately, and he found it didn’t get any easier if you did it in a kitchen. Kreacher stood quite still. Then he turned, and he went away along the passage, keeping very close to the wall, and the door of the boiler cupboard closed; and Lupin, who had been at the stove the whole time with his back to them, stirring something, went on stirring.

“He’ll want feeding,” said Lupin, after a moment. “He won’t let you. Leave it on the step.”

He was a dreadful cook. He did it every night. He’d got very grey, and he had taken to wearing the same jumper, and at seven o’clock he would put in front of Harry something brown, and sit down across from him, and they’d eat it. They didn’t talk much. The dog got what was left.

The results came on the fifth of August, by a brown owl, while Harry was looking at some toast.

There were ten lines, in purple, with a Ministry crest. He’d got an O in Defence Against the Dark Arts, and in Herbology, for some reason. He had got an E in Potions, and he read that twice. He went on down. He got to the last line but one. He looked at it, and something odd began to happen in the region of his chest, and he put the paper down, with care, and got his hand over his mouth.

*History of Magic: D.*

It wasn’t even the right sort of D. He had got as far as question nine. He’d spelt Bagnold. He had done the Goblin Rebellion of 1612 in three neat causes; he had been looking forward to that paper for four and a half years, and he had left it in the middle of a sentence, with a line trailing off the *d* of *would*, and gone to the Ministry of Magic, and somewhere in a cold office an old wizard with an egg-timer had totted up what there was and given him exactly what it came to. The last time that he had done this exam, in another life, he’d got a D as well.

He hadn’t made a noise. It came out through his nose. His shoulders had started. The dog got up from under the chair and came over and looked at him, with its head on one side; and Lupin came in from the scullery with a dishcloth, and stopped in the doorway. It was the first time that either of them had heard anything of the kind in that kitchen since June.

“Harry?”

He held it out. He couldn’t speak. He had tears running down behind his glasses. She had said that, she’d said it on a rug in February: she had laughed so much that she got a stitch. *You’ve had two goes at this subject*, she had said, *and you were present for some of it*. She’d have liked to see this. She’d have had it framed. He sat there with his hand across his mouth, and it went on coming up, and it stopped being funny at some point, and he couldn’t have said when. Lupin put the dishcloth over his shoulder and sat down in the chair next to him. He didn’t ask.

Moody came that Friday, down the area steps, in a filthy temper and a borrowed hat.

“Wiltshire,” he said.

Harry had been expecting it. He’d had nine days to get ready.

They had taken over the back kitchen. Moody sat down on the coal bunker with his bad leg out in front of him and the blue eye going round the walls, and he took a pull at his flask. He’d had six people over that field, he said. Kingsley had been on his hands and knees. There wasn’t a thing there, not a stone, and there were sixty acres on the Ordnance Survey that nobody could make walk straight; you could set off across them with a compass and find you had been thinking about something else. Malfoy Manor had been standing on that spot since the Conquest. It wasn’t there now. Somebody had put it in their pocket. He had one lad who’d been sitting in a hedge in Little Whinging, on the night of the thirty-first, and been run over by a Firebolt. So he’d like to know how Harry had got on to it.

“I had a ring,” said Harry.

“I know you did. Got my hand on the pair of ’em, once. I’ve wondered about that.” The dark eye had settled on him. “Whose was that, on your birthday? Was that her sending? Or was that somebody sending with her hand?”

“It went red. It does that if she’s in danger. It doesn’t do words.”

“And you went.”

“Yeah.”

“I’ll tell you what I heard, Potter.” He screwed the top on the flask. “I had Crouch by the neck, in the Atrium, when they brought that line past. I was as close to Malfoy as I am to this bunker. *By four o’clock, as I asked. You may tell my daughter it was very well done.* There were three of us heard that. I saw that girl on the platform. I saw old Nott bow. I’ve seen ’em do that before, to one or two people, in the last war, and I went to the funerals. Now then. You’ve known her since you were eleven. What did he mean?”

The eye was very blue, and it wasn’t on his face. He could feel it going down his left side, in a thoughtful way, and stopping at his ankle, at a very good dark grey sock.

He had thought about what you did. It was all there, ready to hand. He had been in a room with Lucius Malfoy, on the night he’d lost everything he had. He’d seen him smile. Harry let his shoulders go down. He let himself look tired, which took no effort; and he met the dark eye and he held it, with a slight sour twist to his mouth, that he had taken some trouble over.

“He meant me to hear it,” said Harry. “That’s all. He was going down, and I’d got his daughter once, and he was looking for something to leave in. He’s been doing it since I was eleven. Ask Hermione. Malfoy stood in front of that fire and told me not to go. She had her wand on me. I had to put her on the floor to get past. She told me it was a trap, in front of a witness; if that was the plan to get me out of the castle, it’s the worst one I’ve ever heard.” He shrugged. “She’s got to live there. Her dad says she’s a heroine. What d’you want her to do, tell them he’s wrong?”

It was all true. That was what he’d found. You could build a whole house out of that, if you only took care about the corners. He sat on an upturned bucket with his hands in his pockets, and he looked Alastor Moody in the eye and did it for her, the entire thing, without a crack; and it was very well done. He had no idea, himself, whether he believed a word.

Moody said nothing for some time. He sucked his teeth.

“You’re good at that,” he said. “Aren’t you? Better than you were.” He got up, a section at a time. “I used to like you, Potter. I liked a boy who walked into a trunk.”

Neville arrived on the fifteenth, out of the kitchen fire, backwards, carrying a pot.

He had a small suitcase, and a clean collar, and he stood on the hearth coughing and apologising to Lupin. His gran had said he could. She had read something about Harry in the *Prophet*, and she’d said it was a disgrace, and that a boy of that age shouldn’t be rattling round a house with a werewolf, no offence, and that he could go till Thursday if he wiped his feet. His jaw had set a bit crooked. It gave him a thoughtful look. He had the list from the drawer in his suitcase, and he’d been making notes on it; he had worked out that you could have the third-years on the Thursday if you moved Dean. The grey thing had put out two new stalks. He stood it on the dresser, where it could look at the wall, and within an hour it had eaten a tea towel, and on the Friday the two of them took it up to the first-floor landing and held it in front of the curtains, as an experiment.

There was a very great deal of screaming. *BLOOD TRAITORS! FILTH! ABOMINATIONS OF—* It broke off. There was a thick crunching sound, and a long, outraged gurgle. A fair-sized piece of black velvet went slowly down the plant’s throat, and it sat there, looking pleased. Neville had gone pink to the ears. “Oh, I say,” said Neville. “Sorry. I’m so sorry,” and he and Harry looked at each other, and then at the hole in the curtain, through which one furious yellowed eye was glaring out; and Harry had to go and sit on the stairs.

That was the second laugh since June, he thought. He’d got into the way of counting.

Neville came to Diagon Alley with him on the Monday, because somebody had to. It wasn’t what it had been. A good third of the shops had got boards up, and the rest had a lot of purple posters on their windows, about what to do in the event of. Florean Fortescue’s had a plank across the door, and someone had written a word on it; but they had left the little iron tables out on the terrace, because who’d take them, and Harry sat down at one in a thin bright sun, with his book list, because it was where he had once sat and looked at a girl across the road. He told himself it was to rest his leg. Neville sat opposite and didn’t ask him which.

He heard her before he saw her. It was only heels. He’d have known them on the moon.

There was a small boy coming down the middle of the Alley, about ten paces ahead of anybody, in a pale grey suit. He had a clean collar and a bow tie, and he walked quite straight, looking neither to the left nor the right. People moved aside for him without seeming to know why. Behind him came Theodore Nott, with a bag. It was a lady’s bag, with a silver clasp; he carried that as he’d have carried a writ. And behind Nott, in no kind of hurry, with a parasol, in a long-sleeved summer dress of the very palest green, there was Lyra Malfoy, buying nothing.

She looked very well. She’d put something on her face. There were two or three people with her who’d stopped to bow. He hadn’t seen her for eighteen days.

He had a sock on. He’d worn it since the first of August, on the left foot, with a thing of Dudley’s on the right, and he had told nobody. His hands had gone into his pockets, as if they’d heard a whistle.

She had seen him. She didn’t turn her head. She’d have seen him from the far end of the street. She came along the front of the terrace, with the sun on her, and the small party went on in front, and just level with his table she stopped to look into the window of what had been a hat shop, at nothing, with the handle of the parasol against her shoulder. She was holding it in her right hand. The left was on the clasp of a little glove-case, at her waist, and it stayed there.

“Why, Potter,” said Lyra. It carried very nicely, to the two women at the next window. “They’ve let you out. I do hope that you’ve been given a muzzle. I hear that you bite Aurors now.”

That was the script. It wasn’t on any bit of paper. It was what they did. He knew his line.

“Only the ones who’ve been at your dad’s.” He looked her up and down. He leaned back, so that the iron chair creaked; he was aware of Neville, across the table, going very slowly red. “Nice parasol. Did they let you have that in the cell, or is it just for the visits?”

Somebody drew in a breath. It was a good one, and it would be round the shops by lunch. She turned her head at last and looked at him, and the corner of her mouth lifted by about a sixteenth of an inch, as he’d seen it do in the middle of a duel. It was every bit of it perfect, the same as it had been in January, when they’d rehearsed it, on their stomachs, with a sheet of paper, and he had laughed until he got the hiccups. Her eyes were on his. There wasn’t anybody in them.

He didn’t know why he said it. It wasn’t in the script.

“How’s your brother?” said Harry.

The sun went on shining. Ten paces down the Alley a very small straight back had stopped by itself and was waiting, not looking round, as you’d wait for a bus.

For about as long as it takes to blink, the parasol did not move at all.

“He had an egg,” said Lyra.

She inclined her head to him, by a quarter of an inch. She walked on. Nott shifted the bag from one hand to the other, and he went by the table with his long dull careful face; he glanced down at the sock, and then away.

Neville was looking at his hands. After a while he reached across, without saying anything, and moved the book list a little way along the table, so that it wasn’t in the wet.

The fire went up green at twenty to eleven on the Thursday night, and it was Ginny.

He was by himself. Lupin had gone north for the Order, with a sandwich, and Neville had gone home, and he had the long kitchen table to himself, with the lamp turned down and the Potions list, and was doing something with a cold cup. The hearth coughed. She stepped out on to the stone, brushed the soot off one shoulder, and stood with her hands by her sides, looking at him, in a Muggle anorak, with her hair tied back. She had grown. He’d thought of her, all summer, as about eleven.

The dog got up and went to her. She put a hand down, not looking, and let it smell that.

“Mum thinks I’m at Luna’s,” said Ginny. “Luna thinks I’m looking for something. I’ve got about an hour. Don’t get up.”

He hadn’t. He found he couldn’t have. She pulled out a chair, not the one with the waistcoat. She looked at it, for a moment, as she went by. She sat down across from him and set her elbows on the table, and laced her fingers. They had ink on them. She had very direct eyes. He had thought about them once a good deal, in another life, and it hadn’t helped.

“Ron told me,” she said. “In July. It took him a month. He came and sat on the end of my bed at the Burrow, and he told me all about it, with his ears going; he’d got it written on his hand. I asked Hermione, and she didn’t say it wasn’t so. She wrote back four lines. So you needn’t do your face. I know about the other time, I know about my first year, and I know you were standing next to me in Flourish and Blotts. I want to ask you three things. Then I’m going home.”

“All right,” said Harry.

His mouth had gone dry. He had talked to Dumbledore across that desk, and to Riddle’s long white hand, and it seemed that he had been saving this up.

“How long before?”

“Before what?”

“Don’t.”

He looked at the cup. “Since I was eleven,” he said. “Since I got here. I knew it was your dad’s cauldron and I knew which day. I knew what was in the book. I was standing there, I could have knocked it out of your hand. I’d got a whole year to do something in.” It came out flat, and rather quick. “I needed the fang. There’s a— I needed something that was down there. There wasn’t another way to get it. I needed it to happen, so I let it.”

She didn’t move. It went across her face and came back, like wind across a pool. She nodded, once, as you’d tick something.

“Right,” said Ginny. “That’s what Ron said. I wanted to hear you do it.”

Water was dripping somewhere in the scullery.

“Second. In the other one. You had it all happen once before you did it to me. You saw me, after. You’d have seen me for years.” Her voice was perfectly level. “Was I all right?”

He opened his mouth.

He had an answer. He’d been going to say yes. He had the words ready, it was what you would say. *You were fine. You were brilliant. You played Chaser, and you were the best in the D.A. You were funny, and everybody—* There was a hot wet stone, and a girl on it, in another life; and then there had been a Ginny who laughed at things, who’d had a Bat-Bogey Hex. He’d gone out with her for a bit. He looked for the thing that came after that. He went along the whole of it, six years, from one end to the other. He had sat next to her at breakfast. He had kissed her, in front of the entire common room, and he had thought it was the best thing that had ever happened. He’d been told, he remembered, that she’d been a bit quiet, her second year.

“I don’t know,” said Harry.

It was hardly any sound at all.

“I never asked,” he said.

She looked at him for rather a long time.

“No,” said Ginny. “I didn’t think you had.”

She unlaced her fingers, and looked at the ink on them. She rubbed it with her thumb.

“I burn my essays,” she said. “That’s another thing you didn’t ask. I write it all out, whatever it is. It might be Charms. Then I go over it with a pencil, every letter, to see if I know the hand. Then I burn it, so that it can’t answer. I’ve done that for three years. I’m getting O’s, you’ll be glad to hear. Hermione knows. Did she tell you? No, she wouldn’t. She came and sat with me after, when I was eleven, when she came out of the Hospital Wing. She didn’t say anything. She held my hand, every night, for a fortnight, in the dormitory. She’d read somewhere that you ought to. She’d have been thirteen. I thought she was the only person in that whole school who—” It stopped. She breathed in through her nose. “She told me, in the four lines, what she’s known and how long for. It was two years too late to be part of it. So I’ve still got the fortnight. I’ve told her what that was.”

He couldn’t have said anything if you’d paid him.

“Third,” said Ginny.

She didn’t go on at once. She had stopped looking at his face. He didn’t know when she had done that. She was looking at his right arm. He’d got it down beside him, under the edge of the table, with the left hand folded round it, a little way above the wrist. He’d been sitting like that for some time. He had been sitting like that since about June, in chairs, and he looked down and saw it; and he took the hand away.

“I did that,” she said slowly. “For a year. I used to hold on to it, so that I’d know where it was.” Her eyes came up. They were very bright and rather pale, and they’d stopped being sixteen, or fifteen, or anything else. “Hermione saw something. At the Ministry, when she came up after you. That was the other thing in the four lines. *Ask him about the fountain.* I don’t think she meant to put it. What happened in the Atrium?”

No one had asked him that. Dumbledore had asked him what he’d said. He had told Dumbledore a very good lie.

He looked at the mustard waistcoat, on its chair. There was nobody else in the house. It was a quarter past eleven, and he hadn’t laughed for a while. He could feel his heart, going slowly and hard. And he thought, with a dull surprise, that there was one person alive who could not be told anything that would make her think worse of him. It would be like putting down a heavy bag on somebody else’s floor.

So he told her. He hadn’t told anybody. He told it to the lamp: that he’d run up the stairs after Bellatrix Lestrange because he had decided to, and that he wanted that clear; and how it had been in the gold room, and then that it had stopped being him. His arm had come up by itself, as if it had been lifted by someone who knew the way. He told her there was a word, and what it had done to a woman’s face with half of it unsaid. Then he told her the worst of it. There had been a voice, very quiet, that wasn’t Voldemort’s and wasn’t his. It had come from just here, behind his right ear, and it had said one word, and it had sounded so pleased.

He put up his hand and touched the place. It didn’t feel like anything.

“*Mine*,” said Harry. “That’s all it said. And then I thought, that’s probably just me. It’s the sort of thing that I’d think. It didn’t sound like— It’s not that it’s horrible. It’s the opposite. I don’t know what I’m— I was on the floor, and I’d just cut somebody’s face open, and Sirius was dead. And I heard that, and it was the first thing that had sounded all right.”

He stopped.

Ginny had not moved. He looked up. He had seen her ride a broom through a Bludger, and he’d seen her win a Sickle off Fred, and he had never in his life seen anyone sit so still. All the colour had gone from her. Her freckles stood out on it one at a time. She looked at the side of his head, a little behind the ear. She looked at it as you’d look down a well.

The dog had started to whine, under the chair, very softly.

He thought she might be going to get up. He thought she might be sick. He’d have liked somebody to tell him what he’d just done. When her voice came it was quite small and quite clear, and there wasn’t any kindness in it at all.

“It doesn’t sound like somebody else,” said Ginny. “That’s what nobody tells you. It sounds like you on a good day.”
