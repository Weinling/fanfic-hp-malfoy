# Chapter 78: Mine

There was a notice on the fountain.

It was propped against the golden wizard’s foot, on a little brass easel, in the gilded emptiness of the Atrium: *THE MINISTRY OF MAGIC IS CLOSED TODAY FOR THE TREATMENT OF DOXY INFESTATION. Staff are thanked for their patience.* The fires had burnt down along both walls. Nobody was at the desk. It was twenty past four on a Tuesday afternoon, and you could hear the water falling from the end of the witch’s wand, a long way off, in the dark.

“Doxies,” said Neville faintly.

“Somebody paid for that,” said Cedric. He had his wand out and a Shield half made. He looked up at the long peacock-blue ceiling and along the row of dead hearths. “Somebody’s sent nine hundred people home. Potter, this is about the most obvious—”

“I know,” said Harry.

He was already walking. He didn’t need the signs. He took them through the golden gates and into the lift, and he put his thumb on the button at the bottom of the panel, and when the cool voice told him where he was, he got out. There was the bare passage, and the plain black door. It swung open at the sight of him, like the door of somebody’s house.

The round room spun, and stopped. He didn’t wait. He crossed it and put his hand on the fourth door along, and went through.

It was the same. There were the stone benches going down in their tiers, on all sides, to the pit; and there in the middle, on its raised place, stood the arch, old and cracked and holding up nothing, with the ragged black curtain stirring in it as if someone had just gone through. The air tasted of cold stone. He could hear people whispering. He had heard them last time. He’d never told anyone.

At the bottom of the steps, across the foot of the arch, lay something big and black.

He went down those benches three at a time. He didn’t feel his feet. He heard Hermione say his name. He dropped on his knees on the dais and got both hands into the rough wet fur, and it lifted its head, and looked at him. It had brown eyes, a torn ear, and a bit of frayed rope round its neck. It thumped its tail on the stone, once, hopefully. It was a dog.

It was just a dog. It was some old stray that somebody had picked up off a street, with its leg broken.

Up on the benches, in the dimness, a single person started to clap.

They were sitting all round the room. They must have been there the whole time, on every side, in the tiers, quite still, with their hands on their knees, like people who have come early to get good seats; and now there were lights, at the ends of a dozen wands, going on one after another. There was Lucius Malfoy, standing, thinner than Harry had ever seen him, with his cane under his arm. There was Bellatrix Lestrange on the top step with her finger in her mouth, exactly as she’d been in the dream.

“Oh, *look*,” she said. “He’s found it! Isn’t he sweet?”

The one who had been clapping stopped. He got up from the second bench, and came down a few steps into the light. He was a slight, sallow man of about thirty-five, with straw-coloured hair, and he had a way of running the tip of his tongue along his upper lip and then looking mildly surprised, as if somebody else had done it.

Harry had last seen that tongue in another life, behind Alastor Moody’s face.

“We haven’t met,” said Barty Crouch. His voice was light and dry and pleasant. “Which is rude of me; I’ve had such a lot of you. You did very well in the maze. I watched you stand on that podium with the Cup in your arms, looking at it. You kept on looking at it. Waiting for it to do something.” The tongue came out, and went in. “Whatever did you think it was going to do? It brought you back exactly where it was meant to. We were waiting for the crate.”

Nobody spoke. The curtain moved.

*That creature. It drew blood?*

He had stood there. He’d stood with his leg on fire and watched a dead-faced man carry a Skrewt away in a box, with his blood on its sting; and he had gone on looking at the Cup, because he’d known what happened next.

He got up. He took his hands out of the dog’s fur, and turned round, and there were Neville and Cedric and Hermione, on the steps behind him, back to back, with wands pointing out of all sides of them. Someone had come down and stood behind them. He looked at Hermione’s face, and it didn’t have anything on it.

“Where is he?” said Harry.

“Who, dear?” said Bellatrix, and shook with noiseless laughter.

“A small matter first,” said Lucius Malfoy.

He had got very old. He told it quite briefly: there was a room next door, with a great many shelves. There was a little glass ball on the ninety-seventh row. Nobody could lift it from the shelf but the person it concerned. If Mr Potter would be so good as to fetch it, there was no reason that these three young people should not go home in time for supper. His eyes rested on Harry’s, pale and tired and very clear, and for an instant, with a horrible shock, Harry saw whose they were.

Somebody would come. Ron had run. It would take them an hour. He had got that far, and it was the last thing he had.

So he did what he was good at.

He’d never worked so hard. He talked. “I know what’s in it,” he said, “and so do you, Lucius, or you’d have sent somebody else. D’you want the first line?” He told them the first line, and he saw three heads turn. “How long d’you think you’ll last, once it’s out of the glass?” He asked Rookwood what he’d been promised, by name, across the room. He said he’d fetch it for Neville but not for Cedric. He changed his mind. He made Bellatrix scream with rage, and he got two of them out of the row, and he had them walking him by inches to the door and along an aisle of dim blue lights with the small glass globes going by on either side. He knew who would look at whom. He got twenty-five minutes. He got thirty. He stood at last in front of a shelf with a yellowed label on it, with Bellatrix’s wand under Neville’s ear; he reached up, and his fingers closed on it, and it was warm. He had wondered what that would be like.

Lucius held out his hand. It was a long, fine, beautiful hand. It had a bad tremor.

“Thank you,” said Lucius.

Harry looked at the hand. He thought of a cauldron, in a bookshop. He looked at that for a moment. He opened his fingers.

It hit the stone with hardly any sound. A small pale shape got up out of the bits, in a shawl, and said something in a hollow voice, and nobody could hear what it was, because Bellatrix Lestrange had begun to shriek.

He didn’t have it all, afterwards. He had pieces. He had Cedric’s Shield going up, huge and silver, and holding, and holding, while the shelves came down on either side in a long roar of glass and a hundred small voices started talking at once; and a jet of purple that took Cedric in the chest, and his face going surprised and young. He dragged him by the collar. Hermione had got hold of Neville. Neville was on his knees with his teeth together and his nose running blood, and he had got his wand up; he was saying “*Stupefy*, *Stupefy*,” in a mild, thick, determined voice at Alice Longbottom’s torturer, and missing, and she was laughing so hard she could scarcely stand. Then she flicked one finger. He heard the sound Neville made. They went backwards through a door, all in a heap, and it was the wrong door, or the right one. There were the stone benches. They went down them. He had a dead weight in one hand and a fistful of Hermione in the other; the old dog was struggling up on its three legs, barking; there were eleven of them, coming down the tiers on all sides with their wands up, in no hurry; and he stood on the dais with his back to the arch, and he had thought of everything. There wasn’t anything left that he had not thought of.

High up behind them, at the top of the room, the door blew off its hinges.

He saw Moody first, stumping down through the smoke with both his eyes going. He saw Tonks and Lupin. He saw Kingsley Shacklebolt, and a jet of light that took Rookwood clean off the bench. And he saw, coming over the top of the tiers at a run, leaping from step to step with his long hair flying and his wand in his hand, in a Muggle waistcoat of a violent shade of plum, with the buttons done up wrong and his trousers wet to the knee, a man who was roaring with laughter.

“*Forty-one sheep!*” bawled Sirius Black. “You little *liar!* Forty-one sheep and a hill! Get behind me, you horrible boy!”

He was all right.

He was completely all right. He hadn’t got a scratch on him. He came down the last of the benches in two bounds and hit the dais with a crash, and got one arm round Harry’s neck and shook him, hard, and let him go; and he smelt of salt. There was sand on his boots. His face was burnt red across the nose and there was a bit of dried seaweed on his cuff, and his eyes, Harry saw, were swollen, as though he had been out in a wind, or doing something else, not long ago; and there was a corner of old paper sticking out of his waistcoat pocket.

He stared at it. His mouth had come open.

“Did you have a nice day at the seaside, cousin?” called Bellatrix Lestrange.

She had come down to the bottom tier. She was skipping a little from foot to foot, with her wand trailing. “Cissy’s so sentimental! All those years, and she goes and writes to you about *him*. She really ought to be more careful who she lends her wax to.”

The seaside.

There was a square of pale pink carpet. He saw it. He saw it very clearly, with the green light on it, and a hand held out to him with a ring on it, and a mouth stopped half open round the beginning of a word.

*I know he’s gone. That’s what I’m— Oh, will you listen. It’s my—*

*Potter, he’s with my—*

It took him about a second. He would have liked it to take longer. He stood in the middle of the Death Chamber with the battle going past on both sides of him, and something very cold went in under his ribs, and all the way through.

Then Sirius had shouted, and gone by him, and it was the eighteenth of June.

He knew what this was. He had cleaned his teeth with it every morning for four and a half years. There was the top step of the dais, and there was Sirius on it, fighting his cousin, ducking under a red jet and bouncing up again, flushed and delighted, with his teeth showing. He was shouting something at her. He’d been shut up in a house for months. He looked twenty-five. He looked as he’d looked with his boots on a table, telling you a story about somebody else.

No, thought Harry.

He had a clear view. That was what was so extraordinary. It had been the left, last time; he had been down at the foot, a long way off, with something in his hands. He wasn’t now. He was here, he was four steps away and he’d been here for years. It wasn’t the same angle, and they weren’t on the same step, and it didn’t matter. He knew that arm. He saw her wrist turn over, and he moved before he had worked out how. He went up those steps with everything he had, with his wand arm out and every Shield that he had ever learnt coming up in his mouth, and he got in front. He was there. He was standing in the place; he had his back against that plum-coloured waistcoat, and he was looking straight along her wand. For the first time in two lives there was somebody between Sirius Black and the end of that sentence, and it was him.

A hand took him in the chest.

It was a big, hard, warm hand, and it came round from behind him and shoved. He went sideways. He hadn’t been expecting it. Nobody had been expecting it. He saw Sirius’s face go past him, a foot away, tipped back and laughing, with the whole of his life in it and his eyes alight. He looked immensely pleased with him.

“Good one, Prongs,” said Sirius.

Then the light hit him.

It got him high up. His eyebrows rose a little, as if he’d been told something quite interesting. He stood for a moment with the laugh still on his face; and he took one step back, and another, like a man who has been asked to dance; and his shoulders touched the curtain, and it gave. It moved aside for him as you’d move aside for someone in a doorway. It fluttered, and hung still.

He didn’t come out at the other side. There was the arch, and the air, and nobody.

The curtain stirred a little in the wind that wasn’t blowing.

Somebody was shouting his name. Somebody had got him round the chest, from behind, with both arms, and it hurt; Lupin’s voice was saying, “No. Harry. There’s nothing you can do. Harry, he’s gone,” and it was just the same. He’d said that before, in the same voice. They were the same words. He had moved the entire board, for four and a half years, and the man had looked him in the face and called him by somebody else’s name, and gone through.

*Prongs.*

He had stopped shouting. He didn’t remember doing that. He stopped pulling against the arms. He felt them loosen, a little, in relief.

High up on the benches, somebody laughed, a thin, breathless, delighted peal of it, and he heard her feet going away up the steps.

He went after her.

He didn’t feel anything. That was quite interesting. He got out of Lupin’s arms by doing something to his elbow. He went up through the tiers with people falling down on either side of him, and through the spinning room; he knew the doors, he went along the bare passage, and he came out into the great gold dim length of the Atrium with his breath sawing in his chest; and she was halfway down it, skipping, by the fountain, looking back at him over her shoulder.

“I did it!” sang Bellatrix Lestrange. “Oh, did you see? He *laughed!* Have you come to cry, little Potter? Have you come to—”

His arm came up.

He would have told you he didn’t lift it. It rose, from the shoulder, very smooth and level, with no weight in it at all. It didn’t shake. It was the same as it had been on a landing, and in a pink room. There was somebody standing just behind him. There was a word in his mouth. He knew that word. He had got it out of a book, at sixteen, in the other life, and tried it on a boy in a bathroom, and stood in the red water looking at what it did; and he had sworn. It came up out of the floor of him, up a long dark groove that had been there all the time, and it had a taste, like a coin, and it knew exactly where it was going. It had been waiting since a corridor when he was eleven.

“*Sec*—” said Harry.

It wasn’t a loud noise. There was no flash. Bellatrix Lestrange stopped in the middle of a skip, with her head on one side. Then a line opened on her face, from the temple to the corner of the mouth, as neat as the crease in a letter, and it began to run.

He stood with his hand out. He couldn’t get the rest of it out. It stayed there, on his tongue, half said.

She had gone down on one knee. She put up her long dirty fingers, and touched it, and looked at them. She looked at him. And slowly, with the blood going down her neck, her whole face lit up. She looked as she had looked at a peacock. She got to her feet, and she began, in a high soft crooning voice, to laugh.

“Oh, you little *beauty*,” said Bellatrix.

*Mine.*

He heard it quite clearly. It wasn’t her. It wasn’t anyone. It was in his head, a little behind his right ear, and it was very quiet and rather pleased. It didn’t sound like him. It didn’t sound altogether like the voice that had said *Come and see*. He thought, afterwards, that he must have thought of it himself. It was the sort of thing you would think.

What frightened him was how good it sounded.

The rest of it happened to someone else. The fires went up green along both walls of the Atrium, one after another. A tall old man in midnight blue walked out from the golden gates and said “Good evening, Tom,” to somebody who had come in behind Harry and was looking down at him from a great height with a light, courteous interest. The statues came to life. There was a great deal of noise. He lay against the rim of the fountain, where someone had put him, with wet robes, and looked at it. He didn’t turn his head. There was a moment when a long white hand came into his view, and the light changed, and the whole of the Ministry seemed to be pressing on him from the inside, and he heard himself say, politely, “Not today,” and it went away. There was a lime-green bowler hat rolling on the floor.

They brought them up from below in a line, with their wands gone and their hands bound. Barty Crouch went by, with his tongue moving, looking at nothing. Moody had him by the neck. Behind him came Lucius Malfoy, between two Aurors, with his hair in his eyes and blood on his collar, walking very straight. He had lost his cane. He stopped when he drew level with the fountain. He looked down.

He smiled. It was a small, weary, beautiful smile, and it seemed to give him a great deal of trouble. He raised his voice just enough to reach the people behind him in the line.

“By four o’clock,” said Lucius Malfoy, “as I asked. You may tell my daughter it was very well done.”

They took him on. Harry watched him go, down the long gold floor, with his back straight, until the fire closed round him.

He could not think what he had meant. He didn’t try. He thought about it a good deal, later.

They mended Neville’s jaw on a bench. Tonks came up with the dog in her arms, and it lay on her cloak with a splint on its leg, looking hopefully at everybody. Somebody said that Diggory would be all right. Hermione had a burn all down one arm. She sat by herself on the steps of the fountain, with a blanket that a witch from the Ministry had put round her, and he looked up and found her eyes on him. She had seen the fountain. She’d come up after him, in time. She didn’t get out her notebook. She turned away and looked at the water.

It got to be after three. Dumbledore was sitting on the rim beside him, looking very old, with his hands between his knees. He’d been saying something. He had said it twice. There was a fire lit, he said, when Harry was ready. Harry could go back through to the school. He needn’t talk, if he would rather not.

He felt in his pocket for his glasses. They were on his face. He had thought he might want a handkerchief, because something appeared to be the matter with his nose. He didn’t have a handkerchief. His fingers closed on something else, long and thin, of dark smooth wood. He drew it out.

It lay across his palm, in the gold light. He had held it like this in a corridor, when he was eleven.

*I won’t be long.*

He sat there with the wand in his hand. Over by the gates, a clock had begun to strike.

“Harry?” said Dumbledore.

She could hear the kittens.

There were twenty-three of them. She had plenty of time to establish that. They couldn’t be seen, from the carpet, but they mewed at slightly different pitches, and if you listened long enough you could tell them apart. Somewhere above her left ear was one with a bow on it that had a catch in its voice. The fire had burnt down to nothing. The light went across the ceiling, very slowly, from a warm bar to a long grey shape. It was the only thing that moved.

She found out a number of things about the Full Body-Bind. You could breathe. It was just shallow enough to be unpleasant. Your eyes stayed open. Nobody ever mentioned that. They dried, and stung, and then they ran, and the water went sideways into your hair and into your ears; there wasn’t a thing that you could do about it, and after some hours they stopped running, and it felt as if someone had laid two hot pennies on them. She couldn’t blink. There was a pin missing from the left side of her head. Her hands had been placed very straight at her sides.

He’d done that. She lay and looked at it, in the only direction that there was.

She finished the word. She had been finishing it for some time. It was a perfectly ordinary word and it had two syllables. It went round. *Mother. He’s with my mother. Mother.* She’d had it right there at the front of her teeth. She’d only looked at Granger for a moment. She had only stopped to get her breath, and to think how it could be said, in front of Granger; to think what it would cost. She had looked at that for a moment, as she had been taught.

Granger had stood over her, for some time, with the powder running out of her hand. *It wears off*, he had told her. It didn’t, and Granger had known that since she was eleven. Then there had been a green light. Lyra found she had no complaint to make. There was a boy in Hufflepuff who could have told her what that was worth.

The ring was on the fourth finger of her left hand. She couldn’t see it. She could feel it. It had been hot for a long time, hotter and hotter, like something being held against a stove, and at a certain moment, while the light was still on the wall, it had stopped being anything. It had gone out. She didn’t know what that meant. She lay and waited for it to come on, and after a while it came on, very faint, and it had gone cold.

That wasn’t what she thought about. She thought about that as little as she could. She had a room for it.

What she thought about was his face.

She’d had it above her, at a distance of about a foot. He had been so careful. He had caught her. She kept coming back to that: she had been going over on to the iron, and his arm had come round behind her head, as quick as you please. He’d got her clear. He had looked at her hands and they were wrong, and he’d put them right. And then he had bent down, so that she should be in no doubt about anything, and he had smiled at her.

She knew that smile.

She had known it at once. It had taken her some little while to think from where; she’d had the leisure. It had been in a wardrobe, in the staff room, with a smell of mothballs, and she had been thirteen. It had been patient and quite cold, and there hadn’t been any hurry in it at all. It had looked as if it had all the time in the world, and already knew how everything would end.

She had hoped very much that she never would.

*Stay there*, he had said.

Well. She was. She didn’t see that anybody could fault her on that.

The grey shape on the ceiling went to blue. There were a great many feet, a long way away, and voices on a stair, and a door slamming. Nobody came. Nobody ever came to that room. He’d been right about that. She had been lying there for a very long time, and she found she knew exactly how long; she had always been able to do it, without a clock. She knew what you did with your hands. She lay on her back on the pink carpet, with her eyes wide open, eleven years old, and listened to the corridor.

*Please come.*

There were footsteps.

They were slow and rather heavy. They stopped, and they came on, and went away; they came back. It wasn’t a person who was in a hurry. It was a person who had tried every other door on that floor. The handle went down. The door moved, and someone stood there for a while, breathing through her nose.

A square dark shape came into the top of what she could see, and looked down.

Crabbe didn’t say anything. Her big plain face did not alter. She must have been looking since four. She had got cobwebs on one shoulder and her shoes were wet, as though she had been down to the lake. She took in the carpet. She took in the eyes, and the hands. She stood and looked at the hands, very straight at the sides, for about as long as she had once looked at a wand. Then she took out her own, and she got down on her knees, carefully, and said the counter-curse in her low reasonable voice.

It was like being dropped. Everything came back at once, and it all hurt. Lyra’s eyes shut. She couldn’t have stopped them; they closed by themselves, and she lay with her whole body shaking and something coming out of her that wasn’t quite a sound. A hand went under her neck. There was a cup against her teeth. Crabbe had brought water. She had been carrying it about with her, in a flask, on all those floors.

She drank some. She got her eyes open, a little at a time. The room was full of a thin blue dusk, and it hurt.

“What’s the time?” she said. It came out as a sort of creak.

“Ten past nine.”

“Five hours,” said Lyra. “And ten minutes.”

Crabbe held the cup. She didn’t look at her. She looked at a point on the wall, about a foot to the left, among the kittens.

“This isn’t anything,” said Crabbe.

“No,” said Lyra.

“They’ve brought Diggory back. I saw Madam Pomfrey go down. Nobody knows anything. They’re saying the Ministry.” She screwed the top on the flask. It took her a moment. She got up, and she stood with it in both hands, a thickset girl with a cobweb on her shoulder. “Are you coming down?”

Lyra looked at the fireplace. It was cold and swept-looking, with a small drift of grey on the hearth, and a long pin lying on the fender, side by side with the poker, exactly straight.

She got one hand under her, and then the other. It took some time. She got as far as her knees, and then she took hold of the edge of the fender, and stood up, on the pale square of carpet, and she stayed standing. She took down what was left of her hair and put it up again. There was only the one pin to spare, but she had been very well taught.

“No, thank you,” she said.

Crabbe looked at her. Then she nodded, once, and went out, and left the door as she had found it.

It was a little before five when the fire came on.

She was standing with her back to the window. She hadn’t sat. There was a chair, but she had thought about that, and she didn’t care to be found in it. It had got light behind her, a clean flat colourless light, and the kittens had all fallen asleep. There was a thin sound from the chimney, as of someone breathing in. A small tongue of green ran up from the ashes, and stood, and spread.

He stepped out over the fender.

She had expected, she supposed, that he’d look different. He didn’t look like anything. He was in his exam robes, torn at one shoulder and stiff with something down the front, and he had one sock. His glasses had been mended with something. His lip was split. He stood on the hearth with his arms hanging and looked at the floor, at the pale square of the carpet, where there was nobody. She saw him look at it. She saw his chest stop.

Then he raised his eyes.

Neither of them said anything. The fire went down behind him with a small dry sigh. He looked at her, standing there against the light with her hair up and her hands by her sides. She saw him look for the word, and she saw that there wasn’t one. He had a wand in his hand. He’d had it in his hand, she thought, for some time. He crossed the carpet, and it took four steps. He held it out to her, across both his palms, like somebody returning a thing he had found in the road.

She took it. It was warm.

She waited. He opened his mouth; it took him two attempts. He had no voice left at all.

“Sirius,” said Harry.

She looked at him. She didn’t need to ask it. She had looked at him across a great many rooms. She looked now, and she asked, with her eyes wide open; they still hurt; and he stood in front of her, and looked back, and he told her.

There was a long silence.

The light grew. A long way down, somebody had opened a door to let the morning in, and there was a smell of wet grass. She stood with her wand in her hand and her fingers quite steady. She had thought of a very great number of things that she might say to him, on that carpet, in the blue dusk; she had put them in order. There were some that would have taken the skin off him. There was one, she thought, that would have let him lie down. She looked at his split mouth, and the one sock. She looked at him for rather a long time, and she found that all of it had gone, and there was just the end of a sentence, that she’d been holding in her teeth since four o’clock the previous afternoon.

“He was with my mother,” said Lyra.

He stood quite still.

“I was trying to tell you.”
