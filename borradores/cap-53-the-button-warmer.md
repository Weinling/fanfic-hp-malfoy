# Chapter 53: The Button Warmer

Crabbe did not want to go home for Christmas.

She had not sat at Lyra’s end of the table since a Friday in November. She said so on the first Sunday in December, in the dormitory, with the list for the holidays pinned to the noticeboard downstairs and her name not on it; and she said it to the window, where the lake went past green and slow and full of the shadows of things, and not to Lyra.

“My dad was there,” said Crabbe. “In June. The night of the Third Task. At the house.”

Lyra had been doing her hair. She put the brush down.

She had known, in the way one knows things in Slytherin, without being told: there had been a list in her head since the summer of the fathers who had stood up at once when the Dark Lord called, and Crabbe’s father was on it, and Goyle’s, and Montague’s. She had not thought about what it would be like to be the daughter of one of them. She had been too busy being the daughter of another.

“He came home at breakfast,” said Crabbe. “With mud on him. He didn’t say anything. He sat at the table and ate four eggs, and my mum didn’t say anything either, and nobody’s said anything since.” She pulled at a thread in the bedspread. “He’s different. He laughs more. He laughs at things that aren’t funny.” She stopped. “I don’t want to go home and watch him laugh.”

Lyra thought of her own father laughing over the *Prophet* in July.

She said nothing about it. There was nothing she could say that would not be a confession.

“Then don’t,” she said.

“I have to put my name down. On the list. It’s too late, it went up on Friday.”

“I’m a prefect. I sign the list.”

Crabbe looked round from the window at last.

“You’d—”

“It’s nothing. A line of ink. I’ll say you’ve a great deal of Arithmancy to catch up on, which you have, and that your mother wrote to me to ask, which she didn’t.” She picked up the brush again. “And I shall come back on the second, before the others, and you can tell me what the castle is like with nobody in it. I stayed here at Christmas in first year. It was very quiet. You’ll hate it.”

Crabbe did not laugh. She stood by the bedpost and looked at her for a long time, with her slow, careful, thorough look, as though she were reading a page she had read before and wished to check.

“This isn’t anything,” said Crabbe. “I needed a signature.”

“I know.”

“I’m not telling you anything else. Ever. Not about anybody’s cousin.”

“No,” said Lyra.

Crabbe nodded. She went back to the window. They stayed like that for an hour, one at each end of the room, and it was the most company Lyra had had since the middle of November.

She went into the first-years’ dormitory on the Tuesday, during Herbology, when there was nobody in it.

She had a prefect’s reason ready, in case: a complaint about a smell. There was a smell, as it turned out, from a sock under Urquhart’s little brother’s bed, and she held it in reserve and went to Draco’s trunk, at the end, under the window, with his name on the lid in gold leaf that their father had insisted on and Draco had pretended to be embarrassed by.

It was not locked. Nothing Draco owned had ever been locked. She lifted the lid.

His robes. His books. A tin of the sweets their mother sent every week. A great many Chocolate Frog cards, most of them Krum. And under the robes, at the bottom, where he must have thought nobody would ever look, a box.

It was a wooden box, about the size of a hatbox and very plain, of some dark old wood, with brass corners. It had come by owl; there was a scrap of brown paper still caught under one corner. Draco’s little brass seal lay beside it, with a stick of green wax worn down at one end. He had sealed a band of parchment across the lid with the Malfoy crest. Under it, on a little label, somebody had written:

*For the gentleman. Not to be opened by anybody else.*

Lyra knelt on the floor of the first-years’ dormitory and looked at the label for a long time.

She knew that hand.

On the letter she had carried to Karkaroff, a year ago last November: an old-fashioned hand, with long loops, unsteady at the ends. She had supposed her father had had somebody address it for him. Karkaroff had done everything that letter asked. Her father had called her errand a piece of a great work.

It was the same hand. The same long loops, the same old-fashioned shapes.

But it did not shake.

The loops went round smooth and sure, to the very ends of the letters.

*He used to spill his tea*, Draco had said, at breakfast, in July. *His hands went like this. They don’t now.*

She lifted the box an inch. It weighed what an empty box weighs. He had sealed it anyway, to be ready, as he had packed for Hogwarts in June. The list lay under the stick of wax, folded small; he must take it off for Herbology, for fear of the smell. She unfolded it. Three passes. A place for hiding things. The bust with the wig. With the whole drawing visible, the thing at the bottom was plainly a crown.

She copied the directions and the label into the back of her book of rounds. Then she looked at the seal. Draco would notice if she tore the parchment, and he would ask her before he asked anybody else. Whatever she told him, it would go back to the gentleman. *Somebody in your family looked.*

She could still find out who had sent him without giving that warning. At Christmas she would watch where the box went. She put the list back, and the robes on top, and shut the trunk.

On her way out she confiscated the sock, for form’s sake, and dropped it in the common-room fire. In her pocket was a copy of the hand that had stopped shaking.

The last Hogsmeade weekend of term was the second Saturday in December, and it snowed.

It had been snowing since dawn, big soft flakes coming down out of a white sky with no wind behind them, so that the whole village looked like something out of the lid of a chocolate box: the roofs gone thick and round, the windows lit gold at eleven in the morning, the High Street a trodden river of brown slush between two banks of white. Lyra walked down it with Goyle, and with Crabbe two paces behind, which was where Crabbe walked now, in her best grey cloak and the scarf her mother had sent to replace the one she had not got back, and did not look in at the window of the Three Broomsticks as they passed it.

Goyle wanted to go in. Goyle wanted butterbeer, and warmth, and to sit down, and said so for the length of the High Street and back, until Lyra gave in out of exhaustion.

It was packed. At the table by the door a long mild Hufflepuff was lifting his butterbeer with his left hand. She went past it. She was getting rather good at that. The only table with room at it was a long one in the corner by the fire, where three people were already sitting. One of them had red hair.

Lyra stopped in the doorway.

Potter was beside him.

He saw her. Granger saw her a moment after, and said something, and Weasley looked round, and his face did something complicated and then settled into the one he had worn on the grass after the match.

“There’s room,” said Granger, quite loudly, before anybody else could say anything. She moved her bag off the bench. “If you want it. It’s horrible outside.”

Goyle had already sat down.

So they sat, the six of them, at one long table by the fire, with Granger and Crabbe in the middle like a border nobody had agreed to. Weasley moved his mug out of Lyra’s way without looking at her.

“That song,” she said. “I should have stopped it.”

He looked at her then, hard. “Yeah. You should.”

She let him look. For some minutes nobody said anything. Then Weasley said something about the Chudley Cannons, to Granger, pointedly, and Lyra could not help herself.

“They lost to Puddlemere,” she said. “On Saturday. Three hundred and twenty to ten.”

“They were *robbed*—”

“By whom? Their own Keeper?”

It went on from there: the Cannons, and the Wasps, and whether Lynch had any business calling himself a Seeker, until Weasley was red to the ears and Lyra was pink to hers and Goyle was eating her way steadily through a plate of crumpets as though at a play. Lyra left Weasley’s four goals out of it. When she caught Granger looking at her, she looked away.

She had not looked at Potter once since she sat down.

When she did, it was by accident. She had reached across Crabbe for the salt, and he had leaned forward at the same moment to put his mug down, and his scarf had fallen open at the throat, and there on the collar of his robes, where it had been all the time under the wool, was a button.

A round brass button, about the size of a Sickle, with a little clip on the back.

She knew it. She knew it as one knows one’s own handwriting across a room. She had worn it at her collar on a December afternoon two years ago, in a shack with the sleet coming down outside, and she had torn it off and thrown it across the floorboards, and watched it tip through a gap by the wall into the dark.

He had gone back for it.

And he had been wearing it. All this winter. All last winter, perhaps; under his scarf, where nobody would see it, at his collar, where she had worn it.

She sat with the salt in her hand.

He saw her see it. He left his scarf where it was, and said nothing. He looked at her across the corner of the table, over the crumpets and the Chudley Cannons, and let her look, as he had let her look at the back of his hand in a dark classroom in October; and his face did not ask her for anything.

Lyra put the salt down.

He caught her up outside, in the snow, at the bottom of the High Street, where the houses stopped and the lane went on white towards the Shrieking Shack and the hills.

Crabbe saw him coming, and took Goyle by the sleeve, and went on ahead without a word, without looking at Lyra.

“I’ve got something for you,” said Potter. “For your mother.”

He held it out. It was a small square of folded paper, very old, gone soft and brown at the creases, with a stain on one corner that might have been water once, or something worse.

Lyra did not take it.

“Dumbledore said I could,” said Potter. “I asked him. I told him whose cousin it was. He gave it me on Thursday.” He held it a little further out. “She was his cousin. And there wasn’t anything, after, was there? Nothing to bury. I thought she’d want his hand.”

*I should like to see his hand once more.*

She had never told him her mother said that. He had worked it out for himself, from a cave, and a cousin, and nothing to bury; and gone to Dumbledore, and asked.

She took it. Her gloves were too thick; she took them off, and held the little square of paper in her bare hand, and did not unfold it. It was not hers to unfold.

“We’re not speaking,” she said. “You used my brother. I told you not to come near him.”

“I know.”

“This doesn’t change that.”

“I know. It isn’t for that. It isn’t for anything.” He put his hands in his pockets, in the snow. He had taken to doing that whenever she was within reach; he had been doing it for a month. “It isn’t on anybody’s account, Lyra. It’s just your mum’s.”

She put the paper inside her robes, against her ribs, where she had once carried her mother’s postscript for fourteen months.

“That’s my button,” said Lyra.

“Yeah.”

“You went under a floor for it. There are rats under that floor.”

“Three.” He looked at his boots. “It still works.”

“It is *mine*, Potter.”

She heard it go. So did he. He did not smile; she watched him not do it. His hands stayed in his pockets.

“I know,” said Potter. “You can have it back whenever you like.”

She did not hold out her hand.

She ought to have gone, then. Crabbe and Goyle were small dark shapes a long way up the lane already. She stood in the snow with the warmth of the folded paper against her ribs and her gloves in her hand, and asked him, instead, the thing she had been carrying since June and had never once asked, because she had not been sure she wanted the answer.

“Why am I here?”

He looked at her.

“In your world, there wasn’t any me. There was Draco, at your age, at that table, and in Madam Malkin’s, on the stool. Not me. So why am I here, and not him? What did you do?”

“Nothing. I didn’t do anything. You were here before I was. You were here when I was a baby.” He was quiet for a moment, with the snow coming down between them. “I don’t think anybody did anything. I think somewhere, before either of us was born, somebody tossed a coin, and it came down the other way. That’s all. That’s all I know about it.”

“A coin.”

“Yeah.”

She thought about that. It was not much of an answer. It was, she suspected, the only true one there was, and he had given it to her without dressing it up, which was more than she had expected.

“Well. I hope somebody made a great deal of money on it.”

He laughed. It came out of him in a surprised white cloud in the cold air, the real one, that she had not heard since the sixth floor; and he stopped it almost at once, as though he had not meant to, and looked at her over it. His hands stayed where they were. She understood that he would stand there until the snow had covered him before he took them out.

She was very cold, and it was nearly Christmas, and there was nobody on the lane.

She took one step, and put her forehead against the front of his robes.

It was only for a moment. She felt him stop breathing. He smelled of wet wool and Weasley’s crumpets. Nothing about him moved, not an inch, and she could feel through the cloth what that was costing him; it was like leaning on a door with somebody holding it from the other side.

She stood up straight, and put on her gloves.

“That didn’t count,” said Lyra.

“No,” said Potter. There was hardly any sound in it.

She turned and went up the lane after the others.

At the edge of the village, where the last cottage stood with its roof gone thick and white, there was a dog sitting in the snow.

It was very large and very black, and shaggy, and it was sitting by the gatepost with snow on its back, watching her come. It had pale grey eyes. Those eyes had watched her put a chicken leg on a stone by the fence, two winters ago, before she knew whose they were.

She stopped in front of it.

The dog looked up at her. She looked down at the dog. There was a great deal she might have said to it, about his cousin, and about a note against her ribs, and about the boy on the other side of the village who had gone back under a floor for a brass button; and she said none of it.

She put her hand in the pocket of her cloak instead, and found what was there, which was a sugar mouse from Honeydukes that Goyle had given her an hour ago and that she had no intention of eating, and held it out on her flat palm.

The dog took it, very delicately, as Buckbeak took steak. It ate it in one. Then it put its great black head against her knee, for a moment, heavily, through the snow, as a dog does with somebody it has decided about, and got up, and went away down the lane towards the Shack, and did not look back.

“That,” said Lyra to the snow, “is a chicken leg and a bar of chocolate you still owe me, Black. Don’t think I’ve forgotten.”

On the train home for Christmas, Draco slept nearly the whole way, with his head against the window and a wooden box on his knees.

He had carried it out of the castle himself, in both arms, and would not let anybody put it in the rack, and would not let Lyra hold it while he ate his sandwiches. He had wrapped his scarf round it, as though it might be cold. He was so happy that it made her chest hurt to look at him.

“I found it,” he had told her on the platform, in a whisper, because he could not help it. “The thing on the list. Behind the wall. I’d been thinking about treasure, but you have to mean hiding things. I went after lunch, and it worked. I didn’t tell anybody. Not even—” He stopped. “I thought he meant if somebody asked me to go at night. This was just me. He’s going to be so *pleased*.”

*Not even.* His hand had gone to the pocket where the coin lived. All her rounds, and he had gone in the afternoon, alone, with a reason he could give himself for not telling Potter.

He had kept his secret for an old man in a cottage, as she had kept hers for him.

She kept one hand over the note for her mother and watched the box on his knees, all the way home.
