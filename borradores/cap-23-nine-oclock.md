# Chapter 23: Nine o’Clock

Peter Pettigrew was alive. He had betrayed the Potters; Sirius Black was innocent.

Nobody said so in the *Daily Prophet*.

The Ministry of Magic did not care to admit that it had kept the wrong man in Azkaban for twelve years. Whatever the reason, whatever oath or charm or tangle of old magic lay behind the mistake, it would hardly be improved by the news that the Ministry had then spent most of a year failing to catch him. So the Ministry said nothing at all. Instead, the Dementors were quietly withdrawn from the gates of Hogwarts, and the Aurors who had been searching for Sirius Black were called back to London, and the search for him was, in the Ministry’s own phrase, suspended pending review. A few sharp people noticed that Albus Dumbledore had been up to town twice in the same week. Most people did not notice anything.

Professor Lupin missed a week of lessons after the full moon. Lyra took the long way round to Charms, so as not to pass his door.

The Malfoys had a great many friends at the Ministry, and heard sooner than most. Lyra’s father wrote to her the same day.

*My dear Lyra,*

*It appears the hunt for Sirius Black is over. There is something not altogether straightforward about it, but the Ministry has evidently concluded that he is no longer a danger to anybody. I gather he and Dumbledore have come to some arrangement.*

*A clever girl like you will already have stepped back and be watching from a safe distance; but in case you were wondering, I thought I would let you know.*

*I have also sent you a present. It is late, but you may think of it as a Christmas present. You are not to be downhearted about the Quidditch. Next time, simply crush them.*

*Your loving father*

The present came by six owls, and it was a broom.

It was a Firebolt. The international standard; the broom the Irish national side had ordered, every one of them, for the World Cup. Nought to a hundred and fifty miles an hour in ten seconds, an unbreakable braking charm, and a price you had to write and ask for, because they did not print it. Even for a Malfoy it was not a small thing. Her father had bought the whole team Nimbus Two Thousand and Ones only last year; this one broom must have cost more than all of them together.

He had seen her at Christmas, then, moping about the house, and put it down to Cedric Diggory.

*It was Harry Potter and Sirius Black,* thought Lyra, turning the handle in the light. *And that’s all been settled since.*

All the same, there was no denying that a Firebolt was the only broom really worthy of Lyra Malfoy. She was secretly delighted. She wrote her father a long and graceful letter of thanks, and went down to breakfast with the broom over her shoulder.

The Great Hall went to pieces.

The Slytherins came first, one after another, as though they were being pulled on a string; and then half the second-years from the other Houses, and a cluster of Ravenclaws standing on their bench to see over the heads. Marcus Flint had taken up a position next to the broom like a dragon on a heap of gold, and was slapping hands away from it.

“Merlin. A *Firebolt*. Parkinson! Hands *off!*”

Panther Parkinson snatched his hand back and went on gazing at it.

“We’ll walk Ravenclaw with this,” he said dreamily. “Chang’s only on a Comet Two-Sixty.”

“Lyra. Can I touch it?”

Ordinarily she would not have dreamed of it. But it was a particularly good morning, and she allowed it, graciously, one at a time. Flint glared harder than ever.

“Wipe your hands first! All of you! And don’t knock it! You—you’ve just had your hand in the gravy, don’t you *dare* come near it with that—”

Every time somebody sighed over the Firebolt, Lyra’s shoulders went up a little further.

A broom did not make a flyer. But a broom like this would let a flyer show every last thing she could do. Somebody would say, no doubt, that the Malfoys had bought her a place at the top; and Lyra would laugh in their face. Money was a kind of talent as well, and she had it.

She was enjoying all this very much when somebody said, “Oh, is that a Firebolt?”

Harry Potter came over the Hufflepuff bench and the end of the Slytherin table in two strides. Flint’s face screwed itself up at once.

“Not you, Potter. You don’t touch it. You don’t *look* at it.”

“Come on, Flint, we’ve already played you. Don’t be like that. Let me have a look.”

“After what you did last time—”

Flint glanced at Lyra as he said it. He had no idea what had gone on between the two of them, but he knew perfectly well that it had wrecked his Seeker for most of the winter, and he had a match against Ravenclaw coming up.

“Leave him, Marcus,” said Lyra. “Let him look, if he wants to.”

She liked nothing better than showing an enemy what she had. Flint knew that, and backed off, though he did not stop glaring. Potter came through the crowd. Parkinson leaned in his way with one shoulder, not quite by accident, and Potter hooked a foot round his ankle so that he staggered, and stepped past him to Lyra’s side.

“It really is a Firebolt.” Even he looked impressed. “That’s beautiful. It’s not just the speed, either, look at the line of it.”

Naturally he was impressed. Anybody would be, seeing a Firebolt for the first time; you showed a work of art its due respect, rival or no rival.

Lyra was, at that moment, the most confident person in the Great Hall. She tipped her head back.

“What a shame I didn’t have it when we played. I’d have flattened you, and you wouldn’t even have had the weather to blame.”

“I always thought your Nimbus was a bit ordinary for you,” said Potter. “Everybody’s got one. But this—” He nodded at it. “This is about right.”

Even Harry Potter admitted it. Well, he was a Quidditch player; he could hardly help being envious.

So many pleasant things had happened lately that Lyra was quite carried away. She gave him her most generous smile.

“If you ask me very nicely, I might let you have a go on it.”

“Oh, I’m honoured. Very kind. But no need.”

He stepped back out of the crowd as easily as he had come into it. He patted Parkinson on the shoulder on his way, and waved a hand in front of Flint’s nose, and went off through the Hall without looking back.

Before he sat down at the Gryffindor table, though, he turned round, and smiled at her across the whole of the Great Hall.

“Practise hard, Malfoy.”

*Every time I smile, I’ll think of you.*

Lyra knew exactly what he was thinking about. She could feel her face trying to do something and would not let it.

“You’re the one who’ll need to practise,” she called back, “if you don’t want to look silly next to this broom.”

Potter laughed, and turned away. Lyra sniffed, and that was all anybody saw.

A little way down the table, Goyle had noticed something in the air. She leaned over to Crabbe.

“When did those two make it up?”

“I don’t know exactly,” said Crabbe. “But I’ll tell you one thing.” She looked, for Crabbe, extraordinarily pleased with herself. “I think I made a rather good Cupid.”

“What are you on about?”

Goyle stabbed a sausage with her fork and put it in her mouth. Anyway, it meant there would be no more of Lyra’s tantrums, and the two of them began to edge along the bench towards her, to find out what on earth had happened.

⁂

The Ravenclaw match was closer than it had any business being.

In fact Slytherin very nearly lost it. Ravenclaw had come with a plan, and practised it until they could do it in their sleep, and for the first hour Slytherin were pulled about the sky by it like puppets. At one point they were twenty to ninety down. Another half-hour of that and the Snitch would not have saved them.

Lyra had been waiting for her team to make up the points. She stopped waiting.

Ravenclaw had one weakness, and everybody knew it. They had climbed to the top of the table by drilling and by clever plays, but the Seeker, the one position that mattered most, was their weakest. Cho Chang was a fourth-year on a Comet Two-Sixty, and she had not caught the Snitch once all season. Lyra went past her on the Firebolt as though she were standing still, and took the Snitch out of the air under the Ravenclaw hoops.

Afterwards Chang sat on the grass with her face in her knees and cried, and some of the Ravenclaws stood round patting her shoulders, without much conviction.

It was hard on her, really. Chang was not a bad Seeker, for a fourth-year on a Comet.

“It was only that it was me,” said Lyra, to nobody in particular.

The score was nothing to boast about. But a team, as far as Lyra was concerned, was mostly there to set her off to advantage, and she had snatched the match back out of the fire on her own. She came down off the Firebolt into a roar of green and silver with her shoulders right up round her ears, and did not let it show, much.

She thought that, just then, she could have cast a Patronus without anybody holding her hand.

The Cup came down to the last match of the season: Gryffindor against Hufflepuff.

The arithmetic was horrible. Slytherin had won two and lost one, and Ravenclaw had lost all three. Hufflepuff had won two. Gryffindor had won one and lost one, and had lost it by two hundred and forty points. If Gryffindor won by more than two hundred, the Cup went to Gryffindor. If Gryffindor won by less, it went to Slytherin. If Hufflepuff won, it went to Hufflepuff.

So Slytherin found itself in the absurd position of having to cheer for Gryffindor, but only up to a point, and then cheer for Hufflepuff instead. Somebody in the sixth year solved the problem by charming two hundred little banners, scarlet on one side and yellow on the other, and handing them round the Slytherin stand. You waved the red side for as long as it was safe, and turned it over the moment Gryffindor looked like winning by too much.

“Honestly,” said Lyra, sitting down with hers. “If it weren’t for the points I’d never cheer for Harry Potter in my life.”

Crabbe and Goyle looked at each other, and held a long, silent conference about how much of that was true. They came, after careful deliberation, to Crabbe’s conclusion: that something rather nice had happened between those two.

Down on the grass, the two Seekers stood side by side.

Potter was on a school broom, one of the old Cleansweeps from the shed, with its bristles thin and its handle mended. Lyra had heard Wood had nearly wept about it. Diggory held out his hand with his best smile.

“All right, Harry? Good luck. Best man wins.”

Potter did not smile back.

“Clever trick, that,” he said. “Talking to the other Seeker. I wouldn’t have thought of it. I thought I’d have a go myself.”

A shadow went across Diggory’s face. “You mean Malfoy?”

“Got it in one. I think we’ve got a few things to say to each other. Shall we have a walk before we start?”

Madam Hooch’s whistle went. “Mount your brooms! Three—two—one—”

Twelve players rose into the air. The two Seekers did not. They walked off across the pitch side by side, each with a broom in one hand, as though they were out for a stroll.

Both teams stared down at them.

“*Harry!* What are you *doing?*”

“Cedric?”

But a Seeker could go where he liked, and nothing in the rules said he had to be off the ground. Madam Hooch threw up the Quaffle, the Chasers fell on it, and within a minute everybody had forgotten all about the Seekers, who went on strolling across the grass, talking.

Nobody heard what they said. Lyra watched them from the stand, and could see Diggory’s face, which kept changing, and Potter’s, which did not.

Then Potter got on his broom, and Diggory got on his, and they kicked off together and went up like rockets.

It took two hours.

Afterwards they said it was the best match anybody could remember, and the strangest, because Harry Potter spent most of it not being a Seeker at all. He flew in formation with the Weasley twins and broke up the Hufflepuff Chasers. He went into a screaming dive after nothing, and dragged Diggory and both Hufflepuff Beaters down with him, and left them tangled at the bottom while he slid quietly away. Every time Diggory looked as though he had seen the Snitch, one of the Weasleys knocked a Bludger at him, because Potter had told them to. Diggory lost the Snitch five times.

Hufflepuff had only one way to win, and everyone knew it: Diggory had to catch the Snitch. So the Hufflepuff Beaters stayed with Diggory to protect him, and nobody much was minding the Quaffle. And Potter, who was a very good Seeker, turned out to be a fair Chaser and a fair Beater and a fair Keeper as well. He never touched the Quaffle himself. But he went into the middle of the Hufflepuff side and opened holes in it, and once, when it suited him, he got in the way of a pass with his chest and pretended it had been an accident.

By the time Diggory changed tactics, Gryffindor were nearly a hundred and fifty up. After that it was a brawl, all over the sky, for another hour; and then the gap reached a hundred and sixty, and Potter, as if he had been waiting for exactly that number, went straight up through the middle of everything and caught the Snitch.

“*GRYFFINDOR WIN THE CUP!* Three hundred and seventy to a hundred and sixty! *Gryffindor win the Cup!*”

The Slytherin stand sat with its banners half turned over and its mouths open.

Ten points fewer and the Cup would have been Slytherin’s. Lyra could not pretend she did not mind.

And yet, somewhere a long way down, something in her was fizzing.

He had been angry with Diggory. It was perfectly obvious why: the trick in January, the friendly chatter, the hand on her arm. Lyra thought tricks like that were part of the game, and she still thought so. It did not stop her enjoying, very much, the sight of Cedric Diggory being run ragged for two hours.

The players came down. Both teams lay about on the grass like the survivors of a shipwreck, Gryffindor rather more cheerfully. Diggory was sitting on the ground, soaked with sweat. Potter walked over to him.

Diggory looked up, and gave a great helpless laugh.

“That was a complete thrashing. So that’s how you do it.”

“Well, now you know.”

Potter held out his hand. Diggory took it, and began to pull himself up—

—and Potter let go.

Diggory sat down hard on the grass.

It was a dreadful insult to the losing side, or it would have been, if it had not been so completely childish. People stood about with their mouths open. Lyra, coming down the steps of the stand, was close enough to hear what Potter said, bending down to him.

“That’s from Lyra. What were you doing with your hand on her arm?”

“Ha.” Diggory rubbed his elbow. “You’re a piece of work, aren’t you. I thought you didn’t care about anything. You’re more faithful than you look.”

He got up on his own. This time it was Diggory who held out his hand, and Potter who shook it.

Lyra was walking across the grass towards him by then.

Not to congratulate him, naturally. Gryffindor had won the Cup by ten points; this was a moment for Slytherin to be bitter in. She was only going because he looked a disgrace, and he might as well have a handkerchief, and she might as well tell him while she was at it that next year she would not be handing him the Cup on a plate.

It was in her pocket. She had washed it herself in the end, with a charm, and pressed it, and kept it in the drawer with the ring. She had known since a February night whose it must have been. There had only ever been one person in the castle who could stand on a staircase without being seen.

She was turning it over in her fingers as she came up behind them, and so it happened that she heard.

“I think you’ve got the wrong idea, Cedric,” Potter was saying. “It isn’t like you and Cho. I’m not going out with Lyra.”

“What?”

“Just that. We’re not going out.”

“But the way you—”

Lyra stopped dead.

As a matter of plain fact, it was true. There had never been anything said between them that you could have called binding. If somebody asked whether they were going out, the honest answer was nearer *no* than *yes*.

And yet she felt wronged, and oddly winded, as though somebody had shut a door in her face. It was the feeling of a person who has been let down by a hope, and has no one to blame for the hope but herself. Nobody had made her any promises. Nobody had put anything into words, or made any sort of contract.

But there had been the forest. There had been the brooms, and *only in front of me*. There had been a silver fox on the shingle, made out of the two of them. Was that not plainer than anything anybody could say?

*Very well. Let him think what he likes.*

She felt a perfect fool for having fizzed.

She turned round. Behind her, she thought she heard Potter’s voice, calling something. She walked on as though she had not.

The noise of the Gryffindors running onto the pitch swallowed her footsteps.

⁂

She was halfway up to the castle, going fast, when Potter came out from behind a pillar of the cloister, quite out of breath.

“Shouldn’t the hero of the match be somewhere else?”

“Lyra.”

“What.”

“Sorry.”

“For what?”

She did not look at him. He was breathing hard.

“I’m doing something a bit dangerous. You know roughly what. It’s to do with stopping him coming back.”

The *everything* Dumbledore had talked about. The thing so large that Dementors and an escape from Azkaban were only an incident in it. Harry Potter was in the middle of something that might change the world, and Lyra knew it, and had respected it enough to walk away from Dumbledore without asking.

“So?”

“So if people know how I feel, it’s dangerous. Not for me. For all of us. It’s better, for now, if nobody can see it. Officially.” He hesitated. “Especially next year. If students come from other schools.”

Other schools? Lyra filed that away to be wondered about later; she had better things to do with it just now. Because it was the same thing again. *It’s dangerous, so I’ll keep you at a distance.* A coward’s excuse, dressed up as caring about you, to push you away.

Something in her went quite cold. She was sick of this. Lyra Malfoy was not a girl who got turned down. She would sooner die than be put aside the same way twice. She drew breath to tell Harry Potter precisely what she thought of a boy who believed he could move her about like a piece on a board—

And a small ring swallowed it all.

“But every night at nine o’clock,” said Potter, and held up his hand, with the other ring on it, “I’m going to put this on and smile. I’ll keep my hand under the blankets and keep looking at it. I don’t think I’ll be able to help smiling, even if I try.”

In the silence he took her hand. The ring opened its mouth, very carefully, and closed over the fourth finger of her left hand, and shrank to fit.

It held on. It was a nice sort of tightness.

“I hope you’ll do the same,” said Harry.

It was a very feeble sort of declaration. A ring, put on a girl’s finger: it was the oldest, stuffiest, most hackneyed thing in the world, and there was nothing new in it whatsoever. Lyra Malfoy had no interest in romance. If anybody ever tried anything of the kind on her, she would turn him down flat without a second thought.

That went for Harry Potter too.

“Hmph. Do you imagine I’ll melt because you’ve given me a ring?” She pushed his hand away. “A boy who comes with as much luggage as you do is the last thing I want.”

If there was any turning down to be done, Lyra Malfoy would do it. She tossed her pale hair back over her shoulder with the hand that wore the ring, where he could not possibly miss it, and kept a very firm hold on the corners of her mouth, which wanted to do something silly.

“I shan’t wait long. I’m not a person with time on her hands. Sort it out quickly.”

And she swept past him, with the ring still on her finger.
