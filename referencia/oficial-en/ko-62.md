# Traducción inglesa oficial — coreano 62: Gaunt's Ring

"Harry. I have good news and bad news. Which would you like to hear first?"

"You're going to tell me both either way, aren't you? What does the order matter? Just get on with it."

"Hmm. Then I shall begin with the good news... We have found a new Horcrux."

Dumbledore gestured to the ring resting on the table. A silver ring set with a large stone, it had been reduced to ruin by the Sword of Gryffindor.

"I have told you something of Tom Riddle's origins before, Harry. His mother, Merope Gaunt, was a witch born into the Gaunt family—a pureblood line of wretched poverty, yet direct descendants of Salazar Slytherin. And his father..."

"Tom Riddle the First. The Muggle Voldemort despised so utterly."

"Yes. He was a direct heir of Slytherin, and yet a half-blood. His mother's family was pureblood but degraded and destitute; his father's family wealthy and comfortable, but Muggle. He was born into that contradiction. Raised in an orphanage from infancy, he never knew the faces of his parents—yet he carried within him a vague, consuming mixture of longing and hatred for the very idea of family."

The ring, which must once have been beautiful, had lost all its light and become nothing more than wreckage. Dumbledore murmured with something like sorrow.

"And so Gaunt's Ring—this precious heirloom passed down through generations—was worthy of becoming a Horcrux. And a Horcrux it was. After tracing Tom Riddle's past, I found this ring in the home of his mother's family."

The good news Dumbledore had brought was that another Horcrux had been destroyed. Even under the Ministry's constant interference, he had followed the trail and found it—and unmade it.

But what the existence of this Horcrux implied...

"...Is this ring an artefact of one of Hogwarts' founders?"

Harry asked the question flatly, already half-certain of the bad news to come. Dumbledore shook his head with a look of quiet regret.

"No. Salazar Slytherin's artefact is that locket—and that alone. There may be others, but none were found among the Gaunts. This ring is an heirloom their ancestors preserved. It predates Hogwarts itself..."

Dumbledore stopped mid-sentence when he saw Harry's expression. Harry's face had gone still, and beneath the stillness lay a deep, unmistakable exhaustion.

After a long silence, Harry finally spoke.

"The Horcruxes... there could be far more than we thought? It isn't only artefacts of Hogwarts' founders that can become Horcruxes..."

Dumbledore nodded. Harry pressed his hands together and exhaled slowly. The tendons stood white against his knuckles.

Dumbledore spoke in a tone meant to comfort.

"I am currently seeking a friend who may know more about the Horcruxes. He was a professor at Hogwarts during Tom Riddle's time as a student. With his help, we may be able to determine how many Horcruxes there are."

"When?"

Harry's voice was bleak.

"We haven't even found Ravenclaw's artefact yet, and now there could be several more Horcruxes of unknown identity? What then? Are we meant to search for things we don't even know exist? Like Luna hunting for Crumple-Horned Snorkacks?"

"What a curious name. What is that?"

"That's not the point!"

Harry snapped, shoving himself to his feet. His face was raw with fury and something darker beneath it.

"Professor Dumbledore. I'm withdrawing from school."

"I cannot permit that."

"I'll strap Umbridge to a broomstick and send her to the moon on my way out. There's nothing left to learn in this place except how much I despise people."

"You know as well as I do that you cannot do that—any more than I can."

"How much longer do I have to stay here? There are an obscene number of Horcruxes. The bloody Ministry obstructs everything at every turn. That woman Umbridge looks at me like she'd like to have me for dinner. And I just keep enduring it. Enduring, and enduring, and enduring. Why? They answer to no one and do as they please, while I'm supposed to sit here and take it? Suffer through a school where I can barely learn anything? Why should I—"

Harry's voice cracked into something raw. Dumbledore watched him without moving.

"You said the fighting was for adults, didn't you? But that's not how it is! At least, that's not how they see it! Death Eaters send their own children into Dumbledore's hands while conspiring on every front imaginable! Umbridge isn't just after me anymore—she's keeping Luna in detention now too! Simply because her father edits The Quibbler!"

"Harry."
"Is what you're trying to teach me submission to injustice? Then perhaps we should surrender to Voldemort right now and fall in line beneath him. Because I no longer know what I'm supposed to be protecting!"

Harry emptied himself of every last shred of anger and dropped back onto the sofa.

Dumbledore spoke quietly to the boy who sat before him like the ash left after a fire has burned itself out.

"Are you still having the dreams, Harry?"

"Yes."

Harry answered without strength.

"Every night, I become a snake. I feel a cold exhilaration as I move across stone floors. At the end of a long, dark corridor, there is a door, firmly shut. The snake always crosses the corridor and approaches it—tries to open it. So far, the door has not opened. The snake rages and lashes out, but the door gives nothing back. Yet it feels close. The snake retreats, biding its time, and that is when I wake."

Harry raised his head. Behind his glasses, his eyes were cold and sharp.

Dumbledore—had he still not told Harry about the prophecy? Did he fear that knowing it would make Harry arrogant?

To withhold something from another person out of fear they might become proud of it—to appoint oneself the keeper of that knowledge—was itself a breathtaking arrogance. How could he still not see that?

Lately, Harry had been catching himself in sudden flashes of razor-edged clarity. He suspected this was one of them. He pressed a hand to his throbbing temple and looked at Dumbledore.

"That place was the Ministry. It was the underground corridor I saw when I was brought to the courtroom. What is beneath the Ministry, Professor?"

Dumbledore did not answer. He turned toward the cabinet crowded with silver instruments and sprinkled a silver powder over a flask. A white vapour billowed upward, coiling into shapes—restless, animated, as though trying to communicate something. Whatever it meant, it was beyond Harry's comprehension.

Dumbledore turned back and said:

"I cannot tell you. Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Seek training from Snape in the protection of the mind. When you have completed it, I will tell you everything—including the reason I cannot tell you now."

Harry did not even feel disappointed. He nodded as though he had expected exactly this, and rose from his seat without a trace of lingering hope.

As though he had never expected much from Dumbledore to begin with.

"Good evening, Professor."

"Take care of yourself, Harry."

Harry left the headmaster's office and descended the spiral staircase. His face was set with a cold, clear hostility.

He had come to understand Dumbledore's methods, more or less. When faced with what Dumbledore deemed a minor situation, he tended to choose inaction. Unless it was something as significant as the search for Horcruxes, he would not trouble himself to intervene...

Perhaps, in his foolishness and his arrogance, he was still blaming himself for having failed to teach Tom Riddle properly.

As though the cruelty and the brilliance of Lord Voldemort had not been fixed in him from birth.

Harry walked the corridor alone. His shadow stretched long behind him.

Harry had half-expected he would never play Quidditch again. Either Umbridge would interfere—she meddled in anything that might bring him joy—or Angelina, furious at his erratic attendance at practice, would simply kill him.

But no interference came in the week leading up to practice. Harry found it strange, and allowed himself to enjoy the brief reprieve.

"Ron! Catch!"

"What? Aaagh—"

Ron barely managed to deflect the Quaffle Harry had thrown, but lost his balance in the process and went spinning downward, sailing well past where he needed to be. Harry caught the rebounding Quaffle and lined up a shot at the empty hoops.

"Your centre of gravity is too far forward, Ron. You need to be thinking about braking before you go for the deflection."

"...I don't think I can do this."

"Oh, not again."
Ron said, his voice sinking.

"It was presumptuous of me. What was I thinking, putting myself forward as Keeper? If I play like this tomorrow, Gryffindor is going to be humiliated. I'll be a laughingstock."

"Don't be so hard on yourself. What does that make all the others who didn't make the cut?"

"People who were better than me but unlucky."

"Ron, please."

Harry tucked the Quaffle under his arm and flew toward him.

After Oliver Wood—the previous Keeper—had graduated, Ron had gone through the trials and earned his place on the team. He had been putting in the work, staying behind to practice on his own. But the old affliction had returned: the moment he was watched, the moment the pressure mounted, everything fell apart.

With a match tomorrow, he had managed to stop only one out of seventeen shots in the final trial.

Angelina had left in a fury:

'If you're this pathetic tomorrow, I'm replacing you with a scarecrow for the next match! At least a scarecrow might block one of the three hoops!'

Harry had stayed with him until late into the night trying to lift his spirits. For all the good it had done.

Harry clapped Ron on the shoulder.

"So what if you let in a few goals? Fred and George will be harassing their Chasers before they even get close to you. And whatever slips through, Angelina and Katie will make up for it. Then I'll catch the Snitch and end the game. Stop worrying."

"You don't understand."

Ron muttered, deflated.

"Every time I miss, everyone will jeer. The story will spread—the man who stopped one out of seventeen. If I let in sixteen goals, no Snitch catch can overcome that margin. I could ruin the whole match."

A surge of irritation hit Harry without warning.

He was spiralling over a few missed Quaffles? While Harry was out there hunting Horcruxes and being worn to nothing by Voldemort's dreams every night?

Under normal circumstances he would have swallowed it and found something kind to say. Ron was at his best when his spirits were high, and Harry owed him more than a little.

But patience was a resource, and Harry had been ground down from every direction—waking and sleeping alike—until there was nothing left to spare.

Harry pressed a hand to his throbbing temple.

"Stop whining at me, Ron. I'm not your mother."

Ron looked as though he'd been struck. A faint flicker of guilt rose in Harry, swiftly overwhelmed by a sharp, almost electric satisfaction. He let it carry him.

"The entry's already been submitted. We can't change the Keeper now. All this moaning just makes you look like you're fishing for sympathy by running yourself down. Your morale is already on the floor—don't make it worse, Ron."

Ron's mouth opened and closed. His face had gone as red as his hair—he was furious—but not a single word of rebuttal came out.

His confidence had collapsed so completely that even his defences had failed him.

Harry turned his back on Ron.

"I'd do more good practising my Snitch-catching than trying to help you. The only way to cancel out your goals is to end the game."

Harry had half-hoped Ron would come after him and take a swing. But when he landed and looked back, Ron was still sitting on his broom, shoulders slumped, perfectly still.

The sharp satisfaction flared and vanished like a struck match, and the guilt that had been quietly growing in the corner of his chest spread further. Harry regretted what he'd said before a minute had passed.

But he couldn't bring himself to go back and apologise. He shouldered his broom and headed up to the dormitory. He intended to sleep early—for the sake of tomorrow's match, and to avoid facing Ron.

As he walked, Harry made himself a promise.

Whether for Ron's sake or for victory's sake—he would catch the Snitch before Carina did.
