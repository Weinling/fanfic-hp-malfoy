# Chapter 85: Two Chairs

On the third Monday of term, the room gave them two chairs.

Harry stood and looked at it, with his hand still on the door. There was the bald rug and the fender. There was the old one, down on its left side, with its stuffing coming out and *Some Lesser Houses of Wessex* under the leg, where it had always been. And on the far side of the hearth, about nine feet off, stood another. It was upright and thin, with a hard seat and wooden arms, and it was the kind of chair that you get in the corridor outside a Healer’s. It had not been there on Friday.

Nobody had asked for it. You couldn’t ask for that; he’d been told how it worked. You walked past the wall and thought very hard of what you needed. It was supposed to be the only room in the castle that never lied to you.

He got hold of the thing and carried it across. It weighed hardly anything. He stood it beside the old one, so that the arms were touching, and he stepped back and looked at it, and he went and lit the fire.

She came at nine. She looked at the two chairs. She sat down in the old one, with her book, and he took the new one, and it was like sitting on a verdict. They had a nice evening. She read him some Herodotus, in that voice. On the Tuesday, when he came in from Snape’s, it was back on the far side of the hearth. He looked for marks on the rug. There weren’t any. He carried it across again. On the Wednesday it was nine feet off, with the seat swept.

He stopped carrying it on the Friday. He couldn’t have said he’d decided anything. He got out his notebook instead.

It was a small one with a black cover, that he had bought in Diagon Alley with his Potions things. *Mon. Left 10.10, alone. Went down by the east stairs. Tues. Goyle at the door, 9.40. Weds. Arithmancy till 5. Did not eat lunch. Thurs. Snape, 8. Came up 9.25, right hand on rail.* He kept it in his robes, on the left side, and wrote it up at night with the curtains drawn. He had the Cloak, and he’d started going down to the dungeon stairs again, the way he had in fourth year, except that he didn’t stand at the bottom where anybody could see him. He knew what she looked like going by: she carried her left arm a little in against her ribs, and she had started wearing cuffs. *Sept. 23. Long sleeves at Quidditch.* He looked at that line for some time, and he underlined it.

He knew who had taught him. She had kept a book on him for five years, with columns. She’d shown him. He had thought it was the most romantic thing he had ever heard.

“I want you to teach me something,” said Harry.

It was a Tuesday, and it was raining. It seemed to rain on Tuesdays. He had sat in the upright chair for twenty minutes and given Snape a table with one short leg; it had got rather crowded, that table, and there were some gaps at it now. Snape had gone through it with his long cold attention and had found nothing to say. He’d picked up his pen. That was the end of the lesson.

“Have you been drinking, Potter?”

“There’s a counter-curse. For cutting. There’s a thing you do, with your wand, along the wound. You sing it.” He had worked out how to say this. It had seemed quite good, on the stairs. “I’ve seen it. I want to know how.”

The pen stopped. It didn’t go down. There was a jar on the shelf, with something pale in it, turning slowly over.

“In your other existence,” said Snape.

“I was sixteen. It was in a bathroom. It was a boy who—it doesn’t matter who. There was a word, in a book. I’d found out what a lot of things did from that book, it had been right about everything, and it had *For Enemies* next to that one, and I didn’t know what it was. I just said it, to see. He’d got his hand up. It opened him. All across here. There was so much of it. It went in the water.” His mouth had dried up. He looked at the corner of the desk. “You came in. You got down on your knees in it, in your robes. You did something with your wand, along the cuts, and you were singing. Sort of. I’d never heard anything like it. It closed up. You didn’t say anything to me till after.”

Snape had laid down his pen.

He did it with some care. It lay on the blotter, in line with the edge. He sat with his long yellow fingers on either side of it, and he looked at Harry; and there was a thing that Harry had seen in that face only once, on a February night, when somebody had got to the end of a memory. He didn’t speak. He looked as though someone had told him the name of a street in a town where he’d been a child.

“What book?” he said.

“*Advanced Potion-Making*. It was second-hand. It had notes in it.”

“In whose hand?”

Harry looked up. It took him a moment. He had been carrying that for two lives, and it had never once occurred to him that he might be asked. He looked at the fingers. He looked at a pale scar across the knuckle of the right one, from something that had gone wrong when somebody was young.

“Yours, sir,” said Harry.

The fire gave a small dry tick. Nobody spoke for a long time. In the passage, far away, someone trod on the third flagstone, and stopped, and went away.

“I see,” said Snape. His voice had nothing in it at all. “I wrote that in the spring of 1976, in the boys’ lavatory on the third floor, in a temper. I have never told it to a living soul. There’s no copy of that book in this world but one, and it’s in a trunk, and I have not opened it for twenty years.” He drew one long breath, through his nose. “You have it from me. I suppose it was inevitable. What did you cut?”

“Sir?”

“You have been elsewhere since June, in your own mind, Potter, and I have been shown a table. You don’t come to me for a counter-curse out of historical interest. There is a woman at my master’s elbow with a wound on her face that no Healer can shut. She has told the story at table fourteen times, and she does the voice. Half a word. I have been asked what I make of that.” The black eyes had not moved. “I told him that a boy might find almost anything in a library. What did you cut?”

There was no point in it. There had never been any point, in that room.

“You know what I cut,” said Harry.

“I want to hear you say why you’re asking.”

He had thought about that as well. He had thought he’d say something about being prepared. He’d meant to say it was a useful thing, in a war. He looked at his right hand. He’d got it in his lap. It was lying there on his knee, with the white letters on the back of it, in the dull green underwater light, as quiet as a stone. He looked at it, the way you’d look at a dog that has bitten someone. He knew perfectly well what it was going to do.

“Because it’s going to happen again,” said Harry.

For a little while Snape did not answer. Then he got up. He went to the door and opened it, and spoke to somebody, in about three words, and came back. He took down from a shelf a very old plain wooden board, of the sort used for cutting roots. Harry heard feet coming along the passage. They were slow and heavy. There was a knock.

It was Goyle. She had her sleeves rolled up. She’d got something in her arms, wrapped in a cloth, that must have weighed about two stone, and there was a smell of cold fat and sawdust. She set it down on the end of Snape’s desk, among the third-year essays, and undid the cloth. It was the better part of a side of pork.

“Off the Friday order,” said Goyle. “Sir.”

She looked at it, with her head on one side. She looked at Harry, sitting in the chair, and at the wand in his hand, and at Professor Snape. She didn’t seem to wonder about anything. Her big soft face had got thinner over the summer, or he had started noticing faces.

“You want to mind the grain,” said Goyle. She wiped her hands on her apron, and she went out.

It took him until after midnight. Snape cut it himself. He didn’t say the word. He did it with the point of a thin black wand, in a single movement, without opening his lips, and the skin of the pork fell apart in a long red line. He had a singing voice like a rusty hinge. It went up and down, three notes and a fourth, on and on. You drew the wand along, an inch above, against the direction of the wound; you had to keep the sound going, without any gap, for as long as there was anything open. If you stopped to breathe it came undone. If you were frightened it came undone. If your hand shook, you got a thing like a badly sewn pocket, and Snape looked at it, and made another cut.

“Again. You are not icing a cake. *Again*. The patient has by now bled to death. I congratulate you. Again.”

He had never been so tired. There was blood on his cuffs, or something that was going to have to pass for it. He’d got a line three inches long to close, and stay closed, and he stood looking at it with his arm shaking. It had left a thick pale scar. Nothing that you did would take that away, Snape remarked, wiping his wand. It had not been designed to be taken away. He went round and sat down. He picked up his pen.

“Tuesdays,” said Snape. “After the other. I’d advise you to practise on something that can’t complain. You will tell no one. And, Potter.” He didn’t look up. “When you find you want this, you won’t be in a state to sing. It’s a pity. It’s a very beautiful piece of work. I was rather proud of it.”

There were thirty-four of them that Thursday, in the long room behind the kitchens.

It had a notice on the door now, in Neville’s round hand, with a stamp from Professor Lupin. *Defence Society. Tues. and Thurs. Please wipe your feet.* It still smelt of cold dust and frying, and somebody had mended the white line. Seven new first-years stood along the wall, looking at their wands as if they might go off, and Neville stood in the middle of the boards with his crooked jaw and his sleeves pushed up, and a pot under one arm. He hadn’t wanted to do this. He had told them so, on the first evening, for some time, and then he had shown them, and nobody had coughed. They’d have followed him into a lake.

Harry stood at the front, where he could be seen.

He had the Patronus people, at the far end, on Thursdays. That was all he had. He’d got Dean, and a Ravenclaw called Li, and Susan Bones, who hadn’t spoken at all since she came back. She stood with her glove in her pocket and her wand down at her side, and she listened to everything, and went away. He had told them about memories; it came to about a minute. He went up and down the line. He didn’t say *Good, try again*.

Hopkins had come every week for ten months. He would stand a little to one side, long and mild, with the wand in his left hand, shut his eyes, and frown. He’d been through a lot of them, he said, in an apologetic way, when you asked him. There had been a summer at Whitstable. There’d been his nan. He would get to the place and there’d be somebody’s voice, and a very pretty cup and saucer; and nothing would come but a slight damp breath at the end of the wand. Then he would open his eyes, and look at the wand, and he’d say “Sorry” to it, under his breath.

“*Expecto Patronum*,” said Hopkins.

He didn’t say it very loudly. It wasn’t any different from all the other times. Harry had been looking at somebody else. He heard Dean’s voice stop. He turned round, and a little silver thing had come out from the end of the wand, and it was hanging in the air about a foot from Hopkins’s nose.

It hadn’t got a shape. It was about as big as a bread roll. It was soft, and rather lopsided, and it wavered; it gave off a thin clear light, like the light from under a door, and it stayed. It stayed there for quite a long time. Hopkins stood looking at it with his eyebrows slowly going up.

It had gone very quiet, all the way down the room. Somebody had started to clap, at the kitchen end. It was a heavy, thoughtful noise. Then they were all doing it, and Dean had let out a whoop, and the first-years were jumping up and down without any idea why. It went out. Hopkins went on standing there, with the wand held away from his body. He looked at the end of it. Harry saw his lips part. He saw them begin to make the shape. He stopped. He seemed to think about it, for a moment, with a slight puzzled line between his eyebrows. He closed his mouth, and he put the wand in his sleeve.

Harry looked round. Neville had come up the room, without saying anything, and stood by the wall with his pot. He’d seen that. He had his eyes on Hopkins, and they’d gone very bright. He didn’t mention it. He just bent down, after a moment, and did something to one of the leaves.

“What was it?” said Harry. It came out rather hoarse. “The memory?”

Hopkins looked at him. He had light tired eyes, and he didn’t hurry them.

“I don’t think I’ll say,” said Hopkins. “Thank you.”

They broke up at nine. Neville was showing a very small girl how you stood. He’d got his hand on her elbow, and he moved it an inch; and when he saw Harry coming, he straightened up, and went pink, and looked at the wall.

“Harry,” he said. “Can I ask you something? Not here.” He got it out with an effort. He was looking at a place on Harry’s collar. “That thing that you did. To her. People say—They say it went from here to here. I’ve thought about it. I think about it a lot, as a matter of fact. It’s only that she’s still got my mum’s— I wondered if you could show me.”

There was a smell of fresh bread in the room. He could hear somebody laughing. Harry looked at Neville Longbottom: at the round mild face, and the jaw that had set crooked. He looked at the pot, and it had a rosette on it. He knew what it would take. He knew it would be the easiest thing in the world. It would go up that arm like water up a wick. He’d only have to say *Yes*, and take him down to a cold room, and put his hand over that big warm careful hand, and Neville would thank him.

“No,” said Harry.

It was the only good thing that he did that month. He wrote it down afterwards, in his head.

Neville looked at him for about a second and a half. He nodded.

“All right,” said Neville.

He caught Draco Malfoy at the turn of the stairs.

He had been waiting. It was by the Charms corridor, and there was a bit of late sun. The boy had got taller by about half an inch, and he came along by himself, close to the wall, with his bag on his shoulder and his bow tie perfectly straight. He still came to the long room, on Tuesdays and Thursdays. He’d stand against the wall. He had got a good Shield, Neville said, for twelve; he had worked out where to put his feet. He’d never produced so much as a wisp from the end of his wand, and he had stopped trying some time in the spring.

“Malfoy,” said Harry.

It stopped. It looked up. He’d been frightened of that, and it was worse than he’d been expecting. It wasn’t that the face was unhappy. It was a nice clean pale face, with very good manners. There was nothing on it. He had known another boy with that nose, and he’d seen him cry in a bathroom, and that one had been a good deal more alive.

He’d put a coin in that child’s pocket, a year ago next month, and used him as you’d use a bell on a cat. He’d got fond of him. That hadn’t been in the plan.

“D’you still have it?” said Harry. “The coin.”

For a moment nothing happened. Then a small hand went to a pocket, and came out, and opened. It lay on the palm. It had been polished.

“How was your summer?”

“We had the fish,” said Draco.

“Right.” He got down on one knee. He wasn’t sure why he’d done that. It brought their faces about level. “Listen. You remember what I said to you. When I gave you that. If anybody ever asks you to go anywhere. Doesn’t matter who. Anybody, however nice they are. You tell me first. You said you would.”

The pale eyes rested on him. He had seen them on a very old man. They went over his face, in no hurry at all, from one side to the other.

“Did I?” said Draco.

“You said. By the armour.” He held out his own hand, and turned it over, and there was a Galleon in it, just like the other. “That’s mine. Look. Hermione did it. It goes both ways. Nobody ever bothered, because we only ever used it the one way. You don’t have to send me a message. You don’t have to find me or say anything to anybody. You just hold it in your hand and think, and it’ll get hot. Mine will. I’ll know. I’ll come wherever you are, it doesn’t matter what time it is. I’ll come straight away.”

He heard himself say that. He heard how it sounded. There was a field in Wiltshire, with some thistles.

Draco looked at the coin in Harry’s hand, and at his own. He seemed to go through it, from the top. Harry waited. He had waited outside a good many doors; he had thought he knew what it was like. A bit of dust was going round in the sun. Very slowly, by about half an inch, one shoulder rose, and it sank.

He didn’t say anything. He closed his fingers. He put the coin back in his pocket, and he patted the place, once, as you’d make sure of a ticket; and he went on along the wall towards Herbology, with his hands at his sides.

*The Lestrange vault is empty*, the Headmaster said on the Saturday, in about four more words than that. It had been cleared in the third week of June, while the Aurors were busy, with a key. So that was the cup. That was the ring gone and the cup gone, and nobody knew where; there was the snake; and he’d known exactly where everything was, once, it had all been on a list. He sat in the chair and looked at the bowl of lemon drops. Dumbledore’s left hand was lying on the blotter, and there was something the matter with the ends of the fingers. He didn’t mention it. Harry didn’t ask.

It happened on the Wednesday.

It wasn’t a bad evening, and he would think about that. She’d been reading, Thucydides by then, and he had been on the rug, seeing to the fire. At ten o’clock she shut the book, stood up from the old chair and looked about her for her bag, and she said something in that pleasant voice about the weather for Saturday. He’d been sitting nine feet away from her for four weeks. He had heard Herodotus and the Peloponnesian War, and he had been told what the forecast was. She walked across the rug towards the door, in her long sleeves, about a yard away from him, with a piece of hair coming down at the back of her neck. She would go down by the east stairs. She’d be a thin straight back at the far end of a corridor, in a hundred years’ time, not looking round. He hadn’t heard her say his name for fifty-one days.

He was on his feet. He didn’t remember that. He’d got her by the arm.

It was the right arm, above the elbow, and he’d got it hard. He felt the book slide. He felt how thin it was; his fingers went nearly the whole way round. She stopped. She didn’t pull. She stood there, quite still, with her back half turned to him. He could smell soap. His heart was going like something trying to get out, and he had her, he actually had her, she was warm, there she was. There was a great wave of relief going through him. It was rushing up from under his ribs, it was the best thing that he had felt since June. It went all along his arm. It came out of his mouth. It didn’t feel like saying anything.

“*Mine*,” said Harry.

He had never said that to her. Not like that. They’d said it a good many times, across a Great Hall, without moving their lips. They had said it on a sofa, with toast. He’d said it once with his hand round her arm, and she had started to laugh.

She turned her head. She did it slowly, and she looked at his hand. There it lay on her sleeve, with the white letters on it. She looked for a while. Then she raised her eyes as far as his face.

She’d have had the book out by now. That was what he thought. She would have said *Ten points*. *Ten points from Gryffindor, for grabbing*. There would have been a dry look, and a hard little pinch somewhere, and she’d have told him what it was going to cost. He stood there and waited for it, with his breath coming short. He would have given anything in the world.

“How nice,” said Lyra.

It was very pleasant. It was just the voice that she’d have used to say that the rain seemed to be stopping. Her eyes were on his, wide open, the colour of nothing. There wasn’t anyone there.

His fingers came open. He didn’t seem to be doing it himself. He looked at them. There was a crease in the grey wool, in the shape of four fingers and a thumb, and it slowly began to go away. He had done that. He had done that twice now. He’d said *Sorry* the last time, on a hill. His hand went to his pocket, by itself, like something going back to its kennel. *You don’t take hold of me.* Somebody ought to have been ashamed of that. He looked for it, he felt round for it, in the place where it should have been.

Something had settled, a little behind his right ear.

It was very slight. It wasn’t a voice. It didn’t say anything. It was more the way that a cat will turn round once and lie down. It had the air of being rather satisfied; and for one blink, God help him, so had he.

She picked up her book. She smoothed her sleeve, once, with the flat of her left hand, and he saw there was a tremor in that. She went to the door.

“Do mind that fire,” said Lyra. “It’s spitting.”

He followed her on the Sunday.

He’d been going to stop. He told himself so, on the Thursday, and again on the Friday night; he took the notebook out of his robes, and held it over the flames, and he put it back. He had to know what it was. That was all. There was something wrong with her, and she wouldn’t say. Nobody could be expected to stand that. It was what you’d do for anybody that you wanted to keep safe.

It was the thirteenth of October, and it was raining again. At twenty-five to three she came out of the Slytherin passage, by herself, with nothing in her hands. He gave her thirty yards. He had the Cloak pulled down and his shoes in his pocket. She went up the marble staircase, walking neither fast nor slowly. She didn’t look behind her. She knew all about looking behind you. She turned off at the second floor, along the corridor where nobody went, past the place that had once had writing on the wall, and he stopped at the corner with his heart going, because he knew where this was. He knew that door. It had a sign on it, *OUT OF ORDER*, that had been there since before he was born, or since he was twelve. There was water all over the floor. There had been a sink with a tap that had a little snake on it.

She went in. She shut it after her.

He gave it a slow count of a hundred. He went along the wall, in his socks. One of them was very good. He got down on the wet stone, by the door, with the Cloak drawn over his knees, and he put his ear against the crack. It smelt of old drains. Somewhere inside a cistern was running, and there was a thin far-off sound of somebody crying, that went on and on, and paid no attention to anyone. That was the ghost.

Then he heard her.

She had a chair, or she was standing with her back to something. It was quite close. He could hear every word. It wasn’t the pleasant voice. He’d got used to that; he’d got so used to it that it gave him a shock. This was clear and quick, and rather low. It went on evenly, with a slight pause every so often. It was the voice she’d use for going through a column.

“—still seven of them, in the Society, who would answer to that. I don’t recommend it. The girl has stopped eating.”

There was a pause. He held his breath, and he couldn’t hear anybody else. She seemed to be listening to something. A mirror, he thought; he had had one once, and you’d only need to hold it up. It was Sunday; it had always been Sunday. Her father was in a cell under the Wizengamot, and he had thought that was over. Nobody had sat down on a rug with him for four months and said *What shall we give them?*

“Yes,” said the voice. “Lupin has it from Dumbledore. The hand is getting worse. I have that from three people.”

*Who*, he thought. *Who are you talking to?* He could feel his pulse beating against the door. It was all right. It would be all right. It was only scraps. It had to be. It was the sort of thing that they would have put in anyway, it wasn’t worth a Knut, it was what she’d been doing all along, on the Sunday, for the sake of her brother. He would ask her. She would have some very good reason. She had always—

“And Potter,” said Lyra.

The water ran. A long way off, somebody sobbed, and hiccupped.

She had paused. He heard her breathe in. He had known that breath for five years. It was the small one, that she took on a staircase, before she said a thing that she had made up her mind to say. It was very clear. It was exact and thoughtful, and it might have been the price of something.

“He has a cauldron,” said Lyra. “He’s frightened of his own right hand.”
