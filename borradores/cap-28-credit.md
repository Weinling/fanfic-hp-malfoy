# Chapter 28: Credit

Potter had given her wand back under the cloak, handle first, with a small, solemn bow from the waist, as if they were about to duel. She had taken it without a word. There had not been room under the cloak to hit him again.

“Where were you all afternoon?” Crabbe had asked, that evening.

“Detention.”

Crabbe had looked at her for a moment. Then she had reached over and taken a beech leaf out of Lyra’s hair, and put it on the table between them, and gone back to her homework.

That had been Thursday. On Friday afternoon the fourth-year Slytherins and the fourth-year Gryffindors went down across the frozen lawn to Hagrid’s hut, as they did every week, and stood along the paddock fence in two separate lines with their breath smoking, and looked at the crates.

There were about a hundred of them. They were open at the top, and from inside them came a strange rattling noise, like very small explosions, and a smell like rotten fish.

“Blast-Ended Skrewts!” said Hagrid, beaming over the top of the nearest crate. “Newly hatched. Thought we’d do a project on ’em. Yeh’ll be raisin’ ’em, see what they turn into.”

Lyra looked into the crate.

The skrewts looked like deformed, shell-less lobsters, horribly pale and slimy, with legs sticking out in very odd places and no visible heads. Some of them had stings. Some of them had little sucking things on their bellies that she did not care to think about. Every so often one of them gave off a spurt of sparks from its back end with a small *phut*, and shot forward several inches, and landed on another one.

“And what,” said Lyra, “are they *for*?”

“For?” Hagrid looked down at her over the crates, puzzled, as though she had asked what a sunset was for.

“What do they *do*, Professor? What is the point of them? I only ask because I should like to know what I’m going to be risking my fingers on.”

“That’s next lesson,” said Hagrid, a little huffily. “Today yer jus’ feedin’ ’em. Now, I’m not sure what they eat, so I’ve got a few diff’rent things—ant eggs, frog livers, bit o’ grass snake. Try ’em with a bit of each.”

He did not know what they ate. He had a hundred of them in boxes and he did not know what they ate. Lyra exchanged a long look with the nearest crate, and pulled on her dragon-hide gloves.

Behind the hut, in the paddock where the pumpkins had been, a large grey shape lifted its head.

Buckbeak was tethered where he had been since last year, on a long rope to a stake in the frozen earth, and he had seen her. He looked at her down the length of his beak with one fierce orange eye. Then, slowly, with great dignity, he bent his scaly knees and bowed.

Lyra glanced along the fence. The Slytherins were peering into crates, and the Gryffindors were peering into theirs, and Hagrid was bent double over a box of frog livers. She inclined her head to the hippogriff, very slightly, as her mother inclined her head to people at the opera whom she did not intend to speak to but had no wish to offend. Buckbeak seemed to find it satisfactory. He went back to the pumpkin he had been eating.

She owed him a steak. She had told him so in February, with a werewolf behind them. She did not like to think how long that had been on the books.

“Ugh! It bit me—the filthy thing *bit* me!” Parkinson was shaking his hand at the next crate along. Nothing had bitten him; he had his gloves on. “We ought to kill the lot of them, if you ask me. Before they kill us. Stamp on them. It’d be a kindness.”

“Ah, don’ say that,” said Hagrid, wounded. “They’re only babies.”

“Hang on,” said Potter.

He had come round the end of the fence from the Gryffindor side, pulling on a pair of thick gloves, and before anybody could tell him not to, he reached into Parkinson’s crate and lifted one of the skrewts out by the middle. It was about six inches long and it did not like it. Its legs went in every direction at once. Potter turned it over, so that its pale soft belly, with all its sucking things, was pointing up at Parkinson’s face.

“You just haven’t given it a chance,” he said earnestly. “It’s quite sweet, if you look at it long enough.” He held it a little closer. “*Boo*.”

The skrewt went *phut*.

Parkinson went over backwards into the frozen mud, with a noise like a wardrobe falling downstairs. Somebody on the Gryffindor side laughed out loud. Goyle, who had been standing next to him, made a small sound and ran into Hagrid’s hut and shut the door.

Then Potter turned round, with the skrewt still wriggling in his hands, and came towards Lyra.

He did it in full view of both Houses. He was grinning, and there was nothing in it but the old war, the one the whole school had been watching for three years: Potter and Malfoy, at it again. Nobody looking on could have seen anything else. Lyra knew that perfectly well. She also knew that he was enjoying himself far too much.

“Malfoy. Want to hold him?”

“Come one step closer,” said Lyra, “and I’ll—”

He came one step closer.

There was a broken branch on the ground by her foot. She picked it up and threw it, overarm, and it hit the skrewt exactly where she had aimed: on the end of its tail.

The tail exploded.

There was a bang and a shower of sparks and a smell of burnt fish, and Potter yelped and dropped the skrewt, which shot away across the frozen mud on a little jet of flame and buried itself in a pumpkin. Potter stood looking at the front of his robes. They were smoking slightly.

“Ah,” said Hagrid. “Yeah. Forgot ter mention. The tails explode.”

Lyra brushed her gloves together and went back to her crate.

When she looked up again, Granger was watching her across the paddock. Granger had a frog liver in one hand and an expression on her face that Lyra did not care for at all: the expression of somebody who had recently seen a ferret, and done some arithmetic, and was trying very hard not to smile. Lyra looked back at her coldly until she went pink and turned away.

At the far end of the Gryffindor line, Weasley was feeding his skrewts on his own. He had not laughed at Parkinson. He had not looked up when the tail went off. Potter went back to the Gryffindor side with his robes still smoking, and stood next to Granger, and was cheerful for the rest of the lesson in a loud, careless way that did not fool Lyra for a moment.

Weasley went back up to the castle on his own, too. He left before everybody else, with his hands jammed in his pockets and his head down, and by the time Lyra had got her gloves off and told Crabbe and Goyle to go on without her, he was halfway up the slope, a long thin figure with red hair, walking fast.

She caught him up at the top, where the path came out on to the gravel by the great oak doors. He did not hear her coming. He had his back to her, and his shoulders up round his ears, and it would have been the easiest thing in the world to say something to the back of his head that he would hear for a week.

Her hand had gone to her sleeve, where her wand was. She noticed it there, and took it away, and lengthened her stride until she had come round in front of him.

“Weasley.”

He stopped. He looked at her as though she were one more thing the day had decided to do to him.

“What do you want, Malfoy?”

“What a charming welcome. Your brothers manage better. They’re loud, and they’re vulgar, and they’ve never had a thought between them that wasn’t a joke, but at least they don’t go about with a face like a wet weekend.”

It was the wrong thing to say. She saw it go in. Weasley’s ears went red, and then the rest of him.

“Oh, right,” he said. “Course. You’d know all about it, wouldn’t you. Must be nice.” His voice had gone flat and nasty, in a way she had not heard from him before. “Invisibility Cloak. Secret girlfriend. Lovely life, being Harry Potter, isn’t it? Everything handed to him.”

Lyra’s hand found the ring in her pocket, and stopped there.

She did not let it reach her face. Moody, she thought. Moody, with his eye, telling Gryffindors—but no. Moody would tell nobody anything; it was the one thing she was sure of about him. Then who? Then *how*?

“Jealous, Weasley?” she said. “Is that what this is?”

“Of him? Having you?” Weasley gave a short laugh. “No, thanks. Only Harry could put up with you. Anybody else’d be sick.”

“Only somebody as dreary as you would think so.” She heard her own voice and did not like it, and went on anyway, because she had not come up this slope to trade insults with Weasley; she could have done that in her sleep. “But I didn’t know you were quite so petty. I thought you were his friend. I thought that was the one thing you were good for.”

“If you’re that worried about him, *you* cheer him up. That’s what you’re for, isn’t it? You’re the girlfriend.”

“I *would* if I *could*!”

It came out much too loud. It rang off the great doors. Lyra heard it go, and could not get it back, and the rest came out after it, because it was too late now to do anything else.

“You sleep in the next bed to him. You’re in every lesson he’s in. You eat at the same table three times a day and you can talk to him whenever you like, about anything, and *nobody looks twice*. I get him for ten minutes under a cloak once a fortnight, if he’s lucky and I’m not sulking. And you—you’ve got all of that, and you’re throwing it in the lake.”

She stopped. She looked round, quickly, at the doors and the gravel and the empty slope. There was nobody. A few Slytherins were coming up from Hagrid’s, a long way down, much too far off to hear.

Weasley was staring at her. He had his mouth slightly open.

Lyra took a breath, and lowered her voice, and made it cold, which was easier.

“I watched him today,” she said. “Being cheerful. With a skrewt. He was putting it on for the whole class, and you didn’t look at him once. If you aren’t going to help him, Weasley, at least stop getting in his way. He’s risking his life.”

“His *life*?”

“Work it out yourself. Do I have to explain everything to you?” She drew her cloak round her. Then, because she had come this far and there was only one thing left, she said it. “And don’t you dare be jealous of him. You’ve got a family so big you can’t find the end of it. Brothers who’d hex anybody who touched you. A mother who *knits*.” She heard that in her own mouth, and could not quite believe it, and went on. “It’s the only thing he has ever wanted, and you’ve got it, and you’re standing out here sulking because he didn’t tell you something.”

Weasley did not say anything at all.

“And if you tell anybody what you think you know,” said Lyra, “about anything, I will make you very sorry, by every means I have. And I have a great many.”

She turned and went in through the great doors without waiting for an answer.

Behind her, she heard him say, to nobody, or possibly to the gravel, in a voice of honest wonder:

“She thinks it’s a *secret*.”

Her father’s owl came on Sunday morning, a great tawny eagle owl that the whole Slytherin table recognised, and that ate nobody’s toast. It held out its leg to her as though it were doing her a kindness.

She had written to him on Monday. It had been a very good letter. It had said that she was well, and working hard, and keeping well clear of the Tournament, as she had promised Mother; that Draco had written her two whole sides about Viktor Krum; that Krum ate at the Slytherin table every day and she had not yet found a way to ask him for an autograph that would not make her look like a first-year; and that she supposed, since Father knew everybody, that he would not happen to know the Headmaster of Durmstrang?

*My treasure*, her father had written back, in his long, beautiful hand, *how like you to think of your brother. Igor Karkaroff and I are old friends. I enclose a few lines for him, which you will deliver yourself; he will be delighted to see you, and I think you will find him generous. There is no need to trouble Severus with any of this. Or anybody else.*

The enclosure was sealed with the Malfoy crest, in green wax. It was thick. A few lines did not run to three sheets of parchment.

Lyra turned it over in her fingers, under the table, and did not break the seal.

Her father was using her to carry something. She was using him to open a door. She thought that probably only one of them knew what the other was doing, and that it was her; but she had been Lucius Malfoy’s daughter for fourteen years, and she knew better than to be certain.

*I don’t take anything you bring me*, Moody had said, *because I don’t know who gave it to you to bring.*

Well. She knew now. And she was going to carry it anyway.

The Durmstrang ship lay at anchor out in the lake, black against the black water, with a few dim lights burning behind its portholes like the windows of a house where everybody had gone to bed. Lyra went down to the shore after dinner, in her best cloak, with her father’s letter in one pocket and a square of good card for the autograph in the other, and stood at the edge of the water and did not quite know how one knocked on a ship.

In the end she simply said, clearly, “Lyra Malfoy, for Professor Karkaroff,” to the dark.

Something creaked out on the lake. A long wooden gangplank came sliding across the surface of the water towards her, quite dry, as if the lake had been told to keep its hands to itself, and settled its end on the shingle at her feet.

Karkaroff was waiting at the top of it. He was tall and thin, in furs of a rich silvery colour, with short white hair and a little goatee that finished in a curl, and a smile that showed a great many yellowish teeth while his pale eyes went over her cloak, and her shoes, and the crest on her father’s letter.

“Lucius’s daughter!” He held out both hands to her as though she were a long-lost niece. “And the very image of him. Come in, come in, out of the cold.”

His cabin was low and dark and panelled, and much too warm, with a stove in one corner and a great many furs thrown over everything. He read her father’s letter standing by the lamp, all three sheets of it, and when he had finished he folded it very small and put it inside his coat, and his smile had become a little fixed.

“Your father,” he said, “was a friend to me once, when I had very few friends. When I came out of—when I came back into the world, shall we say. A little money. A word in the right place. One does not forget.” He poured something hot out of a jug into a silver cup and pressed it into her hands. It smelt very strongly of rum. “He wished you to come to Durmstrang, you know. Did he ever tell you? Your mother would not hear of it. Too far, she said. Too cold.” He laughed. “A pity. We would have made a princess of you.”

Lyra had not known that. She kept it, and did not turn it over yet, and held the silver cup, and did not drink.

She had a great deal of practice at men like Karkaroff. Her father had them to dinner. She praised the ship, which was hideous, and Durmstrang, which she had never seen, and above all Viktor Krum, who was a genius, an artist, the finest Seeker of his generation; and Karkaroff purred and grew expansive, and told her how he had found the boy, and trained the boy, and how the boy would bring the Triwizard Cup home to Durmstrang as surely as the sun rose.

“If it had been a fair contest,” said Lyra, with a small sigh. “It’s such a pity about the Goblet.”

His face changed so fast that she nearly spilt the rum.

“*Pity*? It is an outrage! Two champions! Two, for Hogwarts! And what does Dumbledore do? He shrugs, he smiles, he says the Goblet has spoken!” Karkaroff struck the table with the flat of his hand. “Behind everybody’s back. As always. That man has done nothing to my face in thirty years.”

Lyra watched him rage. She had been brought up among people who lied for a living, and she had watched her father lie to the Minister for Magic across a dinner table without a hair out of place, and she knew what it looked like. This did not look like that. Karkaroff was not pretending. He did not know who had put Potter’s name in that cup, and he was furious that somebody had, and the fury was entirely real.

So it had not been him.

“But surely,” she said, “it might not have been the Headmaster at all. The Ministry brought the Goblet, didn’t they? Somebody there—”

“Who? Bagman?” Karkaroff snorted. “Ludo Bagman could not confuse a goblin, let alone that cup. And Crouch—” He shook his head. “Barty Crouch would cut out his own tongue before he bent a rule. A man who sent his own son to Azkaban. His own son, to die there. And when they did for the Longbottoms, he—”

He stopped. He glanced, for no reason Lyra could see, at the door. Then he smiled at her again, with all his yellow teeth.

“Forgive me. These are not stories for a young lady.”

Lyra smiled back, and did not ask. Her father had taught her that too: that the surest way to lose a thing a man had let slip was to bend down and pick it up in front of him. She looked into her cup instead, and said something admiring about the panelling.

There was a knock, and the door opened, and Viktor Krum came in, stooping under the lintel.

He was even more round-shouldered close to than he was across the Great Hall, with heavy black brows and a nose curved like a bird of prey’s, and he looked at her without much interest, as though she were one more thing Karkaroff had found to show him.

“Viktor! Come, come. This is Lyra Malfoy, the daughter of a very dear friend of mine.”

“Lyra Mal-foy,” said Krum, carefully, as if he were putting the syllables down one at a time on a table.

She asked him for the autograph for her brother. He signed it without enthusiasm, with Karkaroff’s quill, in two alphabets, one of which she could not read. He did not look up while he did it.

“She flies a Firebolt, Viktor!” said Karkaroff. “Lucius bought it for her. A Firebolt!”

That made him look up.

“A Firebolt,” said Krum. For the first time he looked at her properly: not at her face, she noticed, but at her hands, and her shoulders, the way a Seeker looked at another Seeker. “You fly Seeker?”

“Yes.”

He considered this. “Then perhaps one day we fly,” he said, and nodded to her, and that was apparently all.

“There!” said Karkaroff, delighted. “What a pair you would make!”

Lyra smiled her mother’s smile, the one that meant precisely nothing, and thought that Viktor Krum was undoubtedly a genius on a broom, and on a broom only; but he had looked at her hands before her face, and she found she rather respected that. She stood up to go.

The gangplank slid away behind her into the dark as soon as her feet were on the shingle, and the lights in the portholes went out one by one.

She went up the slope towards the castle with the signed card in her pocket, and the silver cup still full on Karkaroff’s table, and turned it all over as she climbed.

It had not been Karkaroff. That was one thing. Whoever had Confunded the Goblet was somebody strong enough to fool an ancient magical object that had been fooling nobody for seven hundred years, and was very probably still in this castle, and was not a vain, frightened, furious man in silver furs on a ship. That was a second thing. And the third was the one Karkaroff had wished he had not said: a man who had sent his own son to Azkaban to die, and something that had been done to the Longbottoms, which she did not understand at all, and which had made Karkaroff look at the door.

Longbottom. She thought of a round-faced boy in first year, scrabbling on the floor for the bits of a Remembrall. She did not know what any of it meant. But she knew what it was worth, and she knew exactly who to give it to; and it was not Mad-Eye Moody, who would take nothing from her. It was the boy with the cloak.

There was somebody standing on the path.

She saw the staff first, and then the shape leaning on it, and then the eye: a faint blue gleam in the dark, turning, and coming to rest on her.

“Evening, Miss Malfoy,” said Moody. “Igor keeping well?”

Lyra stopped. She did not go round him, and she did not look away. She stood on the path in front of him with her hands at her sides, and faced him.

“He doesn’t care for you very much, Professor.”

“No,” said Moody. “He wouldn’t.” He looked at her a moment longer. Then he turned, and went stumping off along the bank in the dark, without another word, his wooden leg going *clunk* on the frozen path, until she could not hear it any more.

Lyra stood where he had left her. She knew exactly what he had seen: Lucius Malfoy’s daughter, coming back in the dark from an old Death Eater’s ship, with her father’s letter delivered and her pockets full of whatever had been given her to bring.

She had gone in by the front door, under her own name. It had not made the slightest difference.
