# Chapter 41: The Sleeve

Draco had been up since six, and by breakfast he had already been to the east cottage and back.

“He’s taken it,” he announced, sliding into his chair with grass on his knees. “The chess set. The one with the pieces that argue. He said I could have it for good, at Easter, and now he’s taken it back, because he says he needs something to think with.” He reached for the toast without looking at it. “And he doesn’t play on Sundays any more. He says he’s got work now.”

“Work,” said Lyra.

“For somebody. He didn’t say who.” Draco buttered with tremendous energy, mostly the tablecloth. “But he’s much better. He used to spill his tea, you know, when he held the cup. His hands went like this.” He demonstrated, with the butter knife, and a pat of butter left it and landed in the sugar. “They don’t now. Not even holding a cup. He says he’s never felt better in his life.”

“Draco,” said their mother. “The sugar.”

Draco retrieved the butter from the sugar with his fingers and ate it, which was not what she had meant, and their father laughed.

He had laughed a great deal since the platform. Lyra had noticed it on the drive home, in the carriage, and again at dinner, and again this morning when he came down the stairs: a loose, easy, delighted laugh, like a man who has been carrying something heavy for a long time up a hill and has just been told he may put it down. He sat at the head of the table in his dressing gown with the *Daily Prophet* propped against the coffee pot, and every so often he read a line of it aloud, in the voice he used for the cleverest bits of a play.

Her mother did not laugh. She ate half a slice of toast, very slowly, and looked out of the window at the lawn, where a white peacock was walking up and down in the sun as though it were waiting to be let in.

“Listen to this,” said Lucius. “*The Ministry wishes to question Igor Karkaroff, Headmaster of the Durmstrang Institute, in connection with the death of Mr Bartemius Crouch.* In connection.” He turned the page with a snap. “Poor Igor. He will be so offended. He was never in connection with anything in his life; he was always the one sent out for the drinks.”

“Did he do it?” said Draco, interested.

“Of course not, darling. He was nowhere near.” Lucius folded the paper back on itself and reached for his cup. “No. That was a family matter, by all accounts. Barty was always going to settle it one day, and I don’t suppose anybody who knew them is very much surprised.” He drank. “He had an hour to spare, I’m told. An hour, before the Aurors came up the path. One wonders what they stopped for on the way. Tea, possibly.”

Lyra had her cup in her hand. She did not put it down.

*We were an hour too late*, Moody had said, in the Entrance Hall, among the trunks. And she had stood there and inclined her head to it, as to a bow, as if the hour had been his.

Under the edge of the toast rack, where nobody could see, she counted it on the tablecloth with one finger. Saturday, the podium, and a man in a neat dark suit walking past her with a crate in his arms and a face smoothed out like a sheet. Dumbledore three yards off, with his back to her. Three yards. She had looked at his back and thought of the north and her father’s green wax, and she had not gone. Sunday, nothing. Monday, her father’s letter in her sleeve all day, and the Owlery. Tuesday morning, the tin, and Goyle running up the stairs with it; and Potter, she supposed, out of his bed before it was dark; and Dumbledore; and Moody and two others, up a path, an hour after a son had gone down it.

If she had gone down off that platform on the Saturday night, Barty Crouch would be alive.

She counted it again, from the Saturday, in case she had dropped a day somewhere. She had not. Four days, and the last of them an hour short.

“Lyra,” said Draco. “The marmalade. I’ve asked you *twice*.”

She passed it to him. Her hand was quite steady, which was something.

“Thank you,” said Draco, with heavy irony, and their father laughed again, at that, as he laughed at everything Draco said this summer; and her mother turned from the window and looked, not at Draco, but at Lyra, and at the cup Lyra was still holding, which had gone cold some time ago.

Lyra drank from it, cold, to have something to do, and asked Draco what work an old man in a cottage could possibly have.

Her father sent for her after lunch.

The study was the coolest room in the Manor in summer, and the darkest, and he had the curtains half drawn against the glare off the lawn, so that the four generations of Malfoys in the panelling were mostly frames and faces, pale in the brown gloom, and not much else. He had a glass of something at his elbow. He shut the door behind her himself, which Pobble usually did, and she heard the privacy charm come up in the walls like a held note.

“Sit down, my treasure. No. Sit there, where I can see you.”

She sat. He did not go back behind the desk. He leaned on the front of it, with the glass in his hand, and looked at her the way he had looked at her on the platform, as if he could not quite believe his luck.

“I wrote to you,” he said. “I know I did. But a letter is a poor thing for news like this, and you have had a week of the castle since, and Dumbledore talking.” He smiled. “I want you to hear it in this room. He has asked after you.”

Lyra waited.

“By name.” He let it sit there between them, like a gift on a table. “He knows whose hand carried the letter in November. He spoke of it. He said that Lucius Malfoy’s daughter had done in an evening what grown men had failed to do in a year, and that he would remember it.” His voice dropped a little, not for secrecy, since the walls were humming, but because there were things he said more quietly out of reverence. “He has never singled out a child before, Lyra. Not one. Not in all the years. Do you understand what that is?”

“Yes, Father.”

She did. She understood it a great deal better than he did. A man who remembered whose hand had carried a thing would remember, if it came to it, whose hand had carried the next one, and where.

“There are things I shall need from you,” said her father. “Small things. The first is the House. When you go back in September, there will be some in Slytherin who have heard, from their fathers, or guessed, and who will be excited, and who will talk. Children are careless. A careless word in the wrong corridor, while the Ministry still has its head in the sand, could cost us a great deal. I want them kept quiet. I want them kept *proud*.” He looked at her over the glass. “You can do that, I think.”

“I can do that,” said Lyra.

She had done it already, in a green-lit room on the last night of term, to a lot of frightened second-years and a knot of sixth-years with their fathers’ faces. He did not know that. She found she did not want to tell him.

“And the second,” said her father, “is the Potter boy.”

She had known it was coming since the platform. She had her face ready for it.

“We’ve parted.”

“Have you.” He did not ask why. He turned the glass a little in his fingers, watching the light move in it. “A boy who confides is worth sitting beside, I said. I still think so. Parting is not a door shut, my darling, it is only a door, and you are a great deal cleverer than he is.” He smiled at her, fondly. “If he should wish to confide in you again, let him.”

He did not believe her. That was plain. And it did not matter to him in the least, which was worse.

He looked at her for a moment longer, quite carefully, as he looked across a dinner table at a man he was about to buy something from, to see what the man was keeping back. Lyra let him look. Behind her face, somewhere he could not reach, there was a tablecloth and a finger counting days on it, and a platform, and a man walking past her with a crate. She did not hide it, exactly. She simply did not let it come up to the surface, as one does not let a cough come up in church; and her father looked, and found his daughter, and seemed satisfied.

“Severus was late,” he said, in a different voice, almost idly, turning to the window. “On the night. Did you know? Two hours. He came in when it was nearly over, with mud on him, and said that Dumbledore had kept him at the castle and he had come the moment he could get away.” He shrugged. “He had his reasons, he says. The Dark Lord accepted them. I suppose I must.”

He did not sound as though he supposed anything of the kind, and Lyra thought of a man in an emptying stand, holding his own arm with his eyes shut, listening.

Then her father lifted his glass, not to her, but to the wall.

She followed it. It was the nearest of the four portraits, the one hung lowest, in the darkest corner, where the curtain’s shadow fell: an old man in grey with his chin sunk on his chest and his hands folded on the head of a cane, asleep. He was always asleep. Lyra had never once in her life seen him open his eyes, though the great-uncle above him cleared his throat at silences, and the ladies at the far end talked to each other about hats. She had supposed, when she was small, that some portraits simply came out sleepy, as some children did.

“Your grandfather,” said her father, “would have given a great deal to see this summer.”

He drank. Lyra looked at the sleeping face, which had her father’s long chin and nothing else she could see of anybody.

She knew the story, as one knows the story of a house: Abraxas Malfoy, who had been at school with half the old names in the country and had outlived most of them, and had died of dragon pox when she was too small to remember him, very fast, in the east wing, with the doors shut, so that it should not spread. There had been no lying in state. She had been told that the dragon pox did dreadful things to a face, and that it was better to remember him as the painter had.

“He would have been proud of you,” said her father, and finished his glass, and put it down. “Run along. Your mother will want you for something, I expect. She generally does.”

Her mother did not want her for anything. Lyra met her at the top of the stairs at dusk, on the way down to dinner, and her mother stopped, and looked at her hair.

“Put it up,” she said. “It’s too hot to wear it down at the table.”

Lyra put a hand to it. It was perfectly all right down.

“And eat something tonight. You had a cold cup of tea for breakfast and nothing at lunch that I saw.” Her mother reached out and took a thread off the shoulder of Lyra’s robes, a thread that Lyra could not see, and held it between her finger and thumb for a moment, and let it go over the banister. “You’ll want your strength.”

“For what?”

Her mother looked at her. It was not the careful, unreadable face from the top of the drive. It was the other one, the one Lyra had seen exactly once, on a platform, when her mother’s eyes went past her down to the far end of the train and stayed there.

Then it was gone, and Narcissa Malfoy was going on down the stairs ahead of her, one hand on the rail, very straight.

“For the summer, darling,” she said, without turning round. “It’s going to be a long one.”

She waited until the house was asleep.

That was not true. She waited until the house had gone to bed, which in that house was not the same thing; and then she put on her dressing gown and went down the main stairs in her bare feet, past the long gallery where her great-great-grandmother slept in her frame with her mouth open, and along the passage to the study, where there was a line of light under the door.

She had decided at breakfast. She had decided it with a cold cup in her hand and her finger on the tablecloth, and she had spent the rest of the day finding out whether she meant it. She did. She had once looked at a thing and kept it to herself for three days because it was not yet the whole of it, and a man had died in the time it took her to be sure. She was not going to do it again. Not about this. Not in her own house.

She knocked, and went in without waiting.

Her father was at the desk with a letter. He looked up, and was surprised, and then pleased, as he was always pleased to see her.

“Lyra. It’s gone midnight.”

“I know.” She shut the door behind her. The charm hummed up in the walls. She did not sit down. “Father, show me your arm.”

The study was very quiet.

She had never asked him anything like it. In that house you did not; you went the long way round, by the question next to it, or you waited to be told, and most of the time you were not told, and you learned to read what you were not told instead. He had taught her that himself, over the chessboard, when she was eight. She watched him remember teaching it to her.

She had expected him to be angry. She had been ready for that, and for the cold voice, and for *go to bed*. She had not been ready for what he did.

He smiled.

He put down his pen, and pushed back his chair, and stood up; and with his eyes on hers he unbuttoned the cuff of his left sleeve, and turned it back, once and then again, quite slowly, as a man turns back his sleeve to show a child a scar he got in a war he won.

It was black. She had thought, on the platform, that it would look like ink. It did not. It looked like something that lived under the skin and had come up to the surface to breathe: a skull, and the serpent coming out of its mouth, as dark and clear as the day it was put there, on the white inside of her father’s arm where she had never once in her life thought to look.

He did not hide it. He held it out to her, turned to the lamp, so that she should see it properly.

“There,” he said gently. “Now you know. I wondered when you would ask.” He looked down at it himself, and something went over his face that she had never seen there, not for her mother, not for the Manor, not for any of the things she had thought he loved best. “It went pale, after he fell. Grey, like an old bruise. For thirteen years I wore a faded thing on my arm and told the Ministry I had been bewitched.” He touched it with two fingers. “It came back on the night of the Third Task. All at once. I felt it as you would feel your own heart start.”

Lyra did not say anything. She was looking at his face, not his arm.

“One day,” said her father, “you will have one too. Not yet. There’s no hurry in the world; he is very patient with those who serve him well, and you have served him well already.” He rolled the sleeve down again, and buttoned the cuff, and smoothed it. “I’m glad you asked. I don’t want you to have to wonder about things in this house. Not any more. You’re one of us now, in every way that counts.”

He meant it as the kindest thing he could say. That was what made it a door, and not a gift: she had knocked, and it had opened, and she was on the other side of it, and it did not open the other way.

He came round the desk and kissed her forehead, as he did every night.

“And since we are not wondering about things,” he said, at the door, with his hand on it, like a man who has one more present in his pocket and cannot bear to keep it, “you may as well know what has put your brother out of his chess partner. The old gentleman has work again, after all these years. Errands for the Dark Lord. He is collecting some old things of his,” said her father, “that were left with friends.”

In her room she did not light the lamp.

She went to the bedside table and opened the drawer, as she had opened it every night of every summer since she was thirteen, and put her hand in, and felt for a ring.

There was a handkerchief. Soft, and folded small, and pressed. Nothing else.

She shut the drawer.

The last old thing of the Dark Lord’s that her father had kept for a friend had lived in a drawer too, in this house, in the summer she was twelve. She had asked him what it was. He had told her it was an old book.
