# Chapter 84: The Ear

She did it as Arithmancy, because it had to be done as something.

She did it in August, on the window seat of the old schoolroom, with a clean sheet and a ruled margin, in the second week that she was able to hold a pen. It lay along the inside of her left forearm, from a little below the elbow to the wrist, and it was not like her father’s. His was a thing done in a batch. Hers had been made to measure, by someone who took an interest, over three hours and a half. It was a thin grey-green snake, as fine as a line of engraving, lying in two slow curves, and it had no skull. It had a very small flat head that rested on the pulse. It had one eye, on the side towards her, and the eye was open.

There was no lid. She had looked. It was about the size of a grain of rice, and it was the colour of weak tea, and it looked at nothing.

*Let E be the ear*, wrote Lyra.

She had been given the rule by the Dark Lord himself, in the warmest possible way, when she was in a position to appreciate it. He had thought that she would like to know what she was wearing. Her part in it was to live, and that was all; it needed no attention. The rest she had established for herself, by experiment, as one does.

*1. E hears.* On the fourth of August she had told Pobble, in the linen room, with the door shut, that the second lime from the gate was to be left alone, because a thrush had got into it. Nobody else alive could have known about that thrush. On the sixth, at dinner, as they were taking away the fish, the Dark Lord had asked her how the bird was doing.

*2. E hears whatever is said within about the length of a room, through wool, through a wall of ordinary thickness, and in the bath.* She had not enjoyed finding that out.

*3. E does not see.* She had held up a sheet of paper to it, in a cupboard, with something unspeakable written on the sheet about his mother; she had held it there and waited for three days. Nothing had ever come of it.

*4. E does not sleep.* She had thought about that one for some time before she wrote it down. She had woken at four, and at five, and she’d lain with her arm outside the blankets, and looked. *She* had to sleep. So it would be hearing that, too.

*5. It is not known at any given moment if he is listening.* She underlined that. There was the whole of the difficulty. It might be nothing for a fortnight. It might be that he took it out once a week, as one takes out a good book. He would never say. He’d only, now and then, across a room, pick up some small thing she had said to somebody in a passage, and hand it back to her, with a smile, as you might return a glove.

There was a sixth. It wasn’t a matter of experiment. He had told her that as well, on the night of the thirty-first, near the end, when she had stopped being able to scream; he had taken her chin in his cool fingers, so that she should attend. Lyme was on credit. Her mother owed for that, and had not been asked to pay. On the day that the boy learnt from her what she carried, it would fall due.

*6. If H. learns of E from L.M.: N.M. pays.*

She looked at that. She had been very well taught, and she went through it with the end of her pen, for the leak. It wasn’t a spell. There was no cleverness in it. Nothing would go off. It was simply a contract, between a person and a person, and it would only be enforced if he found out. Very well: then how would he find out? There was what she might say aloud, and that was item one. And suppose that she said nothing. Suppose she took a sheet of the second-best paper, and put it into a boy’s hand, in silence, at the top of a stair.

She had got as far as that, on the window seat, with the wasps going round in the sun. She had felt something start up in her chest. It was very small and it hurt a great deal. She made herself stop, and she drew a line, and she went on.

He had a door in his head. She had seen what came through it: she’d heard him say *He’s happy*, at the top of a staircase. *I had a look at the inside of his head in the Atrium*, somebody had told her, *for a moment*. So whatever Harry knew, that long white hand could take down from the shelf. That was one. She could see it perfectly clearly, and she knew it was a very good reason, and she knew just how much it was worth.

She looked at the other for rather longer.

He would come. He’d read her note, and he would look up, and he’d go white and quiet. He’d count. He would stand very still, with his hands in his pockets, and say that it would be all right. And within the week it would have been arranged. There would have been an atlas. He would know a place, and he’d have been to see about the roof. Somebody would have been spoken to; a very old wizard would have looked at her over his half-moons, with great sadness. She would be lifted up, with extreme tenderness, and her hands would be put straight, and she would find herself on a hillside, safe, counting sheep, while her mother paid, and Draco paid, and he went and saw to it. *Stay there.* He would do it for love. He wouldn’t be able to stop himself. She’d asked him, and he had told her so, on a pink carpet, with one sock on.

She wrote that down as well. It was only fair.

*7. H. cannot be told.*

She took a long breath. She blotted it. Then, because there was a very good fire in the room, for all that it was August, she fed it the sheet, and she watched until the ruled lines had gone.

It was the first of September, and someone was standing outside her door.

She had been up since five. She had done her hair, and got her sleeves right; she’d had the dressmaker put a small flat band inside the left cuff, so that it shouldn’t ride. The trunks had gone down. At the bottom of hers, under the winter things, in a boot, there was a pale wooden box. She opened the door to go down to the carriage, and there he was, on the passage carpet, looking at the wall.

He had got his clean grey suit on, and his hair flat. He was holding a bow tie in his right hand, by one end.

He hadn’t spoken to her for thirty-seven days. He had passed her the salt. She looked at him, and he went on looking at the wall, with his clean shut little face; and she saw that his chin had started, very slightly, to pucker, and that he was doing something about it, with his teeth.

He had always had that done for him. She’d forgotten. He could cut bread into strips of equal width and he could tell you to the Sickle what a Thestral was worth, and he was twelve, and it was five minutes to eight. It had always been their mother, or Pobble, or it had been her; there had been a time, when she was home, that he used to come and stand on a stool. Nobody in that house had thought about it this morning. He must have tried. There was a crease in the silk where he’d tried. He had been standing out here, she thought, for some time.

Lyra didn’t say anything. It was perhaps the most difficult thing that she had done that year. She bent down, and took it from him, and it was warm. She turned up his collar. She could feel his breath, going in and out, very quick and light, on the inside of her wrist; on the right one; she had moved round to make sure of that. She crossed the ends, and brought the long end up and under. She made the bow. She pulled it true. She turned his collar down, and she gave the whole thing a small tweak, to the left, because it was how he liked it.

He looked at her then. It was only for a moment. His eyes were red round the edges. He didn’t thank her; she would have been alarmed if he had. He turned, and walked away down the passage, with his hands at his sides, and she heard him on the stairs: and at the turn, where he had always done it, he took the last four at a jump. She heard him land. There was a short horrified silence. Then the feet went on, slowly, walking.

She stood in the door for a little. She found that she had to put a hand on the frame.

“You’ll be wanting your gloves,” said her mother.

She had come along from the other end, in the grey, and stopped where the light fell from the landing window. She stood with her hands together, looking at nothing. They had not been alone in the same room since the kitchen. It didn’t make very much difference. She had been told what it was, and she knew how far it carried. She’d looked at that arm once, on the morning of the first of August, when Lyra had got as far as her own bed; and she had taken the sponge from the elf, and done the rest herself. Her eyes rested for an instant on Lyra’s left sleeve.

“I had a message for you,” said Narcissa Malfoy, in her ordinary voice. “I had quite forgotten. It’s of no consequence. It was from a person that I met at the seaside. He asked me to say that the chicken was on his account. And the chocolate.”

She held out the gloves. They were a very good pair, in pale kid. She laid them across Lyra’s hand, and she straightened one of the fingers.

“I thought that you ought to have it in writing,” she said. “But I find I haven’t any paper.”

They rose when she came into the common room.

She hadn’t expected that. She’d thought it would be looks. She came down the stone steps behind a first-year with the password, with the green lamps swinging, and Montague got to his feet. So did Warrington’s sister. It went on across the room, in a small rustling wave, from the fire to the door. There were fourteen people in Slytherin whose fathers had been put under the Wizengamot in June, and every one of them knew what had been done, and who had done it; they’d had it from their mothers. *By four o’clock, as I asked.* A fifth-year who had never spoken to her got up and gave her the chair. It was the good one. She had fought Parkinson for that chair for five years, and he was holding the back of it for her now, with shining eyes.

There was a badge on her pillow, in silver: *Captain*. There was a note about it from Snape, one line. Weasley had got the other. She heard that at supper; she had it from Zabini, who said that it was the first thing to have happened in Gryffindor in years that he would pay to see. She had a good laugh, in the right place.

Crabbe was on the end of the bench in the dormitory, with her boots off. Her father had got nine years, on the sixteenth of August, for his part in an affair in a room full of glass. She’d taken some time over one of the laces. She looked up when Lyra came in, slow and careful and thorough, and it went to the left sleeve, and on, and it did not stop.

“I was sorry to hear about your father,” said Lyra.

Crabbe pulled off the boot. She looked into it. She seemed to be thinking about what there was in there.

“It’s quieter,” said Crabbe.

Goyle sat on the next bed, with her big knees apart. She had got a fat black notebook open on them, that Lyra hadn’t seen before, and there was a Muggle pencil in her hair. She’d been talking. You could tell. She had been saying something slowly and with a good deal of pleasure, with one thick finger on the page, and Crabbe had been saying numbers, in her low reasonable voice. It had stopped as the door opened. Neither of them looked as if anything had stopped. Goyle shut the book, with her finger in it.

“You’ve gone thin,” said Goyle. “I brought you a pie.”

“I’m not hungry.”

“No,” said Goyle. She put it on the bedside table, and went back to looking at the wall, and waited for Lyra to go and have her bath.

She went up to the seventh floor on the second night, at nine o’clock. She had thought that she wouldn’t. She’d gone over all that in the summer. There was no reason at all to go to a room that had been read out at dinner, with a rat in it, and she had been round it, in her head, a hundred times, on her knees, looking for the hole. She had walked past the bare wall three times, with her hands cold, thinking about what she needed. There was the door. It seemed to know her.

The fire was laid. There was the bald rug, and the fender. The chair had gone on sinking to the left, on its heap of books. It was just the same. She stood on the threshold and it went through her like a blade. She’d have given anything for it to have been altered; for there to be a hole, a crack, a smear of droppings in the corner. It was an extremely good room. It had never been anything but kind.

He was by the hearth, on his knees, with the cauldron on a trivet and his sleeves rolled up. The gold had gone dark and thick, and there was a slow thread of something going round on it, like a hair of light. He’d got taller. He sat back on his heels, and looked at her; and his hands went, without his appearing to think about it, to his pockets.

“Hello,” said Harry.

It was the first thing that anybody had said to her in a fortnight, in that tone of voice. He had a sock on. She had seen it, in Diagon Alley; she’d seen it on the terrace, with his boot crossed on his knee. He was wearing it now. She looked at that. Something rose up from the bottom of her with such force that she could feel it behind her teeth.

*It hears. It’s on my arm. It’s got one eye. He did it on your birthday and it took three and a half hours, and I screamed for you. I screamed so loudly, Harry. You were in the field. I could feel you there, it was the only thing that I could feel, you were right on top of me. You came. You came in an hour, and I couldn’t—*

It weighed about as much as a grain of rice. It lay quite quietly along the vein.

“You’ve let that go down,” said Lyra. “It wants another half-turn.”

It was the pleasant voice. She’d got rather good at it. She crossed the rug, by way of the far side, and sat down in the chair, and arranged her skirts. She took out a sheet of the second-best paper. She heard herself say that she had been giving some thought to the coming year. It seemed to her that last year’s arrangement had grown slack, and that a certain freshness had gone out of the performance, at any rate on the Gryffindor side. Now that one of them was a captain and the other was not, she proposed a formal programme of hostilities. She’d drawn up some heads. She read them out. She read them in a clear carrying tone, rather slowly, with her chin up, as one might read to somebody at the far end of a long room; and they were extremely funny. There was a passage about his Apparition. There was a good deal about Weasley’s nose.

She didn’t look at him. She could feel him looking at her.

He didn’t say anything for a very long time. When she had finished the third head she turned over the page, and the fire shifted, and he had got up. He stood on the rug with his hands where they had been told to be, and he had an expression that she hadn’t seen on him. It wasn’t anger. She could have done something with that. He looked like somebody outside a house at night, who has been knocking, and can see the lamp.

“Right,” said Harry. “You don’t want to talk.”

She looked at the paper.

*No*, she thought. *No, you stupid, stupid boy. No.*

“I can’t think what there is to talk about,” said Lyra. “Do you want to be Hippogriff or Thestral?”

He turned back to his cauldron. After a moment she heard the ladle start, going slowly round.

That night she sat up behind her hangings, with a stub of candle and a very small piece of paper. She only wanted to see what it looked like. She wrote it in the plainest hand that she could do, and it took her a long time, because of the way her fingers were behaving. *It hears.*

Two words. It would have gone in a sugar mouse.

She looked at them till the candle guttered. She looked at her arm. She held the paper to the flame, by the corner, and it curled up and went small, and she kept hold of it till it reached her fingers.

It was remarkable how much of the term there was.

She had thought that it would be impossible. It wasn’t even very difficult. You simply stopped saying things. You went through your day as you’d go across a floor with somebody asleep on it, and you learnt where the boards were. She didn’t talk in her sleep; she’d had Crabbe’s word on that, in the second year. She found that she was reading aloud rather more, in the evenings, in a pleasant voice, about nothing. A note came to her plate one morning in October, that no owl had brought, in a good round prize-winning hand. *You have such a nice speaking voice. Do go on with the Herodotus.*

There were even, God help her, some good days.

Professor Lupin had come back to Defence. Nobody knew who’d persuaded him. He stood in front of the sixth year, on the first Wednesday, looking as if he had been left out in the garden since June, in the same brown jacket; and at the end of the lesson he asked Miss Malfoy to stay behind. He sat on the edge of the desk. He looked at her for a little while, with his tired kind intelligent eyes. He’d been given a letter once, by a girl of fourteen, about a goblet. She had never asked him for one thing.

“I find that I’m still in your debt, Miss Malfoy,” he said. “I wanted to say so. It’s been a bad year for those. If there should come a day when you’d like to collect, I hope you won’t stand on ceremony.” He smiled at her a little, with his grey face. “I’d take it as a kindness.”

The Marks were always in the left arm. He’d know that. He had been in a war. She stood with her book held against her chest, in the slanting light, and looked at the only adult who had ever offered her anything without a clause in it; and it was quite extraordinary how much she wanted to sit down on the floor.

“I’ll bear it in mind, Professor,” said Lyra.

There was Apparition. It was on Saturday mornings in the Great Hall, with the tables taken away and a man from the Ministry who appeared to have been drawn with a ruler. They were given each a wooden hoop. On the third Saturday in October, with the whole of the sixth year standing round in their socks, Harry Potter shut his eyes and frowned, and turned on the spot, with enormous concentration, and went with a loud crack to the other side of his hoop. He stood there, looking pleased. A hundred people began to scream with laughter. He looked round. He looked behind him, and then, following the direction of their fingers, down.

It was his left eyebrow. It lay in the centre of the hoop on the bare boards, and it looked extremely surprised.

She had to turn her back. She had to put her hand over her mouth, and hold on to Goyle. It got out anyway. She had not laughed since a Sunday in June. It hurt her under the ribs, it hurt where the house was, and she stood with her shoulders going and the tears running down, wheezing; and when she got her head up, he had found her, right across the Hall, with his hand clapped to the place, and half his forehead bald. He was grinning at her. He couldn’t help it. Nor could she. It was only about as long as it takes to say so. Then the man with the ruler had got there, and someone had shouted for Madam Pomfrey, and she’d straightened up, and put her face back on, and taken ten points from Gryffindor for leaving parts of itself on school property.

“It wants more of something,” said Zabini.

That was on the Thursday, over the cards. It was a walnut cake, with something dark in the middle. He had been brought it on a plate. He had eaten one forkful, and sat with his eyes shut and his long fingers on the stem of the fork; and all round the table the play had stopped. Goyle stood at his shoulder with her hands wrapped in her apron. She was breathing through her mouth. There had been a nine in the spring, for something with apples. He opened his eyes, and looked at the plate. He looked at it for rather a long time.

“Nine,” said Zabini.

“What sort of something?”

“I don’t know, Greer. If I knew that, I would be a great man.” He took another forkful. His face had an odd shut careful look on it, that Lyra hadn’t seen before; she had seen him lose eighty Galleons with less. “Nine. Don’t ask me again.”

Goyle went red, slowly, from the neck. She went round the table with the plate. She put a slice in front of Lyra, and she stood there.

It was very good. Lyra had some of it. She had a good deal of the walnut.

Granger sat down opposite her on the second Tuesday in October.

She hadn’t done that for four and a half months. She came along between the shelves, with her bag on her shoulder and the elastic band round the notebook, and she pulled out the chair and sat. Her left sleeve was pushed up. There was a long shiny pink scar on the arm, from the wrist to the elbow; the skin had puckered a little, and she had taken no steps about it. She looked older. She took out a pen, and laid that parallel with the edge of the table. She didn’t open the book.

They were by the window. It had started to rain. Lyra finished the line that she was on, and she blotted it.

“Five hours and ten minutes,” said Lyra.

She hadn’t known that she was going to say that. It had just been lying there, at the front of her mouth, since June.

“I know,” said Granger. “I’ve written it down.”

Somebody went by behind them with a trolley. Neither of them turned her head. Lyra could hear it go, all the way to the end of the bay, with one wheel ticking.

“It doesn’t pass off. I looked that up when I was eleven. You’d need another person, and I was the other person; I was standing a yard away. It takes four seconds. I’ve timed it.” Her voice was quite dry. She had her eyes on Lyra’s, level and brown and adding. “I had powder in my hand, and I looked at you, and I looked at the fire. I thought, she’ll keep. I thought that Sirius might not. I want you to know that I did that on purpose, and I didn’t do it in a hurry. I’d do the same again. He was dying, and you weren’t, and if I’d had four seconds I’d have spent them on the right person. I’ve been through it a great many times, and it comes out.” She stopped. She looked down at the pen. “He died anyway.”

The rain had got heavier. There was a Ravenclaw at the next table, moving his lips.

Lyra hadn’t the least idea what her face was doing. She thought that it must be all right. It usually was.

“How tidy,” she said. “Was that all?”

“No.”

Granger picked up the pen, and turned it over. It took her some time to do that. A slow dull colour had come up in her thin cheeks, and it went on rising. She looked, of all things, ashamed. Lyra had seen Hermione Granger in a good many states. She had seen her with a hand clapped over her mouth, on a staircase. She’d never seen that.

“I was Petrified for six weeks when I was twelve,” said Granger. “In second year. Your father put that book in a cauldron. I’ve known since last November that it’s rather more complicated than that; I know who stood by. But it was your father, and you’d called me what you called me, and you were ten yards away from me the day that I was brought back, and you asked Crabbe if I’d had a nice rest. I don’t remember it. You don’t, you see. You can’t remember anything. I’ve always wondered.”

She raised her eyes.

“Part of me wanted you to know what it was like,” said Hermione Granger. “There was about a second. I don’t know how big a part. I’ve been trying to work it out. It wasn’t why I went. I’m nearly sure that it wasn’t why I went. But it was there, and I looked at it, and I threw the powder.” She laid down the pen again. Her hand was not perfectly steady. “I’ve got both on the same page. I thought that you should see the page.”

Lyra sat quite still.

She could think of a number of remarks. They came up in good order. She had had four years’ training; she’d had five hours and ten minutes. There had been kittens. There was a person sitting across from her, with a burnt arm, who had just said out loud to her face, in a library, for no reason in the world, the only true thing that anybody had told her since the first of August. She would have traded every mark that she had ever got to be able to say: *I know what it’s like. I know what it is to have both on the same page. I’ve got a page that you wouldn’t believe.* It lay along the vein. It had an eye the size of a grain of rice.

She drew the blotter towards her. She turned over her paper. She had got ninety-nine for September, and for five years she had left her marks lying face up on that table, for one person in particular to find. She squared the sheet with its printed side uppermost, and she covered it with her hand.

“I’m working,” said Lyra.

Granger looked at the hand. It seemed to go in. She looked for a moment longer; and it was the strangest thing: she nodded, as she’d have nodded at a figure that she had been expecting. She gathered up her pen. She got to her feet, and took up the bag.

“Yes,” said Granger. “I thought you’d say that.”

She went away, between the shelves, with her pink arm and her straight thin back.

It was on the seventh floor, a little after midnight, on the last night of the month.

She had come off her rounds. She’d been up there rather longer than she had any reason to be, in the room, in the chair, reading out something about the Persian Wars to a person who was stirring. He’d stopped asking. She came out into the dark by the door in the wall, and it closed behind her and was stone. The trolls were asleep in their tutus. There was a bar of cold moonlight across the floor from the end window.

Something moved, by the skirting.

She stopped. It didn’t run. It had been going along the bottom of the wall, close in, with its hindquarters low, and it stopped too, about four yards from her. It sat up. It was a large grey dirty rat, rather bald about the tail, and it held its front paws up in front of its chest, and its nose went, and went. It looked at her. It had very small, wet, bright, sorry eyes.

So it was the same. She had rather thought that it would be.

She felt nothing much. She had felt it all in a linen cupboard. She stood with her book of rounds in the crook of her arm, and looked at the small soft creature that had sat by her fire. It had gone through a boy’s pockets, and heard everything that she’d ever said. *I love you*, and *Please come*; it had seen him with his glasses off. It had a coat that had been made for somebody else. It wasn’t even afraid of her, she thought; or no more than it was of everything. She supposed that it would be told what she did.

She had been caught out once, at thirteen, for not having counted. She’d said so to Weasley, with some heat.

She walked up to it. She didn’t hurry. She went down on one knee on the cold stone, in the moonlight, with her skirts drawn in, about a foot away; and it shivered all over, and it stayed. She took out her wand, and lit the end of it, and held that near. She looked carefully at the right front paw, and at the left.

She counted its toes.

It took her some time. She was thorough. She went through them twice, and there was one missing, on the left, down at the joint. When she had finished she put her hand in the pocket of her robes. There was a crust there, with a bit of walnut cake on it; someone had put it there, she didn’t know when; she was always finding them. She set it down on the flagstone, in front of the wet quivering nose, exactly square.

“There,” said Lyra. It was a very nice speaking voice. “I do like to know what I’m feeding.”
