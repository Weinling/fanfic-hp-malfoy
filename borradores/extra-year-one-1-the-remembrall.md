# Year One, Part 1: The Remembrall

It was a few minutes past nine.

“So you’re going out with her,” said Ron. “At last. I’m amazed. Truly. Knock me down with a feather.”

He did not look up from his Chocolate Frog cards. Harry, sitting on the end of his own bed with the hangings half drawn, put on his most serious face.

“Sort of. But it’s a secret. As far as anyone else knows, there’s nothing going on at all.”

“A secret.” Ron snorted. “You might as well try telling people Trelawney’s a real Seer. At least Parvati would believe you.”

Harry scratched his chin. “Is it that obvious?”

“Mate. Take the ring off, if you’re trying to hide it. I thought you’d gone public.”

“I don’t want to take the ring off. Isn’t there another way?”

“Then you don’t want to hide it, do you. Put it in the *Prophet*.”

He said it as if it were a joke, but Harry could see him thinking that you never could tell, with people. Who in the whole of Hogwarts would have guessed that Harry Potter was going out with Lyra Malfoy? Never mind the whole of Hogwarts. If anybody had told Ron so a year ago, he would have laughed. Two years ago, he would have looked round for Fred and George.

Because in first year, Harry Potter and Lyra Malfoy had been the worst enemies in the school.

The Sorting Hat had shouted it out without a pause.

“SLYTHERIN!”

Lyra Malfoy had walked to the Slytherin table as though there had never been the slightest doubt about it, as though it had all been arranged before she was born, which perhaps it had. She sat down in the middle of the older students like somebody taking her own chair, and Harry, waiting in the line of first-years with his heart going like a rabbit’s, watched her do it and could not look away.

He had seen her once before. He had stood on the stool next to hers in a robe shop in Diagon Alley, a month ago, and she had pushed his shoulders back and tipped up his chin and told him to smile like he meant it. She had told him not to lose his dignity, ever, anywhere. He had carried that out of the shop like a present.

And then on the train she had come along the corridor to his compartment, having heard who he was, and held out her hand, and said things about Ron in front of Ron that Harry would not have said about Dudley. So he had stood up straight, the way she had taught him, and looked her in the eye, and told her he could work out the wrong sort for himself, thanks.

She had not known him. That was the thing he kept coming back to. She had looked straight at him, the boy from the robe shop, and had not known him from Adam. He had been right, then, when he thought she would forget him before she reached the corner.

He wondered whether he could be in Slytherin too.

She had said only the best people went to Slytherin. Harry did not feel like one of the best people. And there was the other thing, which Hagrid had told him in Diagon Alley and which he could not get out of his head: that You-Know-Who had been in Slytherin. It did not matter, Hagrid said, what House a person had been in. But Voldemort had killed his mother and father, and Harry did not want to be near him in any way at all, even that one.

“Potter, Harry!”

The Hall changed when he walked out. All along the tables people were whispering.

“*Potter*, did she say?”

“*The* Harry Potter?”

Harry hardly heard them. He did not notice the necks craning to see his forehead. He was still thinking when the hat came down over his eyes and there was nothing but the black inside of it.

“Hmm,” said a small voice in his ear. “Difficult. Very difficult. Plenty of courage, I see. Not a bad mind. Hard-working, when you’ve a reason to be. Ambition—what’s this? You want to look good for a girl? Small. But very human. Now where shall I put you?”

Harry gripped the edges of the stool. *Next to her*, he thought. *It would be nice to be next to her.*

“Slytherin? For one girl?” The voice dropped. “My young friend, a House is no small thing. It may change the whole course of your life. First loves go down like the sun, sooner or later; but the House you came from will follow you about for the rest of your days.”

*I don’t care*, Harry thought. *I’ll decide what happens to me. I only want to be good enough to stand next to her.*

“Hmm. Slytherin would help you on the way to greatness, no doubt about that. But that isn’t what you’re after, is it? Well then, my greedy, reckless friend. You had better fight for it—in GRYFFINDOR!”

He heard the last word shouted to the whole Hall.

It seemed he was not good enough to stand next to Malfoy yet. Harry took off the hat, a little sorry, and went to the Gryffindor table, and the Gryffindors went mad.

Somewhere behind him, somebody clicked her tongue.

There was one thing Harry found out in his first few weeks at Hogwarts, and it was that Lyra Malfoy was—to put it kindly—very frank.

“Stupid Gryffindors,” she said, in the corridor outside Charms, to nobody in particular and everybody in earshot. “Always first in, without the faintest idea what they’re doing. Why do people insist on calling stupidity bravery? It only gets more people killed. Oh—unless that’s the point. Tidying the useless ones away first, to clean up the world.”

“Poor slow Hufflepuffs,” she said, to a table of them in the Great Hall. “I can’t even be cross with you. Even the Hat could see you were the leftovers. But you might get out of my sight before I notice you, mightn’t you? Surely even you can manage that much.”

To put it less kindly, she was horrible. She said things that went into people and stayed there, and when anything annoyed her she simply got out her wand.

She had a face like a painted doll and a temper like the other place. She was proud and overbearing and absolutely certain that she was better than everybody else. The worst of it was that she seemed to be right. She and the other children from old wizarding families had grown up knowing things that the rest of them had never heard of, and they did well in lessons because of it; and they knew a few curses that the school would never have taught them, which meant that ordinary first-years did not dare stand up to Malfoy and her friends.

*She’s worse than Dudley*, Harry thought, and was startled by it. He had not imagined there was anybody worse than Dudley in the world. Least of all one of the first people in that world who had ever told him she liked him.

“They’re getting worse,” Hermione Granger said, at breakfast. “Yesterday they went for Neville’s toad.”

“Yeah,” said Harry. “I saw.”

Hermione said it as if it were Harry’s fault, and he found that it felt like it. It was a horrible thing, not being able to stick up for somebody you liked.

“Do you know what she said, when I turned Trevor back to his proper colour? She said she didn’t like the look of him, so what was wrong with changing him? As if she can just change anything she doesn’t like! How can anybody be so *selfish?*”

Had the Hat known? Was that why it had kept him out of Slytherin?

If she didn’t like something, she changed it.

Harry sat very still over his porridge.

Why had he never thought of that?

He still liked Lyra Malfoy. But if things went on like this he thought he might end up disliking her even more than Dudley, and he did not want to dislike her. She was generous to people she approved of, and gave them things. Meeting her had made him stand up straight. It was only when she laughed while she was hurting somebody that she reminded him of Dudley, and however soppy he was about her, he could not make that all right.

So.

*If I don’t like something*, Harry thought, *I can change it.*

The Hat had said *fight for it*. Perhaps this was what it had meant.

It was their first flying lesson.

Neville Longbottom was so nervous that he kicked off before Madam Hooch had blown her whistle, and shot up into the air, and came off his broom twenty feet up, and broke his wrist. Madam Hooch told the rest of them that if a single broom left the ground while she was away, the person on it would be out of Hogwarts before they could say Quidditch, and took Neville off to the hospital wing.

Malfoy burst out laughing.

“Did you see his *face?* He can’t even sit on a broom without blubbing.”

“Shut up, Malfoy,” said Parvati Patil sharply.

Malfoy looked down her nose at her. “Why? Is it untrue? He can’t fly, he can’t do magic, he can’t make a potion. What *can* he do? Hufflepuff’s too good for Longbottom. I wonder whether a Muggle school would have him.”

A few of the Gryffindors bristled. That was all they did. Malfoy laughed at them, and then saw something lying in the grass and strolled over to it.

“It’s that stupid thing Longbottom’s gran sent him. A glass ball that lights up when you’ve forgotten something. Not much use to a boy who forgets his own head, is it?”

Some of the Slytherins sniggered, high and nervous.

Now, thought Harry.

“Give that here, Malfoy,” he said quietly. “It’s Neville’s.”

Everybody stopped talking and looked at him. Malfoy turned round with her eyebrows up, as though a piece of furniture had spoken; and then she smiled a nasty little smile.

“All right. Since I’m so generous, I’ll leave it somewhere for Longbottom to find. Up a tree, say?”

“Don’t be stupid. Pinching people’s things and hiding them up trees, like a magpie? Is that what you call dignity?”

Until then Harry Potter had been a quiet boy who did his homework and kept out of Malfoy’s way, and watched her with a long face when she was vile to somebody. Both Houses stared at him.

“Keep your dignity, Malfoy,” said Harry. “Don’t make me any more disappointed in you.”

She did not know where he had got it from. He could see that she did not.

“Who do you think you are, being disappointed in me?”

She leapt onto her broom and kicked off. She had not been lying when she boasted about flying at home; she went up easily and well, and in a moment she was level with the top branches of an oak.

“Come and get it, then!”

It was the sort of thing Dudley would have done, only meaner, because of the magic.

But Harry had decided he was not going to dislike Lyra Malfoy. And for that, she had to stop.

He could not just let her go on until everybody hated her. Himself included.

Once he had decided, he felt wonderful. He put his chin up, the way somebody had once told him to, and grinned.

“You fly very nicely,” he called up. “Just like a magpie.”

A few people laughed. The laughing stopped when he picked up his broom.

“*No!*” said Hermione Granger. “Madam Hooch told us not to move—you’ll get us all into trouble—”

“Sorry, everyone,” said Harry, grinning at them all. “Tell Hooch it was me!”

There was a cheer behind him as he kicked off, and then he was rising, and the air was rushing through his hair and pulling at his robes, and in a few seconds he was face to face with Malfoy.

Nobody had taught him. He did not need teaching; the broom simply went where he wanted. He was not afraid of falling. The sun was warm and the wind was cool and he felt as if somebody had opened a door.

If only Malfoy would be nice, it would all be perfect.

“Give it here, Malfoy.”

He had come up so fast that she looked startled. Then she shook it off, and narrowed her eyes at him.

“Make me.”

“I don’t want to touch you. You might fall.”

And he took both his hands off his broom, and came towards her slowly, to show her he meant it.

He only did it to reassure her. He had no idea, then, that flying with no hands was a trick only very good Quidditch players could manage. The Gryffindors down below cheered themselves hoarse. The Slytherins went quiet and looked anxiously up at Malfoy.

Even Malfoy shut her mouth.

“Stop messing about,” said Harry. “It isn’t dignified or anything. Come down.”

“Don’t you *lecture* me—!”

She wheeled her broom round. Harry was in front of her before she had finished turning, still with his hands off.

He could see it make her furious. He had been brought up by Muggles and had never touched a broom in his life, or so he claimed, and here he was flying circles round Lyra Malfoy with his hands in his lap.

She drew back her arm to throw the glass ball as far as she could.

“If you’re so clever, *catch it!*”

Harry moved. It turned out that everything he had done so far had been playing; he was beside her in an instant, and had her by the wrist before she could let go. The ball dropped out of her fingers, and he caught it with his other hand, without thinking about it at all.

“Got it,” he said.

Malfoy went scarlet.

She had lost, completely. She was not as fast as he was, and she could not fly with no hands, and the ball she had dropped, he had caught out of the air. She wrenched her arm away from him.

“How *dare* you touch—”

She was so angry that she forgot she could not fly with no hands.

“*Look out!*”

She had pulled away too hard. Harry watched her lose her balance, as slowly as in a dream: she tipped, and turned half over, and came off her broom, and began to fall. He saw her eyes. They had gone wide and blank with plain animal fright.

Somebody below was screaming. Two girls in Slytherin robes, one of them round-faced with her hair in two plaits, were shouting her name.

Harry flung the Remembrall away and grabbed his broom and dived.

The ground was coming up very fast, and he did not care in the least. There was only Malfoy, falling, and his own hand stretched out towards her.

Even Malfoy could not help it, falling. She flailed with her arms and legs, and forgot that she had just been fighting him, and reached out her hand to Harry Potter.

Harry laughed out loud. She was only a kid, the same as him.

“*Grab it!*”

He caught her hand and pulled her in, and hauled on the broom with everything he had, and at the very last moment, a few feet off the grass, he turned it.

But a school broom was not made for two. Something gave with a loud crack. Harry felt it go, and wrapped both arms round Malfoy, tight.

He heard her gasp. There was no time to feel anything else. He hit the ground on his back and slid, and his robes came up round his ears, and the earth and the grit tore at him all down his spine, and he kept his mouth shut and held on and waited for it to stop.

“Let—let *go!*”

Malfoy was struggling against his chest. She must be all right, then. Harry’s arms gave up, and he let go.

She scrambled up, shaking, and rounded on him.

“You—*you!* You let me fall! You grabbed my wrist and—that was *dangerous*, you—”

But even Malfoy had to stop. Harry Potter was lying on the grass, scraped and bleeding in half a dozen places, as though a troll had sat on him.

She looked down at him with a white face, and for once she was not grand at all.

“Potter? Are you—all right?”

“*Harry!*”

Ron and Hermione were running across the grass. They got him under the arms, and he groaned, and got up somehow between them.

“You’re hurt! You’ve got to go to the hospital wing!”

“Hang on,” said Harry. “First.”

He made his shoulders go back. His back was on fire, but he swallowed the noise it wanted to make and walked, one step and then another, to where Malfoy was standing.

Malfoy jumped. She looked one way and the other as if for somewhere to go, and then, when he was right in front of her, she pressed her lips together and looked up at him.

“Haven’t you got something to say?”

“W-what?”

“There’s something you ought to say now. Isn’t there?”

The calmer he got, the more his body remembered what had happened to it. His back was burning and every bone he had was aching.

But he did not let it show in front of Malfoy. Crying because it hurt would not have been dignified.

He smiled instead. The corners of his mouth were twitching, but a smile was a smile.

Malfoy looked him up and down, and her mouth opened slowly.

“Th…”

“Th?”

At that she clenched both her fists and shrieked at him.

“Do you think I’m going to say *thank you?* If you hadn’t stuck your nose in where it wasn’t wanted, I’d never have fallen in the first place!”

It was not at all what anybody had expected.

The others stared at her. They could not believe there was such a person in the world. No thanks, and no sorry; she had put the whole thing on him. The Gryffindors were frowning. Even one of the Slytherins muttered, “That’s a bit much.”

But Harry Potter, who was the one it had happened to, burst out laughing.

“Ha! Hahaha—*ow*—”

She had not said thank you. But he had heard it, quite clearly, in everything else she was doing.

She kept glancing at his cuts while she shouted at him. She had folded her arms, but her fingers would not keep still on her sleeves. Her mouth kept almost saying something and not saying it. She was frightened of the blood. And her hair was all over the place, and her robes were twisted round, and she had not noticed; she only stood there stamping her feet and glaring at him.

“What are you *laughing* at?”

“You.”

He lifted an arm. He must have hurt his side as well, because even that made him wince, and Malfoy flinched with him.

Her mouth was not honest at all. The rest of her was hopeless at lying.

However vile she was, there was no need to be frightened of her, or to hate her. She had changed Harry Potter, once, on a stool in a robe shop. Harry Potter could change her in return.

Harry tried not to laugh again, because it hurt, and pointed at the grass.

“No. You should say sorry to Neville. You’ve broken his Remembrall.”

Malfoy looked round. The glass ball was lying in pieces in the grass.

“*You* threw it!”

“You dropped it first.”

“Is *that* what matters right now?”

“What else? My cuts? Hang on—are you worried about me?”

“Don’t be *ridiculous!* I can’t stand looking at you in that state, that’s all! Go away! Go somewhere!”

“Where shall I go? Back to the tower?”

“Are you *stupid?* If you’re hurt, you go to the hospital wing!”

“I don’t know where the hospital wing is yet.”

“Idiot! *Idiot!* It’s up the—I’ll take you—”

She had actually taken hold of his sleeve when Professor McGonagall came running across the lawn. Malfoy let go at once and stepped back, with her mouth pressed shut, looking rather frightened.

Professor McGonagall took Harry to the hospital wing herself. She had seen the dive from a window, it turned out. On the way there she did not say a word about the rule he had broken. She said, rather breathlessly, that she must speak to Oliver Wood.

The next morning, three Remembralls arrived at the Gryffindor table for Neville. They were much better than the old one, and must have cost a great deal more. There was no name on the parcel. Neville was overjoyed; his gran would never have to know.

Harry looked across the Hall at Malfoy and grinned. Malfoy turned her head away so sharply that her hair flew out.

By the end of the week the whole school knew that Harry Potter had been made the youngest House Seeker in a century, for catching a Remembrall in a fifty-foot dive. That was the story that went round, and it was a good story, and everybody told it.

Nobody told the other part. The Slytherins did not want to. Harry asked Ron and Hermione not to, and Ron, who could not see why, told Seamus not to either, and somehow that held. And Malfoy, Harry noticed, never once mentioned it, and stared anybody who did into silence, until by the summer it might as well never have happened.

He did go on flying with no hands, though. He found out quite soon that it annoyed her.

After that, he did it on purpose.
