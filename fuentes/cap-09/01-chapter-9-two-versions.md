# Chapter 9: Two Versions

Oct 4, 2026 · @Edén

“Gryffindor,” said Marcus Flint, “on Saturday. And we are going to beat them.”

He said it to the whole team, standing in a ring on the frozen grass of the pitch with their brooms over their shoulders and their breath smoking, but he was looking at Lyra, which she considered only correct.

She was not in a good temper. She had not been in a good temper since a quarter to four, when she had stood in front of the dormitory mirror and scraped her hair back off her face and tied it at the nape of her neck with a length of plain black ribbon, as tight as it would go, and then wound the tail into a knot and pinned it, because a tail could whip about in a wind and a knot could not. It was the only sensible way to wear it on a broom. It pulled at her scalp, it made her look like a governess, and it would stay that way until the end of practice, when she meant to take it down and wash it and put on enough Sleekeazy’s to make up for the insult. There were days when Lyra Malfoy looked in the mirror and was satisfied, and on those days she was charming to everyone. This was not one of those days. Crabbe and Goyle had seen her come down the dormitory stairs and found urgent business in the library.

Still, a sharp temper was no bad thing in a Seeker. She had noticed that she saw better when she was slightly annoyed.

“Their brooms are rubbish,” Flint went on. “Cleansweeps, most of them, and the Fives are older than I am. The only decent broom they’ve got is Potter’s, and that’s a Two Thousand. We’re on the Two Thousand and Ones, every one of us.” He did not look at Lyra when he said that, which was tactful of him, and also unnecessary. People said her father had bought her a place on the team, and every time they said it Lyra laughed, because it was such a stupid thing to say. Her father had bought the team seven brooms. Nobody could buy you the eyes to see a Snitch from the far end of a pitch in a crosswind, or the nerve to dive at it, and anybody who thought otherwise was welcome to borrow her broom and try. Money was simply one of the things she was good at, like balance and nerve and quick hands. It would have been absurd not to use it.

“Wood’s desperate,” said Flint. “Seventh year. Last chance to win the Cup before he leaves, and he hasn’t won it once. He’ll want to keep Potter in one piece for the rest of the season, so he won’t risk him early. Which means—” he turned to Lyra properly, “—you are going to catch that Snitch before Potter does. That’s all I need from you. Leave the rest to us.”

“Naturally,” said Lyra. “It’s what I’m for.”

There was a short pause, in which several members of the Slytherin team found something interesting to look at on the handles of their brooms.

“I mean, you might,” said Bole, the larger of the two Beaters, to his boots.

“He is good, though,” said Bletchley. “Potter. You’ve got to give him that. He’s never missed one. Not once.”

“Someone ought to do something about him before the match,” said Warrington, “that’s what I think.”

“Montague tried that last year,” said Derrick. “They found him upside down in a barrel of Flobberworms behind the greenhouses. He still won’t say how.”

“You don’t go poking a Doxy nest,” said Bole, who had once poked a Doxy nest.

“And Lyra,” said Montague, as though he had been waiting for an opening and this was it, “no offence, but you are a bit off your game against him. Everyone says so.”

The pitch went quiet.

“A bit what?” said Lyra.

Montague looked at the others for help and found that none of them were available. “Just— he gets under your skin. Last year—”

“Last year,” said Lyra, “Potter caught the Snitch because he threw himself off his broom for it, and broke his arm doing it, which is not a strategy, it is a medical condition. If you are all quite certain I’m going to lose, you can say so now, and Marcus can find a Seeker you have more faith in. I’m sure there’s a second-year somewhere who can stay on a broom.” She tightened her grip on the Nimbus. “I am going to beat him. I am going to beat him if I have to break my own arm to do it.”

“Please don’t break your arm,” said Flint, with feeling. “I couldn’t look your father in the face.”

She kicked off before he had finished the sentence.

The cold hit her like a slap and then went past her, and the ground fell away, and for a moment she was alone in the air above the pitch with the stands empty all round her and the grey November sky very close overhead. This was the part she would never have said aloud to anyone, because there was no way to say it that did not sound soft: that she was happiest here, a hundred feet up, with nobody near enough to talk to her. She took the Nimbus up in a long spiral, feeling it answer her before she had properly asked, and then levelled out and began to quarter the pitch the way she always did, end to end and side to side, looking for gold.

The knot of hair at the back of her neck sat there like a weight, pulling every time she turned her head. She hated it. She had hated it every time she had worn it, which was every practice and every match for a year now. She had never once considered taking it down.

Below her, Flint blew his whistle and released the Snitch, and the rest of the team went up into the air after the Quaffle with a great deal of shouting, and Lyra, circling high above them with her eyes moving and her hair tied back off her face, found herself thinking about last November, which she had not allowed herself to do properly in a year.

It had been a cold day then, too, but bright, with a hard blue sky and a wind coming in sideways off the mountains.

It was her first match. She had been on the team for six weeks, and for six weeks the whole school had been telling itself that she was there because of the brooms, and she had gone up into the air that morning meaning to make every one of them eat it. Her hair had been loose. She had always worn it loose. It was the colour her mother’s had been at her age and it went most of the way down her back, and she had seen no reason whatsoever why a Malfoy should fly a match looking like somebody’s governess. It streamed out behind her when she flew, pale as a banner, and she had known perfectly well that the whole stand could see it.

She had been better than him that day. She could admit that now, because it was true and because it no longer did her any good. The Nimbus Two Thousand and One was a fraction quicker off the mark than his Two Thousand, and a fraction steadier in a crosswind, and it was not much, but between two brooms of the same make a fraction was everything. When the Snitch showed itself at last, low over the Gryffindor end, she had seen it first. She was sure of that. She had been ahead of him all the way down the pitch, close enough to hear his broom over the wind, and she had been shouting at him, because she was twelve and winning and wanted him to know it.

And then the Snitch had gone up, sharp and sudden, past her left ear, and her own hair had come round across her face in the wind like a curtain, and the gold was gone.

She had not been able to see anything. One moment there was the Snitch and the pitch and the sky, and the next there was only pale hair, whipping across her eyes and into her mouth, and she had flung up a hand to clear it and lost a hand from the broom doing it. The Nimbus had gone off line. She had felt it go and not known which way, and by the time she got the hair out of her eyes the Slytherin stand was coming at her, timber and bunting and screaming faces, very close and very fast, and she had understood quite calmly that she was going to hit it and that there was nothing at all she could do.

Something had taken hold of the back of her robes and pulled.

It was a hard, ugly, wrenching pull, and it hauled her round so sharply that her teeth clacked together, and the stand went past a yard from her shoulder instead of through it. She had looked round. Potter was right beside her, much too close, leaning half off his broom with one hand knotted in her robes and the other closed tight round something that fluttered. He had already caught it. He had caught it a second before, and then reached out with the only hand he had left, and a boy could not hold on to a person and a Snitch and a broom with two hands, and he had not tried to. He had let go of the broom.

She had watched him fall. It was not very far, fifteen feet perhaps, but he had come down on his arm, with the whole of his weight on it, and even over the noise of the crowd she had heard it break.

The school had a version of that match. Lyra had heard it a hundred times since, and had told it herself whenever it was told near her, and it went that Potter had caught the Snitch with a broken arm, which was heroic of him, or careless, depending on which House was telling it. It had been the other way round. He had broken his arm after he caught the Snitch, and he had broken it on her. Nobody up in the stands had seen the hand in her robes; it had all been over too quickly, and from below it had only looked as though the two Seekers had collided. Crabbe and Goyle thought they had collided. Lyra had let them think so. She had let everybody think so, and somewhere in the course of a year of letting them she had very nearly come to believe it herself.

Afterwards Lockhart had come striding onto the pitch to mend the arm, and had removed every bone in it instead, and the school had talked about nothing else for a week. That had suited her very well.

She had never thanked him. She had walked straight past him in the corridor the next morning, with his arm in a sling and his face grey from a night of Skele-Gro, and had said something to Crabbe about how some people would do anything for attention, in a voice that carried. And he had looked at her, just once, and had not said anything at all, which was not like him, and she had walked on. That was the one part she had worked hardest to forget. It had taken most of the spring.

She had tied her hair back for every practice and every match since. She told people it was to keep it out of the wind.

A glint of gold, low down, near the far hoops.

Lyra dropped the Nimbus into a dive so steep that Bletchley, guarding the hoops, yelped and ducked, and she pulled out of it six feet above the frozen grass with the Snitch already beating its wings against her palm. She held it up. Below her, Flint blew his whistle and punched the air, and somebody in the Slytherin team cheered.

Four minutes, by Flint’s watch. It was the fastest she had ever done it. She did not feel nearly as pleased about it as she ought to have done.

The eagle owl was waiting for her on the wall outside the changing rooms, looking as though it had been there some time and resented every minute.

Twenty-two days. Lyra took the letter from its leg, stroked its head once, which it accepted with contempt, and put the letter in her robes without opening it. Then she went down to the dungeons and washed her hair. She took the ribbon out and brushed the knots from it, and washed it twice, and combed through a capful of Sleekeazy’s and sat with it for the full ten minutes the bottle recommended, because she had waited twenty-two days and could wait another half-hour, and because she did not intend to read anything her father had written with her hair looking like that. It was only when she was sitting on her bed in a clean dressing gown, with the curtains drawn round it and her hair dry and gleaming down her back, that she broke the seal.

> *My dear Lyra,*
>
> *I have just read in the* Prophet *that Sirius Black has been inside the castle. One hardly knows whether to laugh or to write to the Minister. Dementors at the gates and a mass murderer in the corridors; I confess I can no longer tell whether Dumbledore is running a school or a prison, and a poor example of either. You may be sure I shall be making my views known to the Board. In the meantime you are not to be anxious. Slytherin is well placed and well guarded, and Professor Snape is more than equal to his duties.*
>
> *As to the matter you raised in your letter, I am afraid I know very little.    Forgive me for being of so little help.*
>
> *Take care of yourself. Your mother sends her love.*
>
> *Your loving father*

Lyra read it through once and set it down on the counterpane.

She had waited three weeks for this. Her father, who answered everything she had ever written him inside two days and generally at three times the length, had taken three weeks to tell her in a single line that he did not know. And her mother, who had written *You may tell me anything* in a hand so small it might have been written standing, sent her love by way of her father, at the bottom of somebody else’s letter, as though nothing had passed between them at all.

Then she looked at the gap.

It was in the middle of the second paragraph, between *very little* and *Forgive me*: a blank in the line, the width of two fingers, where nothing had been written. No one who had ever seen her father’s handwriting would believe he had left it by accident. He did not leave gaps. He had first done it when she was eight, for a birthday treasure hunt, with a clue folded inside a letter about something else, and she had been so delighted that he had kept on doing it long after the treasure hunts stopped, for things that mattered more than treasure. She had not seen one in more than a year.

She took out her wand and touched its tip to the blank.

The parchment gave a soft crack, like a seal breaking, and split along its own thickness the way a pastry leaf splits, into two thin layers that lifted apart at the edge. Between them, in her father’s hand but very much smaller, was a second letter.

> *You asked whether Sirius Black is a danger to us. You asked honestly, so I shall answer honestly: we do not know.*
>
> *Black fought against the Dark Lord in the early days. He was the Potters’ closest friend. When it came out that he had betrayed them, we were as astonished as anyone. For a time some of us believed he had laid a trap. The Dark Lord fell, after all, within hours of going to the Potters’ house, and it would hardly be the first time a man had pretended to change sides in order to do harm. But one cannot be sure even of that. The Dark Lord had a great many people in a great many places, and not all of them were known to the rest of us.*
>
> *His brother Regulus, the true heir of the Black family, was among the most devoted of the Dark Lord’s followers. The most devoted of us all, some would have said. It is said the brothers were not on bad terms. It is possible, then, that Sirius was the Dark Lord’s man all along.*
>
> *I tell you all this so that you will understand that I know nothing, and neither, I suspect, does anyone else. Since nothing can be certain, you will not approach him under any circumstances. Watch, if you must, and keep your distance. Whichever side he is on, he can have no business with you. It seems plain enough that it is the Potter boy he wants.*
>
> *Severus may know more than I do. But I should very much prefer that you put the whole matter out of your mind. There is nothing in it that will do you any good.*

She read it twice. Then she pointed her wand at it and said, “*Incendio*,” and the whole letter went up at once in a single soft rush of flame, the outer layer and the inner, and was gone before it had properly caught, leaving nothing on the counterpane but a scorch the size of a coin. It had been made to burn. Her father thought of everything.

Almost everything.

She sat without moving, with the curtains drawn round her and the sounds of the dormitory going on outside them, Millicent complaining about her Transfiguration essay, someone running a bath. Then she reached down beside the bed, took her Arithmancy book out of her bag, and found the strip of parchment folded behind the tables of values.

*P.S. Who is asking? You may tell me anything. I would rather hear it from you.*

She did not need to unfold the rest; she had burnt it herself, and remembered every word. *He changed his mind about something, and it killed him.*

The most devoted of us all. He changed his mind. She had written to her mother about Regulus and to her father about Sirius, on the same evening, and between them they had given her two different people. One of them was wrong. Or one of them was lying. Or, and this was the possibility she liked least, they were both telling her the truth as far as they knew it, and her father had never been told what her mother knew. She thought of the hired owl with the torn ear. She thought of her mother leaving the room whenever Black’s face was in the paper, and of her father reading the financial pages at breakfast and not noticing.

Except that nobody had asked her father about Regulus. He had brought him up of his own accord. And a school owl, however dull and anonymous, still landed at the same house, with the same post, under the windows of a man who had made a habit all her life of knowing what came into it. Perhaps he had seen her letter to her mother. Perhaps her mother had shown it to him, or he had simply been in the room when it came. If he had, then everything lay down neatly at once: the three weeks; Draco’s *Father has been cross all week*; her mother’s love tacked onto the foot of his letter like a second signature on a treaty; and Regulus, offered up without being asked for, the most devoted of them all, to a daughter who had been going round her father to find out whether that was true. Lyra tried the idea from every side and could neither make it certain nor make it go away.

*Us*, he had written. *Of us all.* He had never in her life written that word to her in a letter before, not even inside one. If he knew what she had been asking, it was not a slip. It was her father telling her, in the one place he could, exactly what the family had been and which side of it she was standing on, and letting her know that he had noticed her looking.

She found she had read the line about Potter twice as well. *It seems plain enough that it is the Potter boy he wants.* Her father had added it at the end of a paragraph, as an afterthought. She did not let herself stop on it, and turned instead to what she could use.

Potter wanted Black. She was sure of that now. *Just missed him*, in the Great Hall, with that face. Something about the Shrieking Shack. Whatever he had gone into Hogsmeade for, it had not been sweets, and whatever he meant to do when he found Black, kill him or ask him something or worse, he did not have the first idea what Black actually was. Nobody did. Her father had just said so. And Potter had stood in the Owlery in the freezing dark three weeks ago and asked her, of all people, about Black’s brother, and she still did not know why, but she knew one thing now that he did not, and it was that the Blacks could not agree among themselves on what Regulus had been.

And there was someone who might know the rest. *Severus may know more than I do.* Professor Snape had been a friend of her father’s in the old days, before Dumbledore took him on and everybody agreed not to remember it. If anyone in the castle knew whose man Sirius Black had really been, it would be Snape. And the Malfoys had paid for a good many things in Snape’s life, his post among them, and he had never once in two years denied her anything she asked politely. It occurred to her, too, sitting very still behind her curtains, that Snape knew this castle better than anyone in it, and could open any door in it, and sat at the staff table. She had watched him look at the Defence teacher’s empty chair on Hallowe’en night. She had no idea yet what that meant. It was one more reason to go and ask.

For two years, every time she and Potter had come face to face, he had known something she did not. The diary. The number seven. The house up north. He dealt it out to her in pieces, when it suited him, and she took what she was given and was expected to be grateful. Now, for once, she would be the one holding the cards. She would find out what Black was, properly, from somebody who knew; and Potter would want it, badly; and he would have to come and ask her for it, with his hands in his pockets and that look on his face of someone working out what it was going to cost him.

She lay back on the pillows and considered what it was going to cost him. There was the question he already owed her, which she had not spent, and did not mean to spend on anything small. There would need to be something else on top of that. Something he would mind.

She went through the possibilities one by one, taking her time over each, and was asleep somewhere between the third and the fourth, with her hair loose on the pillow and the ribbon on the bedside table where she had left it.
