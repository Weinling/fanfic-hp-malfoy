# Chapter 25: Old Friends

The ring was purple all night.

Lyra knew, because she did not sleep. She lay behind her bed-hangings at the Manor with the ring on and her hand held up in front of her face, and watched it: purple, *on guard*, a hard steady purple that never once went to red and never once went to anything better. Somewhere, a long way off in a field full of tents, Potter was on guard against something, and she was in a four-poster bed in Wiltshire with a pair of stolen omnioculars under her pillow and no way on earth of finding out what.

At a little after five, with the sky going grey at the window, it turned green.

*Safe.*

She lay there looking at it for some time after that. Then she took it off and put it in the drawer, and went to the window seat, and was sitting there when her father came up the drive.

He came on foot, alone, from the direction of the gates, in the travelling cloak he had not worn to the match. He walked as he always walked, unhurried, with his cane, as though the grounds belonged to him, which they did. The hem of the cloak was dark with mud to the knee. He did not look up at her window.

Once, when she was eight, both her parents had gone away in the night on some business nobody explained, and come back just before dawn, her mother still in her travelling cloak and her father with his wand hand bandaged, and nobody had mentioned either. Lyra had been given a box of sugared violets and had let it carry off the whole bad day. She was not eight now. There was no bandage this morning, only the mud. She found she was looking for one all the same.

⁂

The *Daily Prophet* was beside her father’s plate at breakfast, folded, the way it always was, and he was reading the financial pages with perfect composure. It was her mother who had unfolded the front page. She had laid it beside the toast rack without a word, the way one might lay down a card to see who would pick it up.

There was a photograph across the top half of it, and the photograph was moving.

A night sky, enormous and black over a dark wood. And in the middle of it, hanging there, a vast green skull, made of something like smoke or stars, with a serpent coming out of its mouth in place of a tongue. The serpent moved. It slid out between the teeth, and coiled, and slid back, over and over, and the skull looked down at Lyra over the marmalade with its empty eyes.

Under it the headline said: SCENES OF TERROR AT THE QUIDDITCH WORLD CUP.

She read the article twice. A crowd of masked and hooded wizards, after the match, marching through the campsite with their wands up, setting tents on fire. The Muggle family who managed the site, hung upside down in the air above them and spun like tops for the amusement of the crowd. Panic, the Ministry overwhelmed, people running for the woods in their nightclothes. And then, from somewhere in the trees, somebody had sent that up into the sky, and the masked wizards had seen it, and scattered as if something had bitten them.

*The Dark Mark*, said the *Prophet*, and in smaller letters, *unseen for thirteen years*.

“A joke,” said Lyra aloud. Her own voice surprised her. “It must be somebody’s idea of a joke.”

Nobody answered. Her father turned a page of the financial news. Her mother poured herself more tea. Draco, who had come down with the cheap omnioculars round his neck and was looking through them at a boiled egg, said, “What’s a Dark Mark?”

“Eat your egg, darling,” said her mother.

Lyra looked at the green skull a little longer. Saying it was a joke had not made her feel any better. She had not really expected it to.

It took her four days to get it out of him, and in the end he wanted to tell somebody.

She went about it the way he had taught her to go about anything: never the question you want answered, always the one next to it. On the first day she asked whether the Ministry would be making arrests, and he laughed and said the Ministry could not arrest its own shadow. On the second she said, idly, over the chessboard, that she supposed some people must have been very angry with the Ministry to do a thing like that, and he moved a bishop and said that a great many people had been angry with the Ministry for a very long time. On the fourth, in his study, with the door shut and the hum of the privacy charm in the walls, he put down his glass and told her.

“It was a demonstration, Lyra,” he said. “Nothing more. A protest.”

“Against what?”

“Against being made to creep about.” He said it with real feeling, as though it had been sitting in him for years. “We are wizards. That was *our* festival, our sport, the greatest match in a generation, and the Ministry had us sneaking into it in our thousands like rats, in case some Muggle farmer happened to look out of his window and see a broom. Why should we hide from *them*? If the Muggles round that site were such a difficulty, they could have been moved for the week. A great many of us have been tired of Ministry nonsense for a long time. It was going to come out somewhere.”

Lyra had grown up with that argument at the dinner table, and it had always seemed to her perfectly sensible. Wizards were what they were, and Muggles were what they were, and it was absurd that the better should have to tiptoe round the lesser. She found that she still thought so.

“But the Muggles,” she said. “Hanging them upside down over the crowd. Was that really necessary?”

“Sometimes a gesture makes the point better than any amount of talk.” He waved a hand. “And where was the harm? Nobody was *killed*, Lyra. The Ministry will have Obliviated the lot of them by now. They won’t remember a thing.”

Lyra thought of a man and a woman and two small children turning over and over in the air, in their nightclothes, while a crowd underneath them laughed. She did not care about Muggles. She had never pretended to. But she knew what it looked like to tie up something that could not fight back and parade it about for the fun of the thing, and it looked, more than anything, common.

She did not say so. She said, “And the Mark?”

Her father’s face went very still.

“That,” he said, “was not intended.”

“Only the Dark Lord’s followers can cast it. That’s what everybody says.” She kept her voice quite light. “Wasn’t it one of you?”

“*Lyra*.” He looked round, sharply, at his own study door, in his own house, with the privacy charm humming in the walls; and Lyra, who had never once in her life seen her father look round to see who was listening, sat very still in her chair.

“Yes,” he said, lower. “Most of us were, once. Before he fell. Thirteen years ago.” He picked up his glass and did not drink from it. “But none of us cast that Mark. None of us *wanted* it. We had no intention whatever of setting the Ministry hunting for his old friends over a little high spirits. When it went up, we were as—” he paused over the word, “—surprised as anybody.”

“Then who did?”

“I have no idea. I should very much like to know. It must have been somebody who got carried away, I suppose, but—” He shook his head. “The Mark is not a firework, Lyra. One does not send it up because one is excited.”

Lyra looked at him across the desk.

There it was again. She had asked him once, in July, more than a year ago, what the thing in his drawer had been, and he had told her it was an old book. She had asked him at Christmas what he would say if the Dark Lord came back for it, and he had laughed and poured a second glass. And now somebody had stood in a wood and put the Dark Lord’s sign in the sky over a hundred thousand witches and wizards, and her father had been there in a mask, and he did not know who. He did not know anything. She began to wonder whether the Dark Lord had ever told him anything either, and whether perhaps that had not been entirely an oversight.

Her father saw something in her face that he did not like, and cleared his throat.

“In any case. Did you know, my treasure, that there is going to be a Triwizard Tournament at Hogwarts this year?”

It was a very good change of subject, and it worked, and he kept his dignity.

⁂

She went up to her room afterwards and sat on the window seat for a long time.

There had been a Mark in the sky. Thirteen years nobody had seen it, and now somebody had, over a field full of tents, and the men in masks who had been her father’s friends had looked up at it and run. Lyra had sat in this very room at Christmas and worked out, as carefully as she had ever worked out anything, what she would do if that day ever came. She remembered every word. *The first to go back to him. On her knees before any of the others. The most loyal servant he had ever had. Whatever it took. Even if it meant turning on Potter.*

In the drawer of her bedside table there was a ring that had been green since five o’clock in the morning.

She did not take it out. She sat with her hands in her lap and looked down the drive, at the place where her father had come walking up through the grey with mud on his cloak, and thought about a boy under a cloak in the dark, saying *Go back to your mum. Stay with her tonight*, on a night when he could not possibly have known that there would be anything to stay for.

He had known about the Tournament as well. *Especially next year, if students come from other schools.* He had said it under a cloister in May, three months before the Minister for Magic winked at her about it, and long before her father had heard a word.

Her father, who had been there, did not know who had cast the Mark. Potter, who could not have been anywhere near it, had known to send her inside.

Lyra sat on the window seat until the light went, and did not open the drawer.

Her mother came to her room the night before term, which she did not usually do, and sat on the end of the bed.

“Your father tells me he’s told you about the Tournament.”

“Yes,” said Lyra. “Seventeen and over. I’m furious. If I were three years older I should be Hogwarts champion, and everybody knows it.”

“That is exactly what I came to talk to you about.” Her mother’s voice did not rise. It never did. “They say it was stopped two hundred years ago because people kept dying in it. Whatever precautions they have taken this time, I do not want you anywhere near it. You will not try to enter, Lyra. Not by any trick, not by any means. Promise me.”

It was quite safe to promise. Nobody was going to let a fourth-year without a single O.W.L. near the thing, whatever she did; she had only been complaining, and both of them knew it.

“I promise,” said Lyra.

Her mother looked at her for a long time. Then she leaned over and kissed her forehead, in the place where somebody else had, a week ago, under a cloak, and Lyra let her.

“Write on Sundays,” said her mother, and went out.

The chair at the end of the staff table was empty.

Lyra saw it the moment she came into the Great Hall, before the Sorting, before she had found her seat, before she had looked at the Gryffindor table, which she did not do. It was the chair where a man in patched robes had sat for a year, eating very little at the full moon and rather more the rest of the time. Nobody was in it. Nobody’s plate was laid.

“Lupin’s gone,” said Parkinson, sitting down across from her with the air of a boy who had been saving it since the train. “Resigned. Over the summer.”

“How do you know?” said Goyle.

“My mum heard. Somebody on the Board of Governors had a letter.” He leaned in and dropped his voice, which made it carry further. “He was a *werewolf*. That’s why he was always ill. Every full moon. Somebody found out at the end of term, and they say he went for some students in the grounds. They kept it quiet, but the parents found out, and that was that.”

“A werewolf,” said Goyle, with a shudder of pure delight. “Teaching *us*. Imagine. A man who turns into a monster once a month, and Dumbledore lets him loose in the staff room.”

“I liked him,” said Crabbe. “He taught well.”

“You would.”

Lyra said nothing at all. She cut her roast potatoes into very small pieces.

*They say he went for some students in the grounds.* He had gone for three students and a convicted murderer and a girl on a hippogriff, on a February night with a full moon over the lake, because somebody had put a neutraliser in his potion; and somebody else had carried that potion up four flights of stairs, and seen a line of little prints across a blotter, and wiped the bottom of the goblet, and thought, *whoever drinks it need never know*.

She looked along the staff table without meaning to. Professor Snape was looking at her. He went on looking for exactly as long as it took her to understand that he was, and then he turned and said something to Professor Sinistra.

*Somebody found out at the end of term.* Lyra had a very good idea who had found out, and how long he had been waiting to.

She had kept that secret for nine months. She had kept it from her father and from Snape and from every soul in Slytherin, because she owed it. And at the end of the year he had come back to his classroom grey and tired and said *thank you* to the air, and she had left a bar of chocolate she did not like outside his door and called it a receipt, and now he was gone, and there was nothing left to pay it to.

“Lyra?” said Goyle. “You’re not eating.”

“I’m not hungry,” said Lyra, and ate a potato to prove it.

⁂

Then the doors banged open, and a man came in out of the storm.

He was grizzled and scarred and stumping, with one wooden leg that rang on the flagstones, and a face that looked as though it had been carved out of old wood by somebody who had only heard faces described. A chunk was missing from his nose. And one of his eyes was not an eye at all. It was a great round electric-blue thing, like a marble, and it rolled in its socket, round and up and back, without blinking, and as he limped the length of the Great Hall it swivelled right round into the back of his head and looked, Lyra could have sworn, straight through the wall.

“May I introduce,” said Dumbledore into the silence, “our new Defence Against the Dark Arts teacher. Professor Moody.”

Nobody clapped but Dumbledore and Hagrid.

“Mad-Eye Moody,” breathed somebody down the table. “The Auror. My dad says he filled half of Azkaban.”

Lyra did not need to be told that. Her father had a name he used for Alastor Moody, and it was not a name he used in front of guests.

She looked at the Gryffindor table then. She had not meant to.

Potter was watching Moody limp up to the staff table with his elbows on the board and his chin on his fists, the way you might watch an old acquaintance come into a room. He did not look surprised. He did not look anything at all.

Then Dumbledore stood up again, and told them that there would be no Inter-House Quidditch Cup this year—Montague actually moaned out loud, and somewhere in the dungeons under her feet a Firebolt went on lying in its case for nothing—because Hogwarts, this year, was to host a Triwizard Tournament.

The Hall erupted. Lyra hardly heard it. Two other schools, Beauxbatons and Durmstrang, would arrive in October. A champion from each. Only students of seventeen or over could put their names forward. Dumbledore had to raise his voice over the uproar from the sixth years, and the sound of Fred and George Weasley informing the whole Gryffindor table that they would be seventeen by October if it killed them.

*Especially next year. If students come from other schools.*

She looked across the Hall at Potter again. He was not looking at Dumbledore. He was looking at her.

It lasted perhaps a second. Then he looked away, back to the staff table, with a perfectly ordinary face; and Lyra sat at the Slytherin table with her hands in her lap, and understood that he had known she would be looking, and that he had wanted her to know that he knew.

Professor Moody did not take a register. He stumped into the Defence classroom on the first Thursday of term, put a jar on his desk, and stood looking at the fourth-year Slytherins with one eye while the other went slowly along the rows, and then round, and then into the back of his own head, and came back with what it had found.

“I know your families,” he said. His voice was a low growl, like something being dragged over gravel. “Some of your mothers and fathers sat in front of the Wizengamot because of me. If I’d had a bit more evidence, a few of you wouldn’t be sitting here at all, because you wouldn’t have been born.” The normal eye went round them, slowly. “But the sins of the parents aren’t the sins of the children. Not yet, anyway.”

Nobody said a word. Next to Lyra, Goyle had gone a mottled pink.

“Right. Curses. The Ministry says I’m to teach you the countercurses and leave it there. I say you can’t fight what you’ve never seen. Which of you can tell me which curses are most heavily punished by wizarding law?”

Lyra did not put her hand up. She never did.

“The Imperius Curse,” she said.

The blue eye swivelled round and fixed on her. Then the other one did.

“Ah,” said Moody. “Malfoy. Lucius Malfoy’s girl. You’d know, wouldn’t you?” He leaned on his desk. “Your father did a very beautiful Imperius, Miss Malfoy. One of the best I ever came across. Only one of his crowd that ever walked out of court, and we never did find the people he’d been working like puppets. It’s thanks to men like your father the Ministry had to start testing for it.”

Somebody at the back drew in a sharp breath. Lyra kept her face perfectly still, which was the one thing in the world she was entirely sure she could do.

“I don’t see what that has to do with the lesson, Professor.”

Moody looked at her for a moment longer. Then he gave a short bark that might have been a laugh.

“No. Nor it has. I got carried away. Old habit.”

He took a spider out of the jar, a large black one, and set it on the desk, and pointed his wand at it. “*Imperio*.”

The spider began to tap-dance.

It went cartwheeling across the desk on its eight thin legs, and did the splits, and swung round in circles on a thread, and the class began to laugh: Parkinson first, then the Warrington cousins, then nearly everybody. Moody let them. Then he stopped smiling.

“Funny, is it? You’d like it if I did it to you?” The laughter died. “Total control. I could make it jump out of the window. Drown itself. Throw itself down one of your throats. I could make *you* do it. I could make you do things you’d hang for, and you’d never know it was me.” The blue eye rested on Lyra for one moment, and went on. “Years back, there were a lot of witches and wizards claiming they’d only done the Dark Lord’s business because they’d been Imperiused. Some of them were telling the truth. Sorting out which was the Ministry’s job. Some of them we never did sort out.”

He did not need to look at her again. Lyra could feel every Slytherin in the room not looking at her either.

“Another. Anybody?”

“The—the Cruciatus Curse,” said Goyle, very quietly.

Moody looked at her, longer than he needed to, with an expression Lyra could not read, as though he had been about to say something to her and had decided against it. Then he took the second spider out of the jar and enlarged it until it was the size of a dinner plate, and said “*Crucio*.”

It did not make a sound. Spiders could not. It only drew its legs up into itself and shook, and twitched, and went on shaking, and Lyra watched the bench in front of her instead and heard Crabbe breathing through her mouth beside her, and it seemed to go on for a very long time. When Moody lifted his wand, the spider lay on its back with its legs curled, quivering.

“And the last,” said Moody.

Parkinson put up his hand. He had obviously been waiting to impress somebody all lesson. “*Avada Kedavra*,” he said, and his voice gave out halfway through.

Moody nodded, and took out the third spider.

There was a flash of green light, and a rushing sound, and the spider rolled over and was dead. There was not a mark on it.

“Not nice,” said Moody. “No countercurse. No blocking it. There’s only ever been one person known to have survived it, and he’s sitting in a classroom upstairs right now.”

Lyra did not look at the ceiling. She thought of a boy with untidy hair and a thin white scar, and an empty classroom in May, and a careless voice saying the Dark Lord’s name as if it were just a word.

“Don’t you ever let anybody tell you those three are power,” said Moody. “They’re not. They’re something dirty, spat out of the end of a wand. If you ever see anybody using one, you come and tell me.”

On her way out, with the class streaming past her, the blue eye swivelled round once more and went down, quite deliberately, to the pocket of her robes. It stayed there a moment. Then it went back.

Lyra walked up the stairs to Arithmancy with her hand closed round the ring in her pocket and thought about a travelling cloak with mud on its hem, and men in masks, and a family of Muggles turning slowly in the air above a laughing crowd.

If you ever see anybody using one, you come and tell me.

She would not. She did not even have to think about it.

On the night of Hallowe’en, the Goblet of Fire stood on a stool in front of the staff table, where everyone could see it, and every eye in the Great Hall was on it.

It had been standing in the Entrance Hall for a week behind a thin golden line on the floor that Dumbledore had drawn himself, so that nobody under seventeen could cross it, and the whole school had been to look. The Durmstrang students had come up out of the lake in a ship like a drowned galleon, in heavy furs, with Viktor Krum among them; and Draco, when Lyra wrote to tell him so, had written back for the first time since October, two whole sides, all of it about Krum and none of it about cards. The Beauxbatons girls had come out of a powder-blue carriage the size of a house, drawn by winged horses each as big as an elephant, and had shivered all through dinner in their silk. The Weasley twins had tried an Ageing Potion on the golden line and grown long white beards for their trouble. Lyra had watched it all, and enjoyed it more than she let anybody see, and had not once spoken to Potter.

Now the Hallowe’en feast was over, and the plates were clean, and Dumbledore was on his feet, and the candles in the pumpkins had been put out, so that the only light in the Hall came from the Goblet: a great rough wooden cup, full to the brim with dancing blue-white flames.

The flames turned red. A charred slip of parchment flew out of them and fluttered down into Dumbledore’s hand.

“The champion for Durmstrang,” he read, “is Viktor Krum!”

Nobody had expected anything else. Krum got up from the Slytherin table, where the Durmstrang students had been sitting all week, and slouched up to the front with his shoulders rounded, and went through the door behind the staff table, while the Hall clapped and Parkinson clapped loudest of all.

The flames turned red again.

“The champion for Beauxbatons is Fleur Delacour!”

A girl rose from the Ravenclaw table like a swan getting off a lake. She had a sheet of silvery-blond hair that went most of the way to her waist, and a face that seemed to have been designed for the sole purpose of making boys walk into doors, and every boy in the Hall turned to watch her go. Lyra made a small noise through her nose. Beauxbatons could not have much talent to choose from, she thought, if it had come down to a face.

A third time. The Hall held its breath; this one was theirs.

“The Hogwarts champion,” called Dumbledore, “is Cedric Diggory!”

The Hufflepuff table went off like a firework. Every one of them was on their feet, stamping and screaming, as Diggory made his way up through them, grinning, with people slapping him on the back all the way. The Slytherin sixth and seventh years booed. Montague brought his fist down on the table so hard that Goyle, who had been leaning on her elbow, slid off it.

“Well, that’s Hufflepuff insufferable till Easter,” said Lyra. But she could see why. If it had to be somebody, it might as well be the one person in the castle who was good at everything and played fair while he did it.

“Excellent!” Dumbledore was beaming. “Well, we now have our three champions. I am sure I can count upon all of you, including the remaining students from Durmstrang and Beauxbatons, to give your champions every ounce of support you can muster. By cheering your champion on, you will contribute in a very real—”

He stopped.

The Goblet of Fire had turned red again.

Sparks were flying out of it. A long flame shot up into the air, and on the end of it, a fourth piece of parchment. Dumbledore put out his hand, almost absently, and caught it.

He held it out, and looked at the name written on it, for a long moment. Then he cleared his throat, and his voice, when it came, was not loud at all.

“Harry Potter.”

The Hall did not erupt. There was no noise in it whatever.

Lyra sat at the Slytherin table and did not move. She was thinking of a green skull hanging in the sky over a dark wood, that nobody had seen for thirteen years. Of a tournament that had been stopped two hundred years ago because the people in it kept dying. Of a fourth champion who could not exist, because there were three schools and three champions and an old man’s golden line on the floor. And of the name the fourth champion had been given: a boy of fourteen, the same age as her, who had stood in a field full of tents and told her to go back to her mother.

Lyra was frightened. For once in her life she did not even try to call it anything else.
