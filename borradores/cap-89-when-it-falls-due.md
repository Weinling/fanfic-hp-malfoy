# Chapter 95: When It Falls Due

Her mother undid the bandage herself.

She did it in Lyra’s room at four in the afternoon, with the lamps lit and the door shut, standing behind the dressing-stool. Nothing had been said in the hall. Pobble had taken the fur and burst into tears, and been sent to see to the fires; Draco had gone up the stairs ahead of them, one at a time, with his hand on the rail; and now there was only the small exact sound of pins being laid in a dish.

The linen came away in three turns, stiff with dittany. Her mother folded it. Then she looked, in the glass, over Lyra’s shoulder, which was how she had always looked at most things.

Lyra had not seen it either.

It began under the ear, where the jaw turned, and it was the colour of the inside of a shell. As thick as a bootlace, it stood up from the skin, and went down the side of her neck and over the collarbone like the join in a piece of porcelain that has been mended by somebody who wished it to show. At the point of the shoulder it turned, and ran out along the top of the arm, and stopped a hand’s breadth above the elbow. Below that the arm was as it had been. It lay on the dressing-table among the brushes, with its two slow curves, and looked at the lamp.

Her mother’s eyes went there once. They came back.

“Severus has a very neat hand,” said Narcissa Malfoy.

“Yes, Mother.”

“One wouldn’t have thought so, from his cuffs.” She had taken up the hairbrush. She began at the ends, as she had when Lyra was six, and she did not hurry. “You’ll wear the grey tonight. The one with the square neck.”

“It will show.”

“I should think that it would.”

The brush went on. In the glass her mother’s face was as smooth as a plate, with the lamplight along one side of it; she had on the pearls, and there was nothing at the neck of her dress but a faint pale line where a chain had been.

“I have never once left this house with a mark on me,” she said. “I was brought up to believe that it mattered a great deal. I find I was mistaken about that, as about so much else. Sit straight.”

Lyra sat straight. And her mother, who had put a hand flat on the top of her son’s head every day of his life, and had had it stepped out from under that morning in front of forty people, laid down the brush, and put it on hers. She had not done that since Lyra was nine. It rested there, dry and light and rather cold, for perhaps as long as it takes to say a grace; and Lyra, who was sixteen, with a seam in her from the ear to the arm, kept perfectly still beneath it and looked at the two of them in the glass.

There were seven at table on the Sunday, and he had put Draco on his right.

He let them finish the soup. He never ate; he sat at the head, in her father’s chair, turning a glass by the stem, and Avery told a story, and Aunt Bella picked the almonds off something and put them in a row, as if she were laying out teeth. Professor Snape had come by the afternoon Floo and sat a long way down, in his black, with his sleeves buttoned. From the far side of the sideboard came the sound of somebody breathing through his nose, behind a door. Lyra had her back straight and her right hand where it belonged. The left lay in its sling. The square neck was cold.

“I had such an interesting Thursday,” said the Dark Lord.

He said it as a man might who has been to a concert. The talk went out along the table, voice by voice.

“*Twenty-three.* I did think that good. He has a sense of occasion, your young man; nobody taught it him. And then the speech. *She made you a sandwich.* I had tears in my eyes.” He smiled at her. “He had got as far as the old gentleman’s name, I think, or very nearly, when you had your wand in his face; and what did I hear then? Only the first of it. *Cru*—” He did it lightly, on a breath. “For me. To stop his mouth, in a lavatory, with nobody to see. My dear, I was touched. I don’t believe that Bella would have troubled to stop.”

“I’d have *finished*,” said Aunt Bella.

“Yes. That’s the difference between you.” He turned the glass. “And then his. Half a word again. He never does finish a thing, that boy; I put it down to his upbringing. After that, I am sorry to say, I heard nothing at all, for about the length of a song. It was the oddest sensation. I have had that ear open since the first of August, and it had not so much as blinked.” His eyes had gone to the sling, with a mild, proprietary interest. “It appears to sleep when you are very nearly gone. I hadn’t known that. One never does know quite everything about one’s own work. I shall have to take care that you are not often so ill. Show me.”

She turned her head to the candles.

He got up. He came round behind the chairs, trailing his fingers along the backs, as he had come a year ago, all but a week, to look at a piece of blackened silver; and he laid the tip of one finger under her ear, at the top of it, and drew it slowly down. She might have been having a line ruled on her with a cold pen. He followed it over the collarbone to the edge of the grey silk, and there he stopped, because he had beautiful manners.

“It’s very like,” he said. “Bella, come and see.”

She had come already. She was hanging over Lyra’s other shoulder, breathing on her, with her own cut standing a little open down the side of her face and a wet shine in it; and her eyes went from one to the other and back.

“It’s *longer*,” said Aunt Bella. Her voice had gone high. “It goes right down. He only did my face. Why’s hers longer? I’d killed his dog for him, I’d just *done* it, and he gave me half a word, and she gets it from the neck to the—” She put out a long dirty finger and took it back. “And it’s shut. Who shut it? I don’t want mine shut. I didn’t ask to have it shut. Who did that?”

“I did,” said Snape.

He had not looked up from his plate. He was cutting something small into something smaller.

“The boy came to me in September, my lord, wishing to learn how one undid what he had done in June. I judged that you would prefer me to know what he was asking for. I have had him on Tuesdays since, with a side of pork.” The knife went on. “He has no ear whatever.”

“No,” said the Dark Lord. “I have that.”

It got a laugh. Avery led it. Lyra sat with the finger resting on her collarbone and saw, down the length of the cloth, that Professor Snape’s right cuff was buttoned a good deal more closely than his left.

“And you, Narcissa, may be easy.” He had not moved. “I do keep my bargains. He had it from your son and not from your daughter; I heard every word of that, and it was nicely done on both sides. Lyme stands exactly where it stood. I shouldn’t like you to think that I had lost count.”

Her mother inclined her head. She had her hand on the stem of a glass.

“Now,” said the Dark Lord. “This song.”

He came in.

Nothing announced it, as nothing ever did: no hand, no noise, only that somebody was standing inside the door who had wiped his boots. She had the breakfast table ready, with the sun on it. He went by that without turning his head. He had not come for the furniture tonight. He walked straight through, as a man walks through a house that he has taken for the season, to the one place that she had not had time to make fit to be seen: a wet floor, at dusk, with a row of basins.

She could not keep him out of it. She had not supposed that she could. What she had done, on the stairs, and in the Floo, and behind the bed-curtains at one in the morning, was to go over that floor on her knees and put away what could be put away. A thing that she had said, and another that she’d only half got out: those were in the wall. She did not look at the wall. She stood beside the basin, very small, a girl who had been hurt, and left out for him what there had to be: the cold of the stone under her, the red on the water, a boy’s face upside down with his glasses hanging off one ear. And the noise that he had been making.

He stopped at that.

She felt him bend. He put out a finger and lifted it, the whole of it: three notes and a fourth, cracked right across, in a voice that had no business to be singing anything, with the tears running into its mouth. *Please*, it sang, with no words to it. *Please. Come.* She had lain under that with her eyes going dark and thought that if it was the last thing she heard it would do. He turned it to the light. He looked underneath.

He set it down, a little to the left.

The candles came back. There was a skin on the gravy.

“What a noise,” said the Dark Lord, with real sympathy. He had gone back to his chair. “And a whole term of lessons. Severus, I do feel for you.”

Some minutes went by before anyone spoke to her again, and she used them. She went to look. Nothing was missing. She could have written out the notes on a stave; she knew to the breath where it had cracked, and that she had cried, and why. She knew it as one knows the date of a treaty. She stood in front of it, and waited, and it was a very good likeness of a boy singing.

*That was four*, thought Lyra.

She had asked him on Friday to teach it to her. She was glad that she had got that in first.

“You took my box to Harry Potter,” said the Dark Lord.

He had turned to his right. Draco put down his fork. He had sat through all of this with his hands in his lap and his hair flat, and he was the colour of the cloth, and his voice, when it came, was small and careful and perfectly clear.

“Yes, my lord.”

“You were asked to bring it to me.”

“Yes, my lord. On the seventh floor.”

“And you didn’t. Why was that?”

Her mother’s fingers had closed. Lyra saw them. Nobody at that table was breathing very much, and under it something heavy shifted, and lay along her foot.

“I’d promised him first,” said Draco.

“First.”

“In November. Last year. It was by a suit of armour. He said if anybody ever asked me to go anywhere at night I had to tell him before I went, and I said I would. You asked me after that.” He appeared to check it over. “So his was first.”

Nobody spoke. Then the Dark Lord began to laugh: the pleasant one, the light one, with the delight in it, and it went on for rather longer than was comfortable; and Aunt Bella, who had begun to laugh as well, stopped, and put two fingers in her mouth.

“He keeps them in the order that he made them,” said the Dark Lord. “Lucius, you know, never could. He always paid whoever was nearest.” He looked down at the fair combed head with something that in another man would have been fondness. “He ran to Potter. How very useful that will be. And I have been short of a clerk since the summer; I was saying so to somebody only the other day. I don’t think we shall send you back to Scotland, Draco. The air doesn’t agree with your family. You’ll stay with me.”

“May I keep my Herbology book?” said Draco.

“You may keep whatever you like. Can you pour tea?”

“No, my lord.”

“Then I shall teach you. One holds the lid.”

It was done as easily as that. Lyra had argued for an hour, in July, standing up, with headings, to get that child out from under this man’s hand; she had cried in front of Avery; she had a thing on her arm that was the price of it. She sat and watched it handed back across a cloth in the time it takes to pass the salt. Her brother had been asked to stay in a castle, by somebody who had gone on one knee to do it, and had said *no, thank you*, and come home to this.

He had not finished. He had picked his fork up, and he put it down again.

“Greer Goyle ate one,” said Draco.

“So I heard. I was sorry for it. I’m told that she cooked.”

“She gave me pastry.”

“Then you have something to remember her by.” It was said with great courtesy, and he had already turned away. “Not everybody leaves as much. Miss Malfoy.”

She raised her eyes.

“I said the twentieth. I recall it perfectly. You stood up at this table and asked for the thing, and I gave it to you, and I said by when; and it’s the twenty-second, and the old gentleman has eaten his Christmas pudding, and the box is on his desk beside the sweets.” He turned the glass. “I’m a man of my word. I find, to my regret, that I am also a householder. One does not put down the housekeeper in the middle of the winter; there would be nobody to answer the door. So it stands over. I’ve put it with your mother’s. You needn’t thank me.”

She thanked him. Somewhere down the table a knife touched a plate, once, with a very small clear sound; and when she looked, Professor Snape had laid it down, and was holding his right wrist under the cloth.

“Besides, it was hardly your fault that he came for it,” said the Dark Lord, agreeably. “He’d have found a pale box at the bottom of a trunk in a week, that boy, if I’d told him where to look; and I had it from you yourself, one Sunday in October, that it was in the left boot. I thought of that when I wrote my little note. I do so like a thing to come round.”

She took the soup up on the Thursday, since somebody had to.

They had moved the bed nearer the window. He had got very small in it. The marks had gone the colour of wet slate, and the hands went on under the linen, though more slowly, like something that is running down; and he no longer asked for the knight to be moved. She tied the napkin. It was barley. She took up the spoon, with her thumb on top, and brought it in from the side.

His eyes were on her neck.

“Who sh-shut that?”

“Professor Snape.”

“Ah.” It seemed to give him some private satisfaction, and he swallowed, and she waited for it. “And you’ve had this d-done to you. Since I saw you. I can tell. You’ve stopped watching the spoon and started w-watching the mouth.”

“On Friday.”

“By whom?”

“Potter.”

She had expected the laugh. She had been ready with the cup. It didn’t come; he lay and looked at her, with the pale clear eyes going over her face from one side to the other, and the hands going under the cloth.

“Did he b-bring it in from the side?”

“I told him to.”

“Then he takes instruction,” said Abraxas Malfoy. “I never m-met one that did. I’d k-keep that, child, if I were you. It’s worth more than the other thing.”

He did not say what the other thing was. He shut his eyes, and she gave him the rest of it, a spoonful at a time, and wiped his chin.

It was a year to the night.

She thought he must have meant it so. He had them all in the dining room at nine on the thirty-first: the same cloth, the same six candles, and the soup; only there was a small soft dirty man by the service door where there had been none, and her father’s place was taken by Avery, and at the right hand of the chair a boy of twelve stood up when he was looked at and poured out a cup of tea that nobody was going to drink. He held the lid. He did it very well. He had been at it for a week.

The Dark Lord watched the cup filled, and thanked him, and let it stand.

“I have been sitting here thinking of last year,” he said. “You brought me a little crown in a box, Draco, do you remember, and I put it on the cloth just there; and it had been burnt, and I asked your sister a question about it.” He looked along the table. “I asked how he had known that it was there. A thing that I hid myself, at seventeen, in a room that nobody had found for a thousand years. A boy of fourteen walks up seven floors on his first night back at school and goes straight to it. I thought about that a good deal, in the snow. I’ve gone on thinking about it. It is a year, and do you know, I’m no nearer?”

His eyes rested on her. They were perfectly friendly.

“You have never been able to tell me, my dear.”

“No, my lord.”

“No. Nor has Wormtail, who has been through his pockets for the better part of a year. Nor has Severus, who has him every Tuesday with his mind laid open like a book. I had a moment in the boy’s own head, in June, and I came out with a tent.” He sighed. He seemed to find it rather restful, not knowing. “Well. He’ll tell me himself, one of these days. I shall ask him across this table.”

He turned to Avery, and asked him something about trains.

She went on with her soup.

She had got as far as the fish before she heard what he had said. It went by her, as a thing does that one has been told too often; and then it came back, quite slowly, and stood in front of her plate.

He didn’t know.

She put her spoon down in the proper place. She took up her fork. She ate what was put in front of her, and answered when she was spoken to, and at ten o’clock she said good night to her mother on the stairs and went along the gallery to her own room, and shut the door, and sat down on the window-seat in the dark with her back against the shutter.

Then she did it as a column, because it had to be done as something.

On one side she put down everything that he had ever handed back to her. There was a good deal. A line about a godfather’s death: that was her own report, of the nineteenth of May, on the second-best paper. A hem; a note with lemon in it. *He has nits, and it preys upon him*: a spoilt sheet that she had screwed up and thrown at somebody’s head. *Sat. 17th. 8.30, not 9. Wear the socks*: that had gone into a pocket. The Bishop of Bath and Wells, with a line ruled through him; and she had sat at this table in June and felt the floor go, because nobody on earth had known about the Bishop.

Somebody had written him down, however. She had. She had drafted him, and thought better of it, and crossed him out, and dropped the strip on a rug.

She went on. A thrush in the second lime: that was August, and the ear. *Please come*: the ear again, and six hundred other people; it had been said in the Great Hall at breakfast. A tent with a dragon outside it: he’d had that out of the boy himself, in the Atrium, and had told her so tonight. The Herodotus. The boot.

That was the whole of one side. Every line of it was paper, or it had been said within a room’s length of her left arm after the first of August, or he had taken it from behind a pair of glasses in the time that it takes to sneeze.

She turned to the other.

She put down a chess set, in a cold classroom, in the Christmas of her first year, and who had sat down to it opposite her and played the black so badly; she had found that out in April, in an armchair, and laughed until she cried. She put down what had been in the green book before her grandfather ever read it, and what she had said as she wrote, and that a chair had given way. A boy sitting with his elbows on his knees who thought that he had eaten somebody; and what she had asked him about the feet. *Yours.* The eighteenth of June, three weeks before it came. Another life, the whole length of it, from a cupboard to some trees.

*How had he known that it was there?*

Because he had been before. He had told her so in an Owlery, and after that, in that room, on that rug, with his glasses off, he had told her everything else. If one small dirty frightened creature had been sitting by that fender, on any one night out of a hundred and forty, then there was a man downstairs who would have had the answer for a year.

She looked at the second column for a long time. There was not one line of it that he had ever said.

*You do keep it so warm up there.* Anybody could have told him that. It had a fire in it, and always had; one could feel it through the wall.

The rat had never been inside. It could not have got in. The room came when one needed it, and one had to walk by three times and ask; it had never opened to anybody yet who hadn’t. Harry had said so, in the first week, with his mouth full. *It’s the only room in the castle that can’t lie to you.* The thing had sat in a dormitory, in the dark, on the floor by a boy’s trunk, and gone through his pockets, and through the wastepaper. And it had carried what it found four hundred miles to a person who had been brought up in an institution in south London, who had held other boys’ letters to a candle before he had finished his tea, and had never in his life got out of the habit.

He had read her post. That was all that he had ever done.

She had bitten a hole in a sheet over it.

Something was happening in her chest. It hurt a great deal; it hurt where the house was. She put both hands over her mouth. They were no use, and she got up, in the dark, and went along the gallery in her stockings, past her great-great-grandmother, and down the three steps by the old nurseries, to a door that she had last opened in June. It smelt of lavender and hot pipes. She got in at the back, between the winter blankets. She took down a double sheet from the bottom of the pile, and folded it in four, and put her face in it.

She did not bite it. She laughed.

She had never had a worse laugh. It came up in whoops, with her knees drawn in and the stitches pulling, and tears with it, and no sound that would have carried through a wall of ordinary thickness. A bad armchair with a book under its leg. A boy with egg on his tie. She had mourned that room for six months as one mourns a person. She had sat in it nine feet away from him, reading Thucydides in a pleasant voice, and looked at the skirting. And it had been sitting up there on the seventh floor the whole time with its fire lit, perfectly sound, waiting to be asked for; and it had never been anything but kind.

In the village they had begun to ring.

She stayed until it was over. Then she folded the sheet, damp side in, with the corners true, and put it back.

At breakfast her mother came in from the stillroom with a list in her hand.

“The linen was counted this morning,” said Narcissa Malfoy. She did not look up from it. “I find that nothing has been torn.”

“No, Mother.”

“I am very glad of it.” She turned the page. “Draco, one does not put the milk in first.”

She went back on the fifth, by the Floo in the south drawing room, with one trunk.

He came to see her off. He did that sort of thing. He stood on the hearthrug with his hands behind him and the long shape coiled against the fender, taking the warmth, and at his elbow, in the clean grey suit, with a tray, stood her brother. The tie was under his ear. Nobody had touched it in a fortnight. Professor Snape waited by the mantelpiece with the jar of powder, looking at the clock.

She had her cloak on over the sling, and the square neck under that. She had been wearing it for fifteen days. One got used to where people’s eyes went.

“I should like to send a message to Longbottom, please,” said Draco.

He had not said a word to her since the Entrance Hall. He said this one to the tray.

“He said I could count the Mandrakes again in the spring, when they’ve been potted on. I shan’t be there. Will you tell him that I expect there’ll still be forty? Unless one’s died. He ought to look behind the pots.”

“I’ll tell him,” said Lyra.

“Thank you.”

He did not look up. He stood very straight, holding the tray quite level, as though it had water in it, and she saw that there was a second cup on it this morning, with something in it; and that while nobody was attending he had slid that one, by about an inch, to her side.

She drank it. It was stewed, and he had put the milk in first.

“What a careful child it is,” said the Dark Lord. “He has counted my shirts. Severus, I think we’re keeping you.”

The powder was held out. She took a pinch in her right hand. She had turned to the fire, and got as far as the fender, when the voice came after her, quite lightly, as a hostess calls something down a stair.

“You’ll be home again at Easter, Miss Malfoy. I looked it out; the twenty-second of March, by the afternoon train. A Saturday.” She did not turn round. She could see him in the glass over the mantel, with his head a little on one side, and a small pale face beside his elbow. “He cut you from the ear to the arm, and then he sat by your bed and fed you barley with a spoon. I heard the spoon. A boy who has done that will go anywhere at all, if he’s asked by the right person.”

The flames had gone green. Beside her, a long yellow hand had closed very slowly on the lid of a jar.

“When you come home at Easter,” said the Dark Lord, “bring him with you. I shall have a place laid. And if you should find that you come by yourself, my dear, then I’m afraid we must say that it has fallen due: yours, and your mother’s, both together. I do think it’s kinder to know the day.”
