# Chapter 40: Hidden Ink

The ring did not want to come off.

It had shrunk to fit her finger under a cloister thirteen months ago, and again in a corridor in December, and it had been there ever since, and now that she wanted it gone it held on. Lyra sat on the end of her bed with the hangings drawn on the Tuesday morning and worked at it with her thumb, round and round, until the skin under it was red; and then it gave, all at once, and slid off over the knuckle, and lay in her palm. It was green. It had been green since the podium.

She wrapped it in a twist of tissue paper so that she would not have to look at what colour it went next.

The tin was from Honeydukes, the kind the shortbread came in at Christmas, with a Scottish terrier on the lid in a tartan coat. She had eaten the shortbread in March. She put the ring in, in its tissue, and sat looking at it in the bottom of the empty tin.

Then she opened the drawer of her bedside table and took out the handkerchief.

It had been washed so many times, before she ever had it, that it was soft as old silk, and she had washed it again herself, and pressed it, and kept it folded small in the drawer since a staircase in January a year and a half ago. It was his. She had known it was his since the night of the final. She had never given it back, and he had never asked.

She put it in the tin beside the ring.

She looked at it there, for some time.

Then she took it out again, and folded it smaller, and put it back in the drawer, and shut the drawer.

The note she wrote at the desk by the window, in her best hand: the small, upright, perfect hand her governess had taught her at six and her mother had taught her to be proud of.

*Congratulations on the Cup.*

*This is where we end. The time we had was not entirely dull.*

*L. M.*

She left a gap in the second line. Between *we end* and *The time*: a blank in the middle of the sentence, the width of two fingers, where nothing was written. No one who had ever seen her hand would believe she had left it by accident. She did not leave gaps.

She took out her wand and touched its tip to the blank.

The parchment gave a soft crack, like a seal breaking, and split along its own thickness into two thin layers that lifted apart at the edge, exactly as her father’s letters did; he had shown her how when she was eight, for a birthday treasure hunt, and she had never once used it for anything until now. Between the layers the parchment was clean and pale, like the inside of a shell.

She wrote, very small, with the finest nib she had:

*Barty Crouch put your name in the Goblet. He is under the Imperius. I saw his face on the podium; I know that face. His son is alive.*

She looked at it.

*His son is alive.* She had not known she knew that until she wrote it. She had known it the way she knew where the stairs were in the dark: a man who sent his son to Azkaban *to die there*, and stopped, and looked at the door; a dead man said twice; a grave Moody had stood at; a chair in the top box that had creaked at the first Irish goal, beside an elf with her hands over her eyes, minding a seat for somebody nobody could see; a man walking very straight with a crate in his arms, as though somebody had told him where to put his eyes. She did not know how. She knew who.

She did not write anything about the north. She held the nib above the parchment for a long moment, and did not.

Then she touched the blank again, and the two layers came together with a small sigh and were one sheet, with a gap in the second line where nothing was written; and she folded it, and laid it on top of the tissue paper in the tin, and put on the lid with the Scottish terrier, and tied it up with green ribbon.

“Greer,” she said, through the hangings.

Goyle’s head came round the edge of them almost at once, as though it had been waiting.

“Take this to the hospital wing,” said Lyra. “Give it to Potter. Don’t say who it’s from. Don’t wait for an answer.”

Goyle looked at the tin, and at Lyra’s face, and at Lyra’s hand, which had no ring on it; and for once in her life Greer Goyle did not say anything at all. She took the tin in both hands as though it might go off, and went.

She came back twenty minutes later, scarlet to the ears, out of breath from the stairs. She did not say anything then, either. She sat down on her own bed, and took a Chocolate Frog out of her pocket, and did not eat it.
