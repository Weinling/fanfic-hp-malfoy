# Chapter 87: Ten

Ron found him in the changing room.

It was the Monday. Harry had stayed behind after practice, to do something to his broom that didn’t need doing. It smelt of wet wool and liniment and it had started to get dark. He heard the door go. He knew the feet, and he went on with what he was doing; and they came along between the benches and stopped, and he looked up.

Ron had a badge on, with a dent across the middle. He’d got bigger through the neck. He stood with his arms hanging, in his practice things, and looked at a point on the wall about a foot above Harry’s head. Apart from Saturday, he had said three words to Harry since May, and it looked as if he would rather have swallowed a Bludger than make it four.

“Ginny’s lost an hour,” said Ron.

It didn’t mean anything, for a moment.

“Saturday. After the match. She went up for a long ball, and the next thing she knows it’s dark, and she’s in the passage behind Charms with her gloves in her hand and no idea how she got there. She didn’t tell anyone. She didn’t tell me till last night. I found her in the common room at three o’clock in the morning, with a candle. She’s got everything that she’s written since September, she’s got it all out on the floor. She’s going over it with a pencil.”

His voice cracked in the middle. He brought his eyes down. They were red round the rims, and they were very blue, and they came to rest on Harry’s face, and it was the first time they had done that for five and a half months.

“You know about this,” said Ron. “Don’t you? You know about all of it. You knew last time. So you can tell me. Is it *back?*”

A door banged somewhere. Behind him, at the end of the row of pegs, somebody thin was standing with a notebook held against her chest.

“I’d like you to come and look,” said Hermione. “If you wouldn’t mind.”

They used the boys’ prefects’ cupboard, on the fourth floor, because it had a lock.

Ginny sat on an upturned crate with her knees together. She had got her hair scraped back and two sheets of parchment on her lap, and she didn’t look up when he came in. She hadn’t slept. There was a smudge of lead along the side of her hand. He’d last seen her in a kitchen, on a Thursday in August; she had nodded to him since, in corridors, as you’d nod to someone who owed you money.

“It’s not like before,” she said. “I’ll tell you what it’s like, and you can stop looking at me like that. When it was the book, I’d come to, and there’d be things. There were feathers on my robes. I had paint under my nails. It was all dirty: there’d be bits sticking out, and something would be in the wrong place. I’d have got a sort of taste. This hasn’t got anything. I’ve looked. I had a shower, I think, because I’m clean. I’ve got a split lip that I remember getting. I went up for that long ball, in the last ten minutes, and I was looking over at Malfoy, I don’t know why. I remember she’d lost her cord. I remember the back of her head, with her hair all coming down.” She put out one finger, and drew a short straight line on the top sheet. “And then there’s my glove. There’s somebody telling me I’ve dropped it. I don’t know who. It’s just a voice in the air. There’s nothing in between. It doesn’t hurt, it doesn’t feel like anything. It’s like when you get to the bottom of a page, and you turn over, and someone’s been along it with a razor.”

Nobody said anything.

“It’s got a very clean edge,” said Ginny.

Something went down the back of Harry’s neck, slowly, like a drop of cold water.

He didn’t know why. He stood in a cupboard that smelt of floor polish, and for an instant the whole of the wall seemed to have gone a long way off. There was a stone path, and some trees. *He was not afraid.* He had looked at it with a man in a dungeon. He looked up, and Hermione’s brown eyes were resting on him, with an odd, attentive, careful look. She looked away.

“It’s a Memory Charm,” she said. “I’ve been through everything. It isn’t possession. It’s nothing to do with any kind of diary; I’d stake my life on that. It’s a very good Memory Charm. It’s the best that I have ever heard of. Somebody took one hour out and left the sides quite smooth. It isn’t on the syllabus until the seventh year, and you can’t do that from a book. You’d have to have a wonderful wrist.” She closed the notebook. She didn’t snap the band; she laid it in place. “There can’t be more than four people in this building who could do that. Two of them are on the staff.”

She didn’t look at him. She looked at the sheet of parchment on Ginny’s knee, at the last thing there was on that side of the line, and he watched her do it, as he had seen her go down a column. She had got there days ago. She had added it up and looked at what it came to, and then she had gone and fetched him; and she stood there, thin and straight, with a long pink scar running up under her sleeve, and she didn’t say a word. She’d lied for him once, for six months, and told him what that was worth.

“Well?” said Ron.

He had his back against the door. There wasn’t room for him. He’d taken hold of a mop that was standing in the corner, without seeming to notice, and he had it in both his fists.

“Is it back? Harry. Is it *him?* Is he in my sister? I’ll go to Dumbledore. I’ll go now. I just want somebody to tell me.”

Harry looked at Ginny. She had raised her eyes at last. They were perfectly steady. They went to the side of his head, a little behind the right ear, and they stayed there, for about a second, and came back.

He knew who it was. He’d known it since *the back of her head*. He had seen a Snitch go past the end of somebody’s glove, and a thin face in the rain, screaming with laughter, and he’d seen her go in at the doors. There was a line in a black notebook: *Nov. 9. Hair down. Late to the party. 25 mins.*

*She was eleven*, somebody had said, in a dusty room with a tapestry, with her voice ringing on the glass.

“It’s not back,” said Harry.

“How d’you know?”

He’d have liked to have a good answer. He had a number of them. He looked at his best friend, in two lives, standing in a cupboard with a mop; he had gone out on a moor with that boy, and held the other end of a slate. He could feel his own face. It was tired, and honest, and a bit ashamed. He knew just where to put it. His hands had gone into his pockets.

“I just know,” said Harry. “It was destroyed. I saw it. It’s not that. She’s safe, I swear. It’s not going to happen again.”

He heard it. So did everyone. There was a short silence in the cupboard. A tap was dripping, on the other side of the wall.

Ron looked at him for a long moment. He nodded, slowly, two or three times, as you might if somebody had explained to you about the rent. He put the mop back in the corner, and he stood it quite carefully, so that it shouldn’t fall.

“Yeah,” said Ron. “You said that.”

He held the door open for his sister. She gathered her papers, and as she went by she stopped, about a foot away, and looked down at his boots. “You’ve got odd socks,” said Ginny, very quietly. “I should see to that.” Ron didn’t look back. His big feet went away along the corridor, and it wasn’t until they had got to the stairs that Harry noticed there was something in his hand: a Chocolate Frog, rather squashed, that must have been put there in the changing room. Ron had always carried one, for after practice.

He stood and held it. Hermione had gone. She had left the door ajar.

He went up on the Friday.

He had walked past the wall four times by then, and turned away. He’d had five days to think what he would say, and he had sat in Potions and looked at the back of a neck. *Nov. 12. Ate nothing. Nov. 13. Took 10 pts. from Longbottom for his plant.* He had gone down to the dungeon on Tuesday and closed a long red gash in a leg of mutton, and sung till his throat bled, and he had thought he might be going to be calm.

At a quarter past nine there was a good fire, and the two chairs, nine feet apart, and she was in the old one with her feet under her, reading. He shut the door. He stayed where he was on the end of the rug, with the cauldron shining dully on its trivet between them and his hands in his pockets, and he found it was hard to breathe.

“She lost an hour,” said Harry.

She turned over a page. She seemed to finish the sentence.

“Did she? How careless.”

He’d thought she might say that. He had gone through all the ways she could, on the stairs. He hadn’t expected what it would be like: the pleasant voice, exactly as usual, going in somewhere under the breastbone and keeping on.

“Ginny Weasley. Saturday. She’s got everything she’s written since September out on the floor, and she’s going over it with a pencil. Letter by letter. She’s fifteen. She thought it had come back.” He’d started quite quietly. He could hear it beginning to go. “She thought it had come *back*, Malfoy. She sat in a cupboard and looked at me. You know what that was. You know what you did. There’s nobody who knows better. You stood in that room, the one with the knight, and you said it to me. You stood there with your hair coming down, and you screamed it. *She was eleven!* You said, *she was eleven, Potter*, you said it right to my face—”

“I recall the occasion,” said Lyra.

“Why?”

She laid the book on the arm of the chair, with one finger in it. She raised her head. Her eyes went by him. They went by on the left, quite slowly, and they settled at a point in the air a little above his shoulder; and they stayed.

He’d seen that for three months, and thought it was to do with him. He stood and looked at the small pale composed face, with the fire on one side of it, and it came up from under his ribs, all at once, the whole of it: five months of mornings. It had been in his mouth since a fountain. He had got up with it and shaved with it, and walked round it on a platform and told her *No*. He had told Alastor Moody a beautiful lie, and lain on the floor of a corridor with his ear against a door.

“By four o’clock,” said Harry.

Nothing moved. Somewhere in the chimney a little soot came down.

He hadn’t known it would sound so small.

“He stopped in front of me. In the Atrium. Your father. With his hands tied.” His lips felt thick. “*By four o’clock, as I asked. You may tell my daughter it was very well done.* I never told you that. You’ve heard it, though. I saw you on the platform. I’ve been— I want to know what he asked you.”

She didn’t answer at once. She drew her finger out of the book, let it close, and moved it a little on the arm of the chair until it was straight. There was a slight pause; Hermione would have told you how long, to the second. Her lips parted, she took that small breath, and she spoke, very clearly, with her head turned by about an inch, to the air by the window, in the tone of somebody who would like it to be taken down.

“He wrote to me on the seventeenth of June. I had it at supper.”

The fire ticked.

“He said you must be out of the castle on the following day, by four. He said I needn’t know why. He said I’d always been able to make you go where I wished, and that he had relied on it.” She looked at her hand. “I burnt it in a hairpin box. Then I came up here. You’ll remember. You asked me to wear the ring.”

He couldn’t feel his feet. He’d thought about this, he had thought about it for five months. It didn’t seem to have helped.

“You never told me that.”

“No.”

“You said *Is that all of it*— I asked you. In that room. I said, is that everything, and you looked at me, and you said *Yes*.”

“Yes,” said Lyra.

“You took Sirius off the moor.”

“I did. On the sixth. I’ve had it pointed out to me how well it answered. There was nowhere that you could have looked.” It was perfectly level. “You’d have stayed in your chair, otherwise. You had made such careful arrangements. It was all written down. It needed only the owl to come back with its letter, and you’d go. I had thought it would have to be a good deal more complicated.”

It was very quiet. He could hear his own blood. He had the most peculiar feeling, as though the floor had been tilted by a very small amount.

He knew what she was saying. It wasn’t as though he’d never thought of it. It had gone through his head in a pink room, with the green fire behind her, and looked about, and it hadn’t seemed to be a stranger.

But he’d *seen* her.

“No,” said Harry.

She waited.

“No. That’s not— You ran. I was there. I saw you. You came down that corridor, you’d been all over the castle, you’d got ink on your wrist, you couldn’t breathe. Your hair was coming down. You stood in front of the fire, you got between me and it. You said *Don’t go*. You said *He’s not there*, you said *Listen to me*, you tried to tell me where he was. *He’s with my*— You were saying it! I had to knock you down to shut you up! You lay on that carpet for five hours and ten minutes, Crabbe found you, your eyes were— That’s what happened. I did that. Don’t you dare tell me that you— You told me not to go!” His voice had cracked right across. He’d taken his hands out of his pockets. He didn’t know when. They were out in the air, open, shaking, with nothing in them. “You stood at the fire! You told me not to go!”

For an instant she looked at him.

It was so quick. He would never be sure. He’d go over it, a very great number of times. Her eyes came back from the window, and they met his, full on; and they were not the colour of nothing. There was someone in them, a long way down, at the bottom of something, looking up, with her mouth open. He took a step. She looked at his hands.

Then she had turned her head again, by that inch, and she smiled a little at the air.

“Yes,” said Lyra. “It’s the surest way I know.”

He stood where he was.

It didn’t hurt, he noticed, with interest. There was a sort of ringing. The thing she had said went on and on, in the warm room, and it was just a few words long, and it wasn’t true. He held on to that with both hands; he’d have sworn to it on anything you liked. Nobody could lie on a floor with their eyes running, for five hours, for the sake of a remark. She was lying to his face, very well, in a nice clear voice, reading it out to him off some piece of paper he couldn’t see, and she wasn’t even looking at him while she did it.

Why?

He could feel that opening up. It was worse. He hadn’t known that anything could be. If it was true, then at least he’d know what she was. And if it wasn’t, she had looked at him, standing here with his hands out, and of all the things that she might have said— He thought about a bathroom. He thought about a thin clear voice behind a door. *He has a cauldron.*

“Frightened of it, am I?” said Harry.

He held up his right hand, with the white letters on it, in the firelight. It wasn’t steady. He saw her eyes go to it. Something went through her, from the feet up, and stopped; a very fine thing, like the shiver on a glass when someone has walked across the floor above. Then she was perfectly still. So she knew about that, as well. He’d have liked it to feel better.

“Who are you talking to?” he said.

It was only a whisper. He hadn’t meant to say it.

“You, I’d imagined,” said Lyra.

There was something hot on his finger.

He looked down at his hand, and for a moment he didn’t understand. He’d had that ring on the same finger for two and a half years, and it had been a plain thin band of silver since the morning he stood on a chalk hill. Hers had gone back on its ribbon; he had seen the green at the neck of her dress. It would have to be against her skin.

It had gone blue.

It was a deep, dark, unhappy blue, like the sky an hour after the sun has set. He’d seen that once, in her palm, on a night in October, when he was fifteen. Neither of them had said what it was. They had both known.

He raised his eyes.

She hadn’t moved. She sat in the old chair with her chin up and her face as smooth as a plate, looking at the air above his shoulder. She had taken her feet from under her and set them side by side on the rug. As he watched, she lifted her hands from the arms of the chair, one after the other, without any hurry, and laid them down, very straight, at her sides. She didn’t look at them. She got them quite exact. He had done that once, and thought it was important to get it right.

“It was very well done,” said Lyra. “I’m told.”

He got to the door. He didn’t remember how. He found he’d picked up the cauldron, with his bare hands, by its rim, and it was burning him; he stood looking at that, and he set it down again. He wasn’t thinking anything. He went out, and along the empty seventh floor, with something cold and blue on his finger, and behind him the wall closed up and was stone.

She had stayed in the chair. He’d looked back, as the door swung to. She was sitting there, just as he had left her, as neat as a pin, with her hands laid straight. She wasn’t reading.

It was a fine cold morning, and the Great Hall was full of thin sun.

He hadn’t been to bed. He shaved, and came in at twenty past eight with his bag on his shoulder and his tie done up, and sat down at the end of the Gryffindor table with a piece of toast that he didn’t eat. He felt quite well. He felt very light, and rather cold, and a long way up, and he could see everything with great distinctness. It was a sensation he had known once before.

He could see the Slytherin table, forty feet off. She’d come down, with her back very straight and a cup at her elbow, Goyle next to her and Crabbe two places along. There wasn’t a pin out of place. She looked as if she’d slept for nine hours. She was buttering something.

He got up. It was a Saturday, and he was walking down the Hall, because it was what they did, and had been doing for a year. *A formal programme of hostilities*. People saw him coming and started to grin, and there was a pleasant small stir all along the benches, as there is when the band strikes up. You could set your watch by it: Potter and Malfoy. He stopped at the end of the Slytherin table, by Parkinson’s elbow, with his hands in his pockets, and looked down at her. She took her time. She raised one eyebrow, by an eighth of an inch.

“Why, Potter,” said Lyra. It carried very nicely. “You’ve got egg on your tie. I should have thought it was too early in the day to be so disappointing.”

It got a good laugh, all down the table. That was the script. It was his line next, and he had a very good one, about her hair.

He looked at the small steady hand with the knife in it, and the smooth pale face, and there was nothing there at all. He would have given his right hand, and welcome, to see one thing happen to that face: one crack, one flinch, anything to show that there was a person in the house. He wanted her to look at him. He’d have liked to hurt her. It was as simple as that, and it turned out that he knew how.

He’d been told. She had told him herself, on a rug, on a night in April, with her fist against his chest and her face running with tears; she had given it to him, the one thing that she had never given to anybody. It had been the happiest night of her life. He had been keeping it safe.

He didn’t raise his voice. He had no need to. It was very clear, and rather pleasant, and it went down the length of the bench.

“I hear you’ve stopped writing to your father,” said Harry. “That’s a pity. You always did it so well. How did it go, again? Two words, wasn’t it? Christmas, first year. *Please come*.”

It wasn’t much. Nobody laughed; they didn’t know what it was. A first-year started to giggle, and stopped, and it went a little quiet at that end of the table, in a puzzled way, as it does when somebody has played a wrong note. Two places along, Crabbe lifted her big pale face from her plate and looked at him, slowly. She had been eleven too. She had not known, he saw, about that either.

Lyra had stopped buttering.

The knife stayed where it was, with its edge on the toast. It stayed for one breath. He watched. He watched as hard as he’d ever watched anything. He saw her eyelids come down, about a sixteenth of an inch, and rise. Her eyes were on his. They were on his, and there wasn’t any air between. They were the palest grey, and they didn’t blink. He’d seen them look like that in an Owlery, with her nose running.

Then the knife moved. It went on along the toast. She buttered right up to the crust, and across, and she did the corner. She laid it down, with its handle parallel to the edge of the plate.

“How very like you,” said Lyra, “to remember.”

She picked up her cup.

He stood there. He’d thought it would feel like something. He could feel his own heart going. Behind his right ear, very quietly, something had turned round, and lain down.

There was a small sound, beside him. Blaise Zabini was sitting across the table from her, with his long legs crossed and a plate of kippers. He had been there the whole time. He had taken a pencil from behind his ear. He had a small square of card that he used for the pastry, and he wrote on that, without any hurry. He looked at what he had written. Then he reached across the table and laid it on the butter dish, facing Harry, and he took his fingers away.

*10.*

He had never given one. Everybody knew it; Goyle had been trying for a year and a half. He raised his dark, tired, intelligent eyes to Harry’s face, and there wasn’t any amusement in them at all. It made him look a good deal older.

“I should stop there, if I were you,” said Zabini quietly. He looked along the table, at the straight back and the cup; he looked for a moment, and he looked away. “Both of you.”

Nobody spoke. Goyle was looking at the toast. It lay on Lyra’s plate, buttered to the edges, and Lyra wasn’t going to eat it. Goyle looked at it for some time, with her big soft heavy face. Then she looked down at her own plate, with two eggs on it, and bacon, and a sausage that she’d cut in half. Harry had sat in that Hall for five and a half years, and he had seen that girl eat through a Basilisk scare and a Dementor and the death of a Minister’s dog.

She put down her fork. She took hold of the plate, with one hand, and pushed it away.
