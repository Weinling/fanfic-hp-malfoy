# Interlude: The Wrong Night

There were two rings in Dudley’s second bedroom, and neither of them was any use.

One was on Harry’s right hand, where it had been since a cloister in May a year ago. It was plain silver and it had been plain silver for a month, and it would go on being plain silver, because the ring it answered to was in a biscuit tin at the bottom of his trunk, wrapped in tissue paper, and there was nobody wearing it to be looked for.

He knew exactly what colour that one was without opening the tin. Green. Safe. It had been green when she took it off.

He had read the note in the hospital wing with his leg in a bandage and Ron asking what Goyle wanted. Her best hand, the one she kept for Snape’s essays and for being right, and in the middle of the second line a gap the width of two fingers. She was not a person who left gaps. He had touched it with his wand because it was the only thing on the page that made no sense, and the parchment had come apart in his fingers into two, and inside, very small, was the thing he had spent a year looking for in the wrong place.

*I think his son is alive. Ask Winky whose seat she was minding at the World Cup.*

He had folded it shut again. Hermione had been sitting on the next bed with a book open on her knee that she was not reading, watching his wand, and his face, and the parchment.

“What is it?”

“Nothing,” he had said. “It’s from Malfoy. She’s finished it. That’s all.”

Hermione had not said anything. She had gone on looking at the wand.

It had reached him at half past nine in the morning. He had taken it to Dumbledore at six. *We were an hour too late*, Moody had told him, and Harry had nodded, and had not mentioned what he had been doing with the other eight hours, which was lying on his back in the hospital wing with a shortbread tin on his chest.

Nobody, in either of his lives, had ever sent him anything hidden. He lay on Dudley’s spare bed in the July heat, with the window open on the smell of cut grass and somebody’s barbecue, and thought about that, and then made himself stop.

He came back to Privet Drive because he had to. Whatever his mother had done still held, in his aunt’s blood and in this ugly house, and it held until he was seventeen and not a day after; Dumbledore had told him so in both lives, in very nearly the same words.

Uncle Vernon’s *Daily Prophet* came to the house because Harry paid for it, and Uncle Vernon put it in the bin unread every morning to make a point. Harry fished it out every evening. It was mostly Karkaroff, who had killed Barty Crouch for reasons of his own and was being hunted across Europe, and Dumbledore, who had frightened a hall full of children with an old man’s nonsense and ought to think about retiring; and on Sunday, at the bottom of page nine, under a piece about a Kneazle show, there were two lines.

*Muggle authorities in the village of Little Hangleton are investigating damage to a grave in the parish churchyard. Nothing is believed to have been taken.*

Harry read it, and put the paper down, and looked at the ceiling.

Somebody had taken a bone out of that grave. Harry could think of one reason to want it: the night of the Third Task, the blood, the body he had stood on a podium waiting to see. The ritual could have been done anywhere. Not the house on the hill, not the yew, not the gravestone with the name on it. Somewhere he had never seen in either life.

He did the count instead. He did it most nights, as other people counted sheep.

The diary, two summers ago, with a fang out of the Chamber. The locket, in a cave, last August. The diadem, on the first night of that term, in a room on the seventh floor full of other people’s junk, with the same fang; he had gone up alone at three in the morning, and asked the wall for the place where things were hidden, and found it where he had known it would be, on the head of an ugly stone bust; and he had put the pieces back afterwards, where nobody would look, and told Dumbledore at breakfast, and Dumbledore had said nothing for so long that Harry had eaten two pieces of toast. Three, then.

The ring, in a shack in Little Hangleton, under a floorboard. He knew what it did to the hand that put it on; he had watched it blacken one in the other life. He had told Dumbledore, and Dumbledore had gone to look, and come back with a silver probe blackened to the handle. It had not even reached the ring. A fang would destroy the soul inside it; it would not make the protections around it safe to cross.

*Not that one*, Dumbledore had said. *Not until I can take it out without bringing the curse with it.* That had been the summer before last. Harry had asked again at Christmas, and again in May. Dumbledore was still working on it.

The cup, in a vault at Gringotts, in a family Harry could not get into.

The snake.

And the one he did not count.

Three left, then, and one more. He had them all by heart. He had the shape of the year coming by heart as well, and the date the Dementors would come down Magnolia Crescent, and the hour. The second of August. A little after half past eight. He had been waiting for it since the train.

He would be in the right place, with his wand. He would get Dudley home. He knew what the letter from the Ministry would say before it was written.

It was the one thing, this summer, that he still knew.

On the thirty-first of July he was fifteen, and nobody at Number Four mentioned it, which was traditional.

Two owls came in the morning, to the window of the second bedroom, while Aunt Petunia was hoovering the landing: Errol, with a letter from Ron that was mostly about the Chudley Cannons and said *Hermione says* four times, and a squashed slab of Mrs Weasley’s fudge, and a smart little brown owl from Hermione with a book about Defensive magical theory and a card that said *Don’t do anything stupid, I mean it*. Nothing came from Wiltshire. He had not expected anything to come from Wiltshire. He looked at the window for some time after the brown owl had gone, all the same.

There was a road atlas in the second bedroom, one of Dudley’s, with a coffee ring on Hampshire. It was seventy-four miles from Privet Drive to the place in Wiltshire where the page went green and nothing was marked. He knew that because he had measured it, with the edge of an envelope. On a Firebolt, in still air, it was about forty minutes. Against the wind it was nearer an hour. He had not asked himself why he knew what it was against the wind.

Some days he wanted to go there and break something. He had a speech. He had several; he had spent most of July on them, lying on the bed with his shoes on, and they were very good. They began with *Never own a Malfoy*, in her voice, in his head, and what he had to say about people who stood in the straw with their chins level and told you so. And all of them came out in the same place, with him hearing himself say *Who came?*, and watching what it did to her face. He had known just where it was. She had given it to him herself, at midnight, when she was eleven. He had looked for the worst thing he owned, and it had been there, ready, like a wand in the hand.

And he had been the one who came. That was the joke. He had very nearly told her.

On the other days he only wanted to know whether she had had any breakfast.

*It was always going to be them.* He had known that. She was quite right. Everybody had somebody who came first; it was how people worked, and Harry had always rather assumed that it was the sort of thing that happened to other people, like Christmas.

In the evening he went out, because the house was full of the television and Uncle Vernon was in a temper about the price of petrol, and walked to the park at the end of Magnolia Crescent, and sat on the one swing the gang had not broken, and thought about the second of August.

He had sat on this swing at ten. He knew he had, the way you know the date of a battle. He could remember the boy’s shoes, which had been Dudley’s, and that the chain had pinched. He could not remember what it had been like to be him. There was less of that every year, and he had stopped mentioning it to himself.

It was very warm. The streetlamps had come on, orange, along the edge of the park. Somewhere a dog was barking. Two days, and then the alleyway, and the cold, and Dudley on his knees on the tarmac; he had been through it so often that it felt less like a thing that would happen than a thing he had to remember to do, like a dentist’s appointment.

The dog stopped barking.

Harry noticed it as you notice a clock that has stopped: not the silence, but the place where the noise had been. Then the streetlamp nearest the swings went out. Then the next. The warm air went out of the evening as if somebody had opened a door on to January, and his breath went white.

*No*, he thought, quite clearly. *Not tonight. It’s Monday.*

He was off the swing, and his wand was in his hand. He did not remember drawing it. That had been happening for years: his hand knew things, and told him afterwards. Across the grass, at the mouth of the alley that ran down to Wisteria Walk, somebody was shouting, and then not shouting, and then making a high, thin noise that Harry had never heard come out of his cousin in either life.

He ran.

Dudley was on his knees at the end of the alley with his arms over his head. Over him, tall and hooded and patient, two shapes that blotted out the lamplight, bending; and as Harry came down the alley the cold came up to meet him like water, and with it the voices, the old ones, a woman screaming and a high cold laugh, and under them, newer, a girl’s voice saying *that was the last one*, quite steadily, from somewhere very close.

There was no need to go looking for a happy memory. He had one sitting at the front of his head and had been trying for a month not to take it out.

A tent. A canvas roof, and a dragon somewhere on the other side of it, roaring, and a hand he could not see laid over his on the edge of a bench.

“*Expecto Patronum!*”

The fox came out of his wand at a run, silver and low and furious, and went straight through the nearer Dementor as if it were smoke, and turned on the second, and the second went up and back and away over the rooftops like smoke going up a chimney. The cold went with them. The lamps came on along the alley, one by one, as though nothing had happened.

The fox came trotting back. It sat down in front of him on the tarmac, very upright, with its brush round its feet and its chin up, and looked at him as it always looked at him afterwards: as if it had done something rather fine and was waiting for him to say so.

“Yeah,” said Harry. His voice was wrong. “I know. Thanks.”

It went on looking at him. It had her chin.

“Lyra,” he said, to nobody, and the fox was gone.

Dudley was being sick against the wall.

Harry got him up. It took a while; Dudley was heavier than he looked, and wetter, and he kept trying to sit down again. Harry got an arm under him and walked him back along Magnolia Crescent under the orange lamps, with Dudley’s weight on his shoulder and Dudley’s breath going in great shuddering gulps beside his ear, and all the way he thought about the date.

The Ministry’s letter came at half past nine, by a screech owl that nearly took Aunt Petunia’s eye out, and said everything he had known it would say. Dumbledore’s came twenty minutes later, and said *stay in the house*, and *you will be collected*, and nothing whatever about how the Dementors had known to come on the wrong night. Harry read both at the kitchen table with Uncle Vernon shouting at him from the doorway and Dudley grey and silent on the sofa with a bar of chocolate Harry had given him, eating it very slowly, as if he had forgotten what it was for.

They came for him the next night, a little after eleven, and the first thing through the front door was a wooden leg.

Moody stumped into the hall of Number Four with his staff in one hand and his wand in the other, and his magical eye went round the hall and up the stairs and through the kitchen wall to where the Dursleys were, by the sound of it, all three trying to get behind the fridge. Behind him, in the doorway, filling it, was a man in a long coat with his hair cut short and a face that had been eating properly for a year. Sirius looked at Harry across the hall and grinned, and it took ten years off him and put them straight back on again.

“Happy birthday,” said Sirius. “Sorry it’s late. There was a committee.”

They went by broom, over the rooftops of Surrey and the long dark spread of London, with Moody at the back watching everything at once, and came down in a square of tall shabby houses where a door that had not been there a moment ago came up out of the pavement between Number Eleven and Number Thirteen like somebody shouldering through a crowd.

In the hall, while Sirius was getting the door shut and a portrait somewhere in the dark was waking up and beginning to scream, Harry said to Moody, “Can I have my map back?”

The blue eye came round to him.

“No,” said Moody. “It’s the Order’s now. You lent it to me, Potter. I’m keeping it lent.” He considered. “You can borrow it, if Albus says. Ask him.”

Harry had known it would be no. He had asked so that it should be said, out loud, in a hall, and be finished.

Twelve Grimmauld Place was as he remembered it: dark and narrow and smelling of damp and old money, with the elf-heads on the stairs and the curtains over the portrait of Sirius’s mother, who called him a blood-traitor’s whelp and a filthy half-breed’s brat in a voice that went through the walls until Sirius put his fist through the air in front of her and she stopped. It was not as he remembered it in one way. There were no Weasleys yet, and no Lupin, and nobody had begun to clean it, and Sirius had been alone in it since Christmas.

“I go out,” said Sirius, in the kitchen, when Harry asked. He was making tea, badly. “I’m allowed to go out. That’s the joke. Nobody’s hunting me. I can walk down Diagon Alley in broad daylight if I like, and every second witch crosses the road, because I was in Azkaban twelve years for murdering thirteen people and nobody has ever stood up and said I didn’t.” He put the pot down too hard. “I could clear my name in an afternoon, you know. The proof’s about four feet tall and smells of rat, and it’s in Azkaban.”

Harry knew. Dumbledore had told him last summer, in his office, with Fawkes asleep on the perch: that Fudge had looked at the rat-faced little man for a long time, and then sent him to Azkaban under no name at all, rather than stand up in front of the country and say that the Ministry had locked away the wrong man for twelve years. Sirius was free of the hunt because Fudge was not a cruel man. He was not cleared because Fudge was a frightened one.

“One day.”

“One day,” said Sirius, and gave him a mug of tea with the bag still in it, and sat down across the table and looked at him, pleased, as though Harry were a thing he had been allowed to keep.

Harry drank his tea, and looked back, and did the other count. He had not wanted to. It came anyway.

June. Ten months. A room full of arches, and a veil that moved without any wind.

He knew the date. He had known it since he was eleven. He had always meant, when he got to it, to do something about it; and he had always had a year at least, and the board under his hands, and a good idea of where every piece would be. He looked at his godfather across the kitchen table, laughing at something Moody had said about the portrait, and thought: *I knew the date of the Dementors as well.*

The Order met in the kitchen most nights, with the door shut and Harry on the wrong side of it, and on the third night he came down the stairs for a glass of water at the wrong moment and met Snape coming up.

They stopped on the same step, in the dark, with the elf-heads looking down. Snape had his cloak over his arm. In the light from the kitchen door his face was very tired and very yellow, and he looked at Harry the way he had looked at him since a winter when Harry was eleven: as if Harry were a letter he had not been able to make himself open.

Harry looked back.

It was not as it had been in the other life. He knew what Snape was. He had been told once already, on a dusty floor, with a man dying beside him and a great deal of silver coming out of his eyes, and he had carried it a long way since. He knew what the arm under that sleeve had cost, and what it had been for, and whose eyes it had been for. He knew how it would end, if nobody did anything, and that he did not know, any more, whether anything he did would make a difference to it.

He did not hate him. He had stopped, in this life, a long time ago, and Snape had never worked out when.

“Potter,” said Snape.

“Sir.”

Snape went on looking for a moment longer. Then he went on up the stairs, past him, without a word, and Harry heard him stop on the landing above, and stand there, before he went on.

The hearing was on the twelfth. He was cleared, by a show of hands, with Dumbledore beside him and Fudge looking at the wall, and it was exactly as he had known it would be, and it made him feel no better whatever.

The Weasleys came on the fourteenth, in a great noise in the hall, with Ron grown another inch and Fred and George with an explosion already arranged for the drawing room, and Hermione last of all, with a trunk and a cat and the expression from the hospital wing.

She asked him nothing. She hugged him, and said he looked thin, and went to help Mrs Weasley with the curtains; and at dinner, across the long table, when he looked up from his plate, she was looking at him. She went on doing it. Not all the time. Just whenever he had forgotten that she might.

He stopped waiting for Dumbledore on the sixteenth.

He told Sirius in the drawing room, while the twins were upstairs blowing something up and Mrs Weasley was shouting at them about it: the shack, the ring, the blackened probe, the curse that killed by inches. Sirius listened with his chin on his fist.

“We can look,” he said. “We don’t touch a thing. If the protection’s still there, we come back.” Then, before Harry could ask, “When?”

Harry had to look at the fireplace.

They went that night. Sirius took his arm in the back yard, among the bins, and turned on the spot, and the world went small and black and squeezed, and came out again as a dark lane between hedges, with the smell of cows and wet grass and, a little way off, below them, the lights of a village and the square tower of a church.

“There,” said Harry, and pointed up the slope, away from the village, to where a black mass of trees came down to the lane.

The shack was where he had seen it in a memory that was not his, in an office that was not his either, in the other life, a year further on than this one: crouched under the trees with nettles to its windowsills and moss on its roof, and a dead snake nailed to the door. Sirius looked at the snake, and at Harry, and changed without a word, and sat down in the lane beside the gate, a big black dog with his ears up and his eyes on the dark.

Harry lit his wand and tested the threshold, then the boards, as Dumbledore had taught him. Nothing answered. He checked again before he went in.

It was one room, low and filthy, smelling of rot and mice. He went straight across to the hearth, to the place on the left where the boards were shorter than the others, and crouched; and he had his hand out before he saw that he did not need it.

The board was up. It lay to one side, on the floor, the right way up, as somebody had set it down who had not cared whether anybody knew. Underneath it there was a hollow in the earth the size of two fists, and in the hollow there was nothing.

He knelt there with his wand lit and looked at it for some time.

Then he made himself look at the rest, as Moody would have, as Malfoy would have; and saw what he had walked straight past. The dust on the floor had been disturbed, and not lately: there was a fine grey skin over the marks, a few weeks thick. A man had come in, and gone straight to the hearth, as Harry had, and gone straight out. And on the threshold, where the door scraped the boards, there was a little dry crust of earth that did not belong in the lane outside, which was mud. It was paler, and finer, and there were bits of something white in it.

He thought of the churchyard below them, and the two lines in the paper. The earth might have come from anywhere. But somebody had wanted a bone from that village, and somebody had come here, gone straight to the hiding place and got past protections Dumbledore had not yet lifted.

It could have been the same journey. The same man, or one of the same men, sent for two things instead of one.

He had known where the ring was for three and a half years. He had left the curse to Dumbledore and counted the ring as something they could still get whenever they were ready. He had not imagined Voldemort would move it before they got there.

The wand-light shook. He put his other hand round his wrist and held it until it stopped.

There was a place in his head he did not go.

He did not go there now, either. But kneeling on that floor in the dark, with the empty hollow under his light and the dog at the gate and the whole of the year in front of him gone blank, he came nearer the edge of it than he had in a long time: near enough to feel the cold coming off it, and smell the leaves, and see, for no more than a breath, a light, green, very bright, very close, coming towards him through trees.

And after it, nothing. There had never been anything after it.

Outside, in the lane, the dog whined once, low.
