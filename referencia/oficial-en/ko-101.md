The warm, peaceful days that had stretched on since spring were like a candle burning down to nothing. Watched closely, they seemed to pass with agonizing slowness—yet glance away for a moment and they were gone, melted without a trace, leaving only a faint fragrance to ghost across the senses. Happy days, every one of them.

But good days always end too soon. Happiness was a brutal coachman who whipped time into a gallop. Before she could catch herself, the holiday that had seemed so distant came crashing to the door.

Harry Potter and Carina Malfoy. Their last holiday had begun.

Harry had to return to Privet Drive. The thirty-first of July. He needed to spend at least a week there to renew the protective magic that would hold only until he came of age. The moment the holiday began, Aurors dispatched by the Ministry of Magic arrived at Hogwarts in person. Britain's finest, faces drawn tight with tension, formed a close escort around him.

Or perhaps they were there to escort the world from Harry. Either way.

Carina watched him recede under that heavy guard and made herself a promise.

She would find a way. She would.

Nurmengard was a small tower wedged into the cleft of a cliff.

It had clearly been far grander once. The foundations spread across the entire face of the rock, and at the base lay rubble heaped like a mountain—enough to suggest an original scale that could no longer be imagined. But now everything had crumbled away, leaving only a desolate cliff and a single tower repurposed as a prison.

At the top of that tower, Gellert Grindelwald was confined.

The old brickwork looked ready to collapse at any moment. Every gust of wind sent dust spiralling from the cracks. Whatever dignity ancient rulers had so desperately craved was clearly no match for time. The symbol of authority that had once imprisoned countless wizards who dared oppose them was, by any honest measure, a ruin.

"You have one hour."

Wolfram Schwardt, an official from the Austrian Ministry of Magic's Department of International Cooperation, said this as he turned the key in the lock of the wooden door. The hinges, long unexercised, gave a dry, grating shriek.

He pocketed the key and pushed the door open.

"The stairs go straight up—you won't need a guide. I'll wait here."

"For a prison holding an infamous Dark witch, there doesn't seem to be much in the way of surveillance."

Wolfram smiled with easy warmth.

"She isn't restrained in any particular way. She has no interest in leaving the tower to begin with. A house-elf brings her meals every day and cleans the room once a week. But in all our observation, over fifty years of it, we have never once caught her attempting anything resembling an escape."

Carina pointed out the flaw with cool precision.

"Assuming that yesterday's peace will hold today is complacency."

"Perhaps. But even Grindelwald is human. She is old. Even the late Dumbledore could not cheat age—so what of her, locked in this tower for more than fifty years? Grindelwald has had no opportunity to outrun time, no magic to disguise her years. She never had a friend like Nicolas Flamel."

Carina listened to Wolfram's explanation with a growing sense of puzzlement. He was speaking as though Dumbledore had died of old age. But Dumbledore had been fighting fiercely right up until the end—no one but Voldemort had ever been able to stop him. He had died only because he was caught off guard. She corrected him.

"Albus Dumbledore died fighting Voldemort. As far as I know, there was never any suggestion that he was ailing from old age."

Wolfram burst out laughing—the laugh of a man who has just heard a charming joke. But Carina had meant every word, and she let her displeasure show at being treated as though she'd said something absurd.

He waved a hand in quick apology.

"Ha. Miss Malfoy, was it? You've only ever known the old Dumbledore. I don't think you quite understand."

The old Dumbledore. When Carina had first arrived at Hogwarts, he had certainly been elderly—but he had moved under his own power and carried himself with vigour. To her, Dumbledore had always been the sort of ancient wizard who belonged in old stories.

Evidently, that was not how Wolfram saw him.

"If Dumbledore had been young, do you think that man could ever have walked free? However strong and ruthless he was, Dumbledore would have found a way to impose his will. Though—ah, he did succeed in the end, didn't he? It was Dumbledore who brought him down, after all. Oh. The time—"

Wolfram glanced sharply at his watch.

"Good heavens. Five minutes gone already… We may run out of time at this rate. You should head up."

He gave her a nudge, and Carina found herself stepping into Nurmengard before she quite intended to. The door swung shut behind her with a heavy clang. The silent prison—empty of all prisoners—received her with nothing but stillness.

She walked down a corridor lined on both sides with iron-barred doors and let her thoughts drift.

The young Dumbledore.

He had already been a white-bearded old man when she was ten years old and first set foot in Hogwarts, and he had not changed in the slightest over the six years she had known him. She found it nearly impossible to picture him young. He seemed like someone who might have been born with a beard already growing.

But Dumbledore had been human, which meant he had come into the world as a newborn, like anyone else. Unless he had leapt directly from infancy to old age, there must have been something in between.

What—or who—had Dumbledore been, back then?

Lost in thought, she climbed the stairs until the walls narrowed and the ceiling tapered to a point. She had reached the top floor. Carina paused before the door and steadied her breathing.

Grindelwald would be on the other side. She had no idea how the woman would react to her—but she needed to extract from her the means to save Harry.

She composed her expression and knocked. From within, a voice answered as though it had been waiting.

"Come in, child."

She opened the door onto a spare, unadorned room that could be taken in at a single glance. A square, hard-looking bed. A single desk. A straight-backed wooden chair, paper, and a pen. A meagre room that would make anyone nod in recognition if told it was a prison cell.

Yet for all its austerity, the space was saturated with the texture of a life lived. Every corner bore evidence of someone who had worked, over many years, to make this place habitable. A gentle warmth suffused the air, and clean sunlight fell through a well-polished window—warmer, somehow, than the pale light Carina had seen outside Nurmengard's walls.

And there, reclining against the headboard with a book in hand, was an elderly witch.

Gellert Grindelwald.

The Dark witch who had plunged the world into chaos before Voldemort—the Dark witch who had come before. An aged woman with silver-streaked hair and quiet lines across her face, who beckoned to Carina with one hand.

"A visitor, after so long. Well then, young witch. What is it you've come to seek from me?"

Only then did Carina understand what Professor Slughorn had meant when he said she was closer to Dumbledore than to Voldemort.

This was a Dark witch? She looked exactly like the witches in fairy tales—the kind who appeared to those who had lost their way and showed them the path forward. The kind who turned pumpkins into carriages and mice into coachmen, who offered remedies in exchange for a voice.

There was a peculiar ease about her, the ease of someone who had spent a lifetime accumulating knowledge. Carina had the distinct impression that if she bowed her head and asked, the answer would be given.

But that was no reason for Carina to bow.

She flicked her wand. The wooden chair by the desk walked briskly across the room on its four legs and stopped near the bed. Carina crossed the centre of the room with the unhurried grace of an actress crossing a stage, came to stand beside the chair, gathered her skirt slightly, and sat.
Carina Malfoy had faced Dumbledore himself without flinching. A Malfoy did not lower her head before someone who had already lost.

This was a negotiation. An equal one—or rather, one in which Carina held the overwhelming advantage.

She addressed Grindelwald with cool directness.

"I've come for your knowledge. Teach me how to separate a soul."

Grindelwald met her gaze with deep, unhurried eyes. She smiled at the impertinent young stranger, set her book aside, and turned to face her. A low, soft voice emerged from dry lips.

"Knowledge always comes at a price. Have you prepared one?"

"I don't know who you are or what you want. How could I have prepared anything?"

Even as she said it, Carina drew a folded paper from inside her robes.

"That said—when you visit someone's room, you ought to bring a gift. I've brought news I thought you might find interesting. Perhaps you'd care to look."

Grindelwald took the paper and unfolded it slowly. The carefully creased parchment contained the eulogy Slughorn had written for Dumbledore—a piece that traced the arc of Albus Dumbledore's life and achievements, sang of his glorious end, and mourned his passing.

Grindelwald ran a thin finger across the parchment.

"Ah. Albus. Our flawed hero. So you died a hero's death, in the end."

Longing surfaced in her eyes. Carina recalled that the two had shared something singular, and found herself wondering whether that bond had still been alive even on the day Dumbledore defeated her.

A prison—though comfortable enough compared to Azkaban. Dumbledore, who had imprisoned her here and yet had clearly taken care in doing so.

And Grindelwald, who had made no attempt to escape despite the absence of any real surveillance—she who had once burned with ambition.

This strange arrangement was difficult to explain without some extraordinary bond between them. And the nature of that bond was probably—

Grindelwald spoke at last.

"Who killed him?"

The plan to bring news of Dumbledore appeared to be working. Carina smiled with easy confidence and raised one hand in a slight, questioning gesture—as if to say: your move. Whatever Grindelwald wanted to know now, only Carina could answer.

Which meant the advantage was firmly in Carina's hands.

"Curious?"

She smiled with perfect self-assurance. Their eyes met. A brief silence passed, and then Grindelwald gave a short laugh.

"You want to trade knowledge for knowledge. Was that your plan from the beginning?"

"I'm adapting to your terms. If this hadn't suited you, I had other forms of payment ready."

"Fair enough. But a trade ought to be equal, don't you think? I've already told you what I want. Shouldn't you tell me what you want? How else can I set a value on it?"

"I can't do that. What I want to know is precisely what will answer your question."

"…You are a remarkably clever child."

The one who had killed Dumbledore was Harry—his body under the direction of Voldemort's soul. And the means to sever that soul's hold was the knowledge Carina needed. She could not obtain what she wanted without explaining the circumstances, which meant she had to make Grindelwald impatient first.

Her plan had worked.

Curiosity kindled in Grindelwald's eyes.

"You don't seem to be lying. Very well. Tell me everything. In return, I give you my word—I will answer every question to the full extent of my knowledge."

She did not bother demanding an oath. That would have been beneath her.

Carina began, carefully and methodically, to tell the long story of Dumbledore and Voldemort and Harry Potter.

"Ah."

When the last word had been spoken, Grindelwald let out a long, slow breath.

"Albus. Albus. So that is how it ended for you."

The thin, trembling voice was impossible to read—joy or grief, it could have been either. But she was mourning him, that much was certain. She covered her face with a weathered hand and recited her own private elegy.

"You imprisoned me here, and imprisoned yourself in duty. Which of us was truly in a cage? Nurmengard, or your own convictions? Within these walls that bound my body, my mind was free. I watched the world change and let my thoughts roam where they would. I could examine what I had done and what I had done wrong. I could revise my own judgement of myself. But you—"

There was no way to know exactly what Grindelwald meant. There must have been some history stretching back long before Carina's time, some thread of events she had no access to.

She had not lived through those years. She had no way to understand. She simply listened as Grindelwald's eulogy ran its course.

"Your body was free, but your mind was always in chains. You faded, in the end, alone in your solitude."

A small sigh, and something that might have been a trace of relief. With that, Grindelwald brought her words to a close.

"Farewell, my dear friend."

Silence. A moment of stillness. Grindelwald bowed her head and held it there.

Interrupting a moment of silence was a discourtesy—but Carina had very little time left. She cleared her throat and drew Grindelwald's attention back.

"Excuse me. I think it's time we—"

"You mean the method for tearing the soul that has attached itself to Harry Potter?"

Carina nodded. Grindelwald raised her head slowly. For some reason she could not quite name, Grindelwald looked younger now—more alive—than she had a few minutes ago.

Grindelwald smiled with absolute confidence.

"There is a way."

"There's a way?"

Carina heard the brightness in her own voice and snapped her mouth shut, startled.
She had promised herself she would show nothing. And yet the moment those words landed, a flash of unguarded joy had slipped through before she could contain it. Grindelwald had clearly noticed.

In any negotiation, the more desperate party always comes off worse. What if Grindelwald had caught the desperation in her and was about to renegotiate? Carina felt a flicker of unease and closed her fingers around the small vial inside her robes. If it came to it, she would use Veritaserum—even if that meant subduing an old woman and forcing it down her throat.

"And what's more, you already know the method."

But Grindelwald's next words hit Carina like a blow to the head. She stared blankly.

"I… already know the method?"

"Of course. The answer is already inside you. Think a little, and you'll find it."

"That's impossible. Even Dumbledore couldn't work it out. How could I possibly—"

A burst of laughter cut her off.

Grindelwald erupted into full, unrestrained laughter—the kind that shook the bed as she slapped her palm against it, as though Carina had just delivered the most delightful joke she had heard in years.

"Ha ha ha! Dumbledore didn't know? Impossible—out of the question! Dumbledore knows. Everything. All of it! He simply couldn't tell you!"

"What do you mean? He explained everything before he died. I told you—he said there was no way, that Harry would have to—"

"Yes! The man who kept a phoenix as a familiar and borrowed, however briefly, the power to overcome death itself! Do you really think he couldn't tear out a single grafted soul?"

Surely not. However cryptic the old man had been, he would not have withheld the method for separating a soul in his final moments.

But Grindelwald had spent long years with a Dumbledore Carina had never known. She probably understood him many times better than Carina ever could.

And if that were true? If that infuriatingly oblique old man had kept one last truth hidden, even as he died?

"He couldn't bring himself to say it. That is who Albus is. He does not judge. He does not presume. He is stronger and wiser than anyone alive, but he could never accept that about himself. He would never think of controlling others, and he could never permit that kind of method—not from himself! But the method exists nonetheless!"

The Grindelwald before her now seemed to have shed decades. Silver-streaked hair swept around her as she moved, and a fierce, undeniable charisma filled the room. She fixed Carina with a gaze from two differently coloured eyes, her smile blazing with certainty.

"Think of the darkest knowledge you possess. Among it, find the thing that requires the word soul to explain. That is the answer you've been looking for!"

The darkest knowledge. The curses she had briefly encountered when she entered Voldemort's service. The serpent. The poison. The Dark Mark. Horcruxes. Ravenclaw's artefact—

Soul. The dark magic that tore a soul apart and sealed the pieces inside objects.

Could it be—

"…A Horcrux?"

"Correct, young witch!"

Carina turned her head slowly.

There she was—the witch who had once captivated everyone she met and bent them to her will, dazzlingly beautiful even now. The Dark witch who had sought to tear down the boundary between the Muggle and wizarding worlds through war. Gellert Grindelwald, laughing with wild, triumphant joy.

"Dumbledore could not say it. He was imprisoned by his duty. As Headmaster, he could not counsel any of you to commit murder—it would have been a violation of every moral principle he held! But the fact that he would not say it does not make the method disappear. You already know it! Perform the ritual, take up your wand. With the killing curse that has come down from the most ancient times, take a human life! When you do, the bond of a soul that should be whole is weakened!"

Darkness settled over Carina. Grindelwald wrapped an arm around her shoulders, smiling with a brilliance that was wicked and yet so compelling it was impossible to pull away—the same darkness that had driven countless people to ruin, now whispering directly into Carina's ear.

"Tear away a fragment of the soul and bind it to an object you hold dear. Seal it with the incantation and set it aside as a safeguard against your own death. That is a Horcrux. A shard of the soul."

The devil whispers. They cheat the scales sometimes—but at their core, they are fair. To save a life, they demand another life be placed upon the balance.

A perfectly reasonable and equitable exchange. But selfish human beings call it the devil's whisper and deny their own weakness by dismissing it.

"—Voldemort. To tear him free. Harry Potter must be made to kill someone. Though at your current level of ability… finding the means to create a Horcrux may prove difficult. I said I would answer anything, so if you wish to know, I will tell you."

Grindelwald pressed a wand into Carina's hand. Hawthorn, unicorn tail hair—a wand that slid into her grip as though it belonged there. A magical instrument capable of killing or obliterating with the simplest command.

Carina gripped it tightly. Her eyes were steady and resolved as she gave a single nod.

"Tell me."

Grindelwald smiled beautifully at the answer she had been waiting for.

Albus, you were wrong.

Human beings are foolish by nature. Stupid, and weak of will. They need a leader to guide them. Someone wiser and more capable, to make the right decisions on their behalf.

You had everything. An intellect that blazed like stars in the sky. The ambition to make the world better. The power to see it done. And the charisma to make others follow your vision. That was a gift without equal.

But, Albus. You lacked faith in yourself.

You refused to judge others? You believed you had no right to? Albus—why? Was it really because of that one day, your sister, that you lost yourself? Was one mistake enough to make you turn away from everything you could have been?

Do you think they'll thank you for watching and withholding judgement? Do you think they'll revere you for holding to the most moral path? No, Albus. They'll blame you. They'll curse you. They'll hold you responsible for every evil in the world. Rather than feel ashamed of their own ignorance, they'll be angry.

Why didn't you destroy the evil sooner, they'll say. Why didn't you tell us the whole truth.

The very effort you made to preserve their chance to find courage against darkness, their right to seek truth for themselves—even that they will diminish.

They'll reject tyranny, and yet demand the tyranny of the philosopher-king. In the end, they'll go exactly the way I always argued they would.

Yes. I was defeated. I won't make excuses. You were stronger, and you had the right to decide the shape of the world. I accepted that, without resistance.

But if you keep walking that road—if you insist on it—

A day will come.

Either you will die.

Or you will become another version of me.

Until that day arrives, I will watch from the top of the prison I built.

Farewell, Albus.
