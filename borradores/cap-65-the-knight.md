# Chapter 70: The Knight

“You are the only person I have ever met,” said Malfoy, “who can lose a game of chess by looking at it.”

It was the last Sunday in April, and the report was done. It had taken twenty minutes. There was nothing left in it but a good thing about a toothbrush, because her father had stopped asking for jokes, and she had sealed it without reading it over and gone to sleep for a quarter of an hour with her head on the arm of the chair. Then she had woken up in a better temper than he had seen in a month. It might have been the weather. The window of the little room had started showing them a blue evening with a star in it, and she had got out of the chair, and gone away, and come back up seven flights with a flat wooden box under her arm.

He knew the box. He had known it for a long time. He had his hands round a mug of the second-worst tea in Scotland, and he watched her pull the small table up to the fender and lift the lid.

“Your father’s,” said Harry.

“I’m aware of that. I have had enough of being in the same room as him; I don’t see why I shouldn’t have his chessmen. Sit up. You have been moping since breakfast. You can have the black, since it’s the side that likes you.”

He ought to have made some excuse.

They bowed to her as she set them out. They had always done that. They were old and rather chipped, black and silver, and a good deal ruder than they looked. When she had finished she sat down on the rug on the far side of the table, with her hair coming out of its pins and her knees drawn up. He looked at the row of small dark faces that had turned towards him; and the black king, who had a crack in his crown, gave him the smallest possible nod. He had been getting that nod from time to time for a year. He had got it in April last year, on the third floor, with the rain coming down. He had gone red then, and she had thought it was funny.

She moved her king’s pawn. He looked at the board with his chin on his fist.

It was a very nice evening. He had got through a week of it. He had been down to Snape’s office on Tuesday, and given him the dull thing, a good clean room with a bad table in it, and Snape had walked round it twice and told him that it was the first thing he had ever produced with the decency to be boring. He had found a place in the atlas that had no roads. It was going to be all right. He looked at his hand, going out across the board, and it wavered about, as it always did. It came down on a knight.

“Oh, *don’t*,” said Malfoy. She was smiling already. “Please. I’ve had a very trying month.”

He put it on the edge of the board, directly in front of its own pawn.

It went on as those games did. She took a bishop. He gave her a rook by accident, and she thanked him. She had started to look like somebody on a holiday; she had stuck a pin in her mouth, to have her hands free for her hair, and she talked round it. He thought it was the first time he had seen her eat anything without being told to, when she reached over without looking and took the last of the toast. By the tenth move he was in check. He looked at it for some time. There did not seem to be any way out. She had her queen across the whole of the back rank, and her rook coming up, and his king was standing in the middle of nothing with a pained expression. So he did the only thing he could think of. He took hold of a pawn, and moved it in front.

There was a small dry sigh.

It came from the black bishop. There was only one of them left, and it had been standing at the side of the board with its hands folded inside its sleeves. It had been standing at the sides of boards for two hundred years. It looked at the pawn. It looked at Harry, with a long, thin, chipped, unbearably weary face, and it shook its head.

“Not *there*,” said the bishop. “I told him. Not *there*, I said.”

He stopped with his hand in the air.

It had said that very quietly, to nobody. It was the sort of thing that it said. Across the board Malfoy had been reaching for her queen. Her fingers had got as far as the small silver crown. They stayed there.

He saw it happen. He would have given anything he had not to. It was exactly like watching the Snitch go past somebody’s ear. She did not move. She took the pin out of her mouth, slowly, and she laid it on the table, and she looked at the bishop. All the holiday had gone out of her face and there was nothing there that he could read, only a kind of stillness, with a great deal of attention behind it. He had seen that once, in the straw. She had looked at him like that with a list in her head.

“What did you say?” said Malfoy.

The bishop looked at its feet. The whole of the black side had gone very quiet.

“I beg your pardon, miss,” it said.

“You said that before.” It was hardly a voice at all. “I’ve heard you say that. You said it to me. I was standing over a desk. I was—” She stopped. He watched her eyes go to the pawn, standing in front of its king, a square from where any sane person would have put it. They went to the edge of the board, where a knight had been sitting for ten moves with its sword in its sheath, unable to reach a thing. They went slowly over the rest of the black pieces, one after another. Nobody had spoken a word of complaint. Nobody on that side of the board had complained in a year. The queen was gazing straight in front of her with an expression of long-suffering and affection, like an old nurse.

“They hate everybody,” said Malfoy. “They told my father he castled like a tradesman.”

He took his hand down off the board. It seemed to take a long time to get to his knee.

“Who taught you to play?” she said.

“Ron. You know that. You—”

“When?”

He could have lied. It went by him, the whole of it, in about a second. He had been lying to this person since the age of eleven, and he had become very good at it, and he had never wanted anything so much. *You could have lied*, somebody had once said to her, in a very ugly voice, up in a tower, with the owls going round. *Once more. That’s all.*

“That term,” said Harry. “The autumn of first year. I wasn’t any good. I’d only just—”

She had turned her head. She looked at the black king.

He was a very small figure. He had a crack in his crown, and he had seen a good many things. He looked up at her, and then, for a moment, as much as chessmen can look at somebody, at Harry; and he drew himself up.

“He asked us not to say, miss,” said the black king, with enormous dignity.

There was not a sound in the room. Even the fire seemed to have stopped.

She did not say anything for a long time. She sat on the bald rug with her knees drawn up and her hair half down, with one hand on a silver queen. All the colour had left her face. She was looking at him now, and it was not a look that he had ever seen. She seemed to be having some trouble with her breath. It came in little pulls; and he watched her go back, he could actually see her going, down a long way, through a good many doors, to the far end of something. The cold, and the one lamp. The half a sentence on the blackboard about moonstone. Every night, to the end of the holidays. She had been eleven years old.

“It was you,” said Lyra Malfoy.

He could not speak. He nodded.

“There was a *pawn*. On Boxing Day. It was the stupidest move I had ever seen. And the queen. You put the queen in the middle of the—that was *you*. You came down. You came down there in the middle of the night. Every night. You’d have had to come down from *Gryffindor Tower*, in the dark, in that—you had the cloak. You must have had it by then. You—” She had got to her knees. He did not think she knew. The table had gone over, with a crash, and there were black and silver pieces all over the rug, shrieking, and she took no notice of them at all. “You *sat in that chair!*”

“Yes.”

“You saw me!”

“I was in the door,” said Harry. His voice had gone somewhere. “The first night. I’d followed you from the Hall. I saw you doing the voices.”

She hit him. It was with her closed fist, on the chest, and it was a good deal harder than you would have thought. It knocked what was left of the air out of him. “*The Baron!*” said Malfoy. Her voice cracked in two. “I curtsied to the *Bloody Baron!* I got down on the dungeon stairs in my good robes and I *thanked* him! I have been thanking a dead man in *chains* for four and a half years, and you stood in front of that mirror, with your stupid ears, and you *knew what it was*. You knew what I was looking at. I was eleven years old, and I’d have given every Galleon I had, and the whole of my heart’s desire was you, *losing*—”

She hit him again. There were tears running down her face. She did not seem to have noticed them. They were simply coming out, and going in at the corners of her mouth, and she was laughing, or something was; he had never seen anything like it. It was as if somebody had taken down a wall.

“You asked me who came!”

He shut his eyes.

“In the *straw*. You stood there with your leg bleeding, and you looked me in the face, and you said it. *Who came?* And it was *you!* You *knew!* You had the whole of it in your hand, and you used it to—oh, you *vile*— I’ve got eight answers left to that, Potter. I have lain awake. I’ve said them to the *ceiling*.” She had got hold of his jumper, in both fists, and she shook him. “Nobody came. I told you that. I stood there and told you, *nobody came*. I’d written to him. Did you know that? I wrote to my father that Christmas. I’d never told a soul. I wrote him a letter, it took four tries, it only had two words in it, it said *Please come*. And he wrote back about the weather. That was the morning. That was the morning when you put the queen out, the Friday, it had come at breakfast, and I went down there with it in my pocket and I thought I would never— And there she was, in the middle of the board, the stupidest move in the history of chess. And I laughed. I stood there and I *laughed*. I was eleven, Potter, and there was nobody in that entire castle, and somebody had got up in the night to lose to me.”

He had not known about the letter.

It went in very slowly. He sat quite still and felt it go. *Please come.* He had stood in the straw. He had looked for the worst thing he owned.

“I didn’t know,” said Harry. “Lyra, I didn’t—”

“*Somebody came!*” said Lyra.

She let go of his jumper. She took his face in both her hands. They were wet and they were not at all steady. She held it, hard, and she looked at it, every part of it, from about six inches off, as if she had never been allowed to look at it before. He had seen her happy. He had thought he had. He had seen her with a Snitch, and he’d seen her on a hill, and in the chair with her forehead down, laughing about a D. He understood now that he had been looking at the outside of a house. Her whole face had come open. There was somebody in there, right at the back, with her chin up and her hair in a ribbon; and she was eleven, and she had been waiting by herself in the dark for a very long time.

“It wasn’t for anything,” she said. It had almost no voice in it. “Don’t you see? It wasn’t the other one. It wasn’t the one who knows everything, with his plans, and his boards, and what comes next. I’ve had him. He’s clever, and he’s a liar, and he’s put me about like furniture since I was twelve. That was *before*. That was the Christmas of first year. You told me. It was the January, in the corridor, that it all came back. You hadn’t got any of it, had you? You didn’t know who I was. You didn’t know a single thing. Nobody had told you I’d matter. You couldn’t play. You had Spellotape on your glasses and you were scared of the dark and you thought a Galleon was a kind of boat. You were just a horrid little boy who’d seen somebody by herself, and you got out of bed.”

She laughed. It broke in the middle. She laid her forehead against his.

“You weren’t anybody yet,” said Lyra. “You just *came*.”

She kissed him. She kissed him on the mouth, and on the cheekbone, and on the bridge of his glasses, crying and laughing, and then she put her arms round his neck and held on with all her strength. Her heart was going like a bird’s. She was saying something into his collar, over and over, and he could not make it out at first. It was *thank you*. It was very quiet and rather cross.

He put his arms round her. They went round her by themselves. Over her shoulder he could see the little room, the fire, the timetable, and the pieces picking themselves up off the rug; and quite near his knee a small black knight was getting to its feet and putting its helmet straight, and looking up at him.

There was something the matter with his chest.

It did not hurt. That was the odd thing. It was only cold. It was a small flat coldness, about the size of a hand, that had started somewhere behind his breastbone, while she was talking; and it was spreading out from there, without any hurry, the way that water finds its way across a floor.

*That was before.*

He smiled. He knew he had to. She had drawn back, to look at him, with her eyes swimming and her nose running and her whole face shining like a lamp, and she took him by the ears, and he smiled at her. He found the corners of his mouth and he lifted them. It was the hardest thing he had ever done. He had walked into a forest, once, and it had been easier than this.

“You’re crying,” said Lyra, delighted.

“Yeah,” said Harry.

He got to the bathroom at some time after one. He had stayed until she had to go. She had made him pick up every one of the pieces and say something civil to the bishop. She had kissed him on the stairs, in plain sight of a portrait, and she had called him something that he didn’t catch, and gone down seven flights two at a time, like somebody of about nine.

The dormitory was dark. He could hear Ron. He shut the bathroom door, and lit his wand, and stood in front of the row of basins, with his hands on the cold edge of the middle one, and he looked in the glass.

There was a thin boy of nearly sixteen in it, with bad hair and a scar.

He knew it had happened. There had been a doorway. He could have told you what the cloak had smelled like. He had been eleven, and he’d just learned to play; he had sat down in a chair, facing a blackboard, and said *Er*. He could tell you the words. He had told himself all that, on a good many nights, the way you know the date of a battle. He shut his eyes, and went down there. He went along the passage to the place, and stood in the door of the classroom, and he looked for the boy.

*Who was he?* Hermione had said. *Before.* She had been smiling at something. *I liked him. He was so pleased about everything.*

It was very quiet. There was a chair, with nobody in it. A lamp had been left burning. He stood there for some time, and he called, in a manner of speaking. He waited.

A few weeks later that boy had gone down a corridor behind a man in a turban, on a winter evening, with his scar beginning to split. Somebody had started to come in through it. Somebody seventeen years old, who had buried a good many people, and could do sums. It had taken him until Easter. He had looked out through that child’s eyes at the breakfast table, a little more of him every morning, until he knew how everyone was going to die. He had picked up his fork with that child’s hand. And he had gone on from there; he had used it all, the bed, the friends, the place at the table, and he had used her. He’d been so busy. It had not once occurred to him to ask what had happened to the person who had been there first.

She had been waiting for him for four and a half years. She’d held him just now as if she would never let go. She had thanked him. She was asleep under the lake with a smile on her face, and it was for somebody with Spellotape on his glasses, who had thought a Galleon was a kind of boat.

He opened his eyes.

He looked at the boy in the glass, and the boy looked at him. He was quite good at that look. He had learnt it on purpose. He had seen it do a lot of damage. He leaned a little closer, until his breath came and went on the silver; he looked into one green eye, and then the other, a long way in, as far as it went.

“Hello?” said Harry.

The tap dripped.

After a while he ran the water and cleaned his teeth.
