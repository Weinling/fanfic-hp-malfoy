# Chapter 99: Not Yet

Professor Dumbledore read the contract twice, as she would have wished, and the second time he took off his spectacles.

At a quarter to ten on the Saturday morning, with a low white sun on the snow and all the silver instruments going, Harry had come up the moving stair with four sheets of second-best paper inside his robes, and had put them on the desk without sitting down. The box was where he had left it in December. Somebody had dusted round it.

“This is her hand,” said Dumbledore. “To the eighth.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And the ninth is yours. She has struck it out, and you have written it again.” The long forefinger of the right hand rested beneath the place where the pencil had gone through. The left lay in his lap; the black had got as far as the knuckles. “I shouldn’t have had the courage of the first eight. I find that I recognise the ninth.”

He said it without any particular expression, and laid the sheet down.

“Is it her wish?”

“It’s her writing.”

“That was not what I asked you.”

“It’s her wish,” said Harry. “She’s done the figures. She did them without me.”

“And the reason. There is something that she can say only when that thing sleeps.” The blue eyes had come up, mild and tired, without the glass in front of them. “What will you do with it, Harry, when she has said it?”

He had known that this would come. He had got it ready on the stairs, as he got everything ready: the tired face, and the bit of shame. He looked for them now, and he found that he was not going to use either. He thought of a field in Wiltshire, and of a boy pouring tea.

“I don’t know yet,” said Harry.

That was a lie, and a poor one, and it lay on the desk between them beside a box of chocolates.

Dumbledore regarded it for a moment. Then he did what Harry had not seen him do in either life, and looked away first.

“I notice that I no longer ask you things,” he said. “I’ve been aware of it since the summer. I believe it’s because I have begun to be afraid of the answers; and that is a very poor condition for a headmaster to be in, and I can’t at present see my way out of it.” He drew the box towards him, and lifted the lid. “You will want one of these.”

He took it out himself. He used the little silver tongs from the bowl of lemon drops, and he chose from the corner, beside the empty paper with the brown mark on it; and he wrapped it in a twist of the yellow cellophane, with two turns, as neatly as a man who had been doing up sweets for a hundred years. He put it on the blotter. He opened a drawer, and set down beside it two small shrivelled brown stones.

“Two,” said Dumbledore. “People drop them.”

Harry had one already. He had changed it over that morning; it was Saturday, and it was in the left. He took the others, and the yellow twist, and he noticed that his hand was perfectly steady, and that the old man had noticed it as well.

“I make one condition. Professor Snape is not to hear of this beforehand. He would be obliged to prevent you.” He paused for no longer than it takes to choose a word. “I use the term exactly.”

“Yes, sir.”

“If she should die of it, I shall have killed two children with one box of sweets that were meant for me. I shall say so, to whoever is left to hear. I don’t offer that as a comfort. It’s merely that somebody ought to have said it before you went downstairs.”

He put on his spectacles again, and began to read a letter.

Harry got as far as the door. He had four sheets of paper against his chest and a thing in his pocket that had killed a girl in a little more than a minute. He looked back, once, at the long bent silver head. *Dumbledore had been so fond of him that he had brought him up, with great gentleness and every attention, for the purpose of being killed at the right moment.* He had written that on the floor of a bedroom, at fifteen, in his head. He had not expected to be handed the same thing across a desk for somebody else, and to have put out his own hand and taken it.

There were twenty-two in the box. He heard the lid go down behind him.

The shop was on the wrong side of Hogsmeade, between a cobbler’s and a place that sold second-hand cauldrons, and it had a board in the window.

He saw that first, leaning against the inside of the glass, where a card had been: a plain deal board, three feet long, done in green paint with a ruler, by somebody who had never painted a letter before and had not intended to be caught out. *GOYLE’S*. The apostrophe was exactly where it belonged.

The door was on the latch. It was twenty-five past two.

She was there already. She stood in the middle of a low dim room with a stone floor and a smell of mice and old apples, in her grey cloak, with her back to him and her head a little tilted, as if somebody had been pointing things out: a bricked-up flue, and a patch of cleaner flagstone, about four feet from the wall, where a counter would go. And by the door, where the light fell, somebody had lately set down a three-legged stool of the kind that is kept for old ladies.

He shut the door behind him. She did not turn round.

They had the lines by heart. He had learnt his in the bath, and at breakfast behind a propped-up Charms book, and on the road down with his lips moving; and at the stile he had burnt the fourth sheet with the end of his wand. There were eleven of them. He had not known, until he opened his mouth, that he would mean every one.

“Malfoy,” said Harry. “What are you doing here?”

“I thought I’d come and look at it.” It was the pleasant voice, and it was worn through. “She brought me in December. The oven was to go in that wall. There’s a yard at the back.”

“You shouldn’t be on your own.”

“I’m not. You’ve seen to that. I can’t so much as cross a village.” She had turned, at last. She was the colour of the snow on the sill, with the line running down from under her ear into the fur, and her eyes went over his face once, quickly, as they had gone over a sheet of headings. “You may as well stay, since you’re here. I’m tired, Potter. I’m so very tired.”

That was the sixth, from the fourth sheet, in her writing. He had said, when she showed it to him, that nobody would believe she’d say such a thing; and she had looked at him over the top of the Persian Wars and not troubled to answer.

“Then come back up to the castle,” said Harry. That was the seventh, and the feeblest thing on the sheet.

She took off her gloves. They were the kid ones. She laid them on the stool, and she put her right hand into her cloak.

The thing was about the size of a tooth. He had stirred it for a hundred and seventy-four days, on his knees, and dropped it into her bag without a word; and he watched her draw the cork with her thumbnail, in the grey light, and tip back her head, and drink the whole of it in one swallow, like medicine.

None of that was in the lines. It needed no words.

Nothing happened for a moment. Then he saw it reach her. He had drunk that once himself, in another life, and he knew what it did: it came up through the feet, like going into warm water. Her shoulders came down by a quarter of an inch. She breathed in. She looked about the room, slowly, with her lips a little parted, as he had seen her look at a chessboard when the whole of it had suddenly come clear; and then, for no reason that anybody could have given, she went across and moved the stool six inches to the left, out of the draught from the door, and sat down on it.

She held out her hand.

He gave her the yellow twist. He gave her, with it, what she had told him to bring: a pleated paper case, and her own small silver fruit knife, which she had sent up to the seventh floor in a glove. She undid the cellophane on her knee. It lay there in its paper, dark and round, with a curl on the top, and a bit of a shine on it still. It smelt of chocolate, and under that, of an orange on a cold morning.

“She kept one for me,” said Lyra. That was the eighth. “Did you know? She held it out. I didn’t take it.”

Her voice had cracked right across, which was not on the sheet. She had written that line on Friday for a thing on her arm to hear, and thought it rather good.

She cut it in three. She did that as she did everything, with her tongue between her teeth, and it took her a long time, because of her hands; and she looked at the three pieces, and chose the smallest. He saw her raise it. And he saw her stop, with her head on one side, exactly as though somebody had touched her on the elbow; and she put it down again, and cut that piece in half.

His heart turned over. Too little. She had done the sum for a third.

He could not say so. He had a line.

“Lyra, don’t,” said Harry. “Give me that.”

He had thought that the ninth would be the hard one to say, and it came out of him as though it had been waiting since December.

“Hold my wrist,” said Lyra.

That was not anywhere. He went down on his knees on the stone, beside the stool, and she pushed back her left cuff; and there it lay on the thin blue skin, grey-green, in two slow curves, with the small flat head on the pulse and the eye wide open. He put his thumb on it, for the first time since a bathroom. She was looking at him. She was not looking at anything else.

She put her other hand into her cloak, and brought out something that she closed his fingers on: a small stoppered bottle, cold, with a thing in it like a thread of smoke, and a slip of paper round the neck. He read that sideways, by the window. *Christmas morning, 1991. Somebody gave me a queen. Keep it out of the house.*

Then she picked up the sixth part of a chocolate between her finger and thumb.

“It’s a nice one,” said Lyra, and put it in her mouth.

He had one line left, and it was *No*. He heard himself say it.

After that there was nothing written down.

She chewed it slowly, with her eyes shut. He felt her swallow; it went down the whole of her arm. For perhaps the length of a breath she sat quite still on the stool, with the snow-light on one side of her face, and her pulse went under his thumb as it always had, light and rather quick.

“Oh,” said Lyra. “That’s orange.”

He would have given a great deal not to have heard that. He did not know why. It had the sound of something that had been said before.

It began at the feet. She told him so. She was allowed to tell him that; it was what a girl would say. *My feet have gone. That’s funny.* She said that it was cold in here. A minute afterwards she said that her hands had pins and needles, and he saw the right one open and shut on her knee. He had her wrist. He could feel it start to hurry. He had the brown stone in his other fist, down by his side, held so hard that it had printed itself on his palm, and there was a clock somewhere; there was a church, at the top of the High Street, and it had struck the half while she was cutting.

He counted, since nothing else was permitted him.

Three minutes. The eye was open. It lay under the ball of his thumb, the colour of weak tea, perfectly round, looking at nothing. Her breath had begun to come short. She was still upright. She had her lips pressed together and her chin up, and a line had come between her brows, small and fixed, as though she were listening for a noise in another room.

Four. Her pulse stumbled. It caught itself, and went on, faster. She swayed, and he got his shoulder under her, and she came down off the stool an inch at a time, into the angle of his arm, with her cheek against his collar and her legs anyhow on the flags. She weighed nothing whatever. He could feel her heart going through two cloaks.

The eye was open.

*She cut it in half.* He had that going round with the count. A third; she had done the figures for a third, with the label, on a window-seat, and at the last moment something had told her to take a sixth, and she’d listened to it, because that was what the stuff was for. It did not promise anything. He had read that as well. It told you which foot. And a sixth might be a thing that made a girl ill on the floor of a shop for twenty minutes and got better, with the ear wide open the whole time, and everything spent: the sweet, and the gold, and the key that Crabbe had left on a table.

Five minutes. Six.

She had started to shake: not a shiver; it came from the middle of her, in long hard pulls, and her teeth had begun to go. Her eyes were open and fixed on his. She could not tell him anything. She had told him all of it beforehand, in red pencil, with numbers: *Not if I am sick. Not if I ask for my mother.* A little froth had come at the corner of her mouth. He wiped it off with his cuff. He could not have said whose hand did that.

It was his right hand that he had to watch.

It did not go up by itself. That was the thing that he would remember. He had been waiting for it; he had looked at that arm for a year as you would look at a dog that has bitten somebody. It lay against his thigh, with a stone in it that would have stopped all this in the time it takes to swallow, and it was shaking so that the knuckles knocked on the floor; and every bit of that was him. Nobody was holding it down for him. There was no voice. He had only to open his fingers. *I can’t fix that*, he had said, to a child. He could fix this. He had done it once before, on a hearthrug, with his thumb. He knelt on the flagstones of a pastry-cook’s shop with the only person he had ever come for dying against his chest, by her own arrangement, and he kept his hand shut.

Seven. Her lips had gone blue.

“Harry,” said Lyra.

It was hardly a breath, and it was not on any sheet. He bent his head.

“Mother,” she said. “I want—”

*Not if I ask for my mother.*

He shut his eyes. He held on.

And something moved, under his thumb.

He knew it before he looked: smaller than a pulse, a slow, slight, drowsy shifting, like a child turning over. He took his thumb away by a quarter of an inch. The lid had come down. It came from the top and the bottom, thin and grey, and it stopped halfway and drooped, and the last dull bit of brown looked at him without seeing him at all, and went out.

There was a small flat head lying on a girl’s wrist, with its eye shut.

He felt all of her let go at once.

“It’s asleep,” said Lyra.

It was the voice from the wind. He had heard it once before, for the length of a song, and thought that he would never hear it again.

“Don’t talk. You said. Listen.” Her hand had come up, somehow; it had got hold of the front of his robes. Her eyes were enormous, and there was somebody in them, right up at the front, and no time at all. “First. Nine words. You’ll see it. Malfoy Manor stands two miles south of Marlborough, Wiltshire.”

Something happened in his head.

He had flown over that ground twice. He had crossed it on his hands and knees, in August, with his ring burning, and found thistles. Now it stood up. It was as though a sheet had been pulled off a piece of furniture: there were gates, with the gravel going away between two lines of limes, and a long pale front with a great many windows; there was a lake; there was a white bird on a lawn. He could have walked to it in the dark. He knew which was the east stair.

“Say you’ve got it.”

“I’ve got it.”

“Good.” She dragged at a breath. It did not go far. “The rest is on paper. All of it. I’ve done it in order. It’s in the green book, under the fender, the room’ll give it you. This is only—” She had to stop. He saw what it cost her to start again, and he did not help, because it was the seventh clause and it was hers. “This is what I wouldn’t write.”

The snow was going past the window. He could hear her teeth.

“The second of June. I burnt a letter. You held up my coat. There was a dinner that night, and he’d wanted all of us; and I wasn’t there. So he looked at my chair. And then he looked at the one beside it.” It was coming in pieces, each on its own small breath, quite clear. “*You were missed.* He put that on a card. He told my brother he would do. He gave him the box. That’s why. I stood up in July and asked for it, in front of eight people, with my nose running. I said it was mine. And he put this on me to settle the account.”

Her fingers had tightened in his robes.

“You’ve thought since August that I went back to them. You’ve got it in a notebook. I didn’t. I never went anywhere. This is what it cost, to say *you*, on that rug. That’s all it is. It’s the bill.” She was glaring at him, as she had glared at him over the cloak, with her whole face shaking. “And I’d do it again. Do you hear? Them or you. I would do it tomorrow. I’d ask for it in Latin.”

He could not have spoken if the contract had allowed it.

He had sat on a stone seat with porridge on his cuff and thought that she had sold him. He had stood at the end of a table and put *Please come* through her in front of six hundred people, to see if there was anybody in the house. She had been in there the whole time, with the bill in her arm, buttering toast.

“I hope you win,” said Lyra. Then her mouth twisted, and it was very nearly the old look, the one for Weasleys. “No. That’s what one says. I hope you *live*. I don’t care in the least who wins.”

Her head had gone back against his arm. Her breath was coming at the top of her chest now, and not very often, and her lips moved for some time before anything came. He thought that she was at the end of the list.

She was. It was what came after it.

“I could have shouted.”

It was a different voice, with no headings in it.

“On the Sunday. I had the word. It was *there*, I had it in my teeth, I’d only to open my— She was laughing at me. She’d learnt to say no. She was so pleased.” Her face had come apart. He had seen it do that in an Owlery, at fourteen, with her nose running; he had done that, as well. “I did the sum. My mother, and Draco. It came out. It always comes out, I’m so *quick*, Harry, I was done before she’d swallowed. And she held the next one out to me. This one. She couldn’t see properly, by then, and she got it as far as my mouth, and she said— she said it was—”

It went out from under her, and what came instead was not loud; she had no breath to be loud with. She lay in the crook of his arm on the floor of Greer Goyle’s shop with her mouth open and the tears running into her ears, and cried, in small dry terrible sips, for a girl who had left her a sandwich; and the thing on her wrist slept through it.

He held her. He had been told not to interrupt.

He did not know how long that was. The church had not struck. He had her wrist, and under his fingers the beat was doing something that he had felt once before, on a wet floor, in the dark: it ran, and it stopped, and it ran. He had hardly been able to find it then. It was lighter now.

His right hand had come up. It was only a hand; it had the stone in it, and nobody behind it but him. He took it down.

Her eyes had found him again. They were going past him, to something over his shoulder, and she brought them back, and it took her some trouble.

“Not yet,” said Lyra.

He nodded. He could not see her very well.

“There’s— I hadn’t done. There’s Bode’s sister. You’re to— the gloves. And Vanessa. I never told her that I—” It ran. It stopped. Her lips had gone the colour of the flags. “Not *yet*.”

That was twice.

He waited. He had been waiting for six years, one way and another; he had thought that he was good at it. Her mouth moved again. It made the shape. He watched it, and there was no sound with it at all, and he did not know what the shape was. It might have been *not*. It might have been his name. Her eyes had not closed. They had simply stopped being fixed on anything.

*If you can’t say, I decide.*

She had ruled a line through that, quite straight. She had put the apostrophe in.

He did not use it at once. He would have liked, afterwards, to be able to say that he had; or else that he had held on to the last possible instant, as she’d have done, with a watch. He did neither. He knelt there with his thumb on a sleeping snake and counted her pulse through four more beats, because she had said *not yet*, twice, and she was the only person alive who had ever told him no and meant him to hear it. One. It stumbled. Two. He had put her on a carpet once for five hours and ten minutes, and gone away. Three.

There was not a fourth. He was not as brave as she was. He never had been.

He got her mouth open with his thumb, and put the stone on the back of her tongue, and pushed.

Nothing happened.

He had known that it would take a moment. It had taken a moment with Ron. He held her jaw shut and stroked her throat, as you do with a cat, and said her name, and went on saying it. He counted ten. He counted twenty. Her head was heavy on his arm. *I waited*, he thought, quite clearly. *She told me to, and I did what I was told, for once in my life, and I’ve killed her with it.* He could hear a noise like somebody sawing wood, and it was him.

She coughed.

It was a small, wet, ugly sound. Her whole body jerked. She turned her head and was sick on the flagstones, and on his knee, and dragged in a breath that went all the way down, with a whoop in it, like a child’s; and under his fingers the beat came back, a little at a time. He had felt that once before. He knew what came next.

He looked.

The thin grey lid had begun to draw back. It slid up and it slid down, in no hurry. A line of dull brown showed, and widened. It was perfectly round, and quite awake, and it looked at him.

He had about a second. He had it ready; they had not written this part down, but he had known since Friday what it would have to be.

“You *idiot!*” said Harry. His voice broke in the middle, and he let it. “You stupid— You could have *died!* One of *hers!* I was standing there, I was right in front of you, what did you think— Breathe. Breathe, damn you. I’ve got you.”

Her eyes were on his. They were wet, and exhausted, and there was nobody in them. She had gone back in. She had done it between one breath and the next, and shut the door, and he would not have known that there had ever been anyone at the window, except that the fingers of her right hand were still fast in the front of his robes, down where nothing could see.

“Let go of me, Potter,” said Lyra, in the thin remains of the pleasant voice. “You’re kneeling in my friend’s shop.”

The door hit the wall.

He came through it as he had come through another, in December: all black, with the snow on his shoulders and no breath left in him, and his left hand clamped round his right wrist. He had run from the gates. He stopped on the threshold. The black eyes went over the room, the stool, the two cloaks on the floor; they went to a pleated paper case, on the flags, with five-sixths of a chocolate in it, cut very neatly; and then to her face, and to what was lying on her arm, open.

For an instant Harry saw him understand all of it.

It went across the long yellow face and was put away, in no time at all. He had been doing that for longer than either of them had been alive.

“Miss Malfoy,” said Snape. He had not got his breath. “I see that grief has made you stupid as well as thin. Your mother will be so interested.” He crossed the floor, and bent, and took up the paper case between a finger and thumb, and folded it shut; it went into his robes. He looked down at Harry, on his knees, with sick on his cuff. “And Potter. On the floor again. I had not thought it possible for you to be of less use than you were in December.”

He let that stand for the space of a breath.

“I congratulate you,” said Snape. “You appear, on this occasion, to have been in time.”
