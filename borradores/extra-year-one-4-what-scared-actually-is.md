# Year One, Part 4: What Scared Actually Is

A few evenings after Harry Potter went up the Headmaster’s stairs to complain about him, Severus Snape went up them himself, and did not sit down.

The Headmaster pointed him to a chair with the air of a man who had been expecting him for an hour and had kept the tea warm. Snape walked past the chair and the tea and stopped in front of the desk.

“What,” he said, “did you tell the boy?”

“That you owe James and Lily Potter a debt you can never repay,” said Dumbledore, “and that until it is paid, you will keep their son alive.” He folded his hands. “He came all the way up here on his own, you know, to complain about you. He did not know the password. He stood at the bottom and recited sweets until the gargoyle gave in. It took him eleven.”

“He is eleven. He complains about everything.”

“He looked me in the eye and told me that you are unfair. Not very elegantly. But I could not find anything in it to correct.”

Snape did not trouble to deny it.

“And the rest?” he said at last. “Did you tell him the rest?”

“No.” Dumbledore regarded him over his spectacles. “Whatever else Harry knows, Severus, he did not have it from me.”

“He knows it,” said Snape. “He said as much to me. In a corridor, after dinner.”

One of the silver instruments on the shelf behind the desk let out a thin puff of smoke and was still.

“You may be as disagreeable to the children as you please,” said Dumbledore. “I have long since stopped hoping otherwise. But it is not to cost us the Stone. Watch it as you have been watching it.”

“I have not been careless.”

“The boy hates you, Severus, and he is a wizard, however small. I should not like you to be looking at him when you ought to be looking somewhere else.” He paused. “And I know whom you suspect. Keep it to yourself until you can prove it. To anybody.”

He ought to have bowed and gone. He did not.

“The boy has changed,” he said. “In September he was merely insolent. Now he says the Dark Lord’s name across my classroom as though he were reading it off a list. He has begun to learn every page before I set it, so as to leave me nothing to say. And he watches me.” He stopped. “He watches me, Headmaster, the way one watches a man one has already seen make a mistake, and is waiting for him to make it again. When I look at him, I think of the Dark Lord. I do not know how else to put it.”

He had expected the Headmaster to be troubled. Instead the old man looked interested, and then, unforgivably, amused.

“You need not worry about Harry,” said Dumbledore. “He has something I lack, and that you, once, had a great deal of.”

“Don’t.”

“Love,” said Dumbledore, quite unrepentant. “It sent him up my stairs this week.”

Snape had heard the sermon before, and would hear it again. He went out without a word.

The staircase carried him down, turning, at its own slow pace, and somewhere about halfway he found that his right hand had closed over his left forearm, through the sleeve, on the place where nothing had burned for ten years. He took it away. By the time the gargoyle let him out into the corridor, he had decided that he had not done it.

The fourth-year came in to breakfast with his hair wet.

It was flat and stringy and parted in all the wrong places, and it had not been near a bath. He came down the Slytherin table with his eyes on his plate and sat at the very end of it, and the people nearest him moved along. By the time he had reached for the toast the smell had reached Lyra, eleven places up: the boys’ lavatory at the bottom of the dungeon stairs, the one with the cracked cistern that nobody used if they could help it.

Nobody laughed. A month ago it would have been the funniest thing to happen in the dungeons since Christmas. Yesterday afternoon, on the main staircase, this same boy had put out his foot as Weasley went by, and Weasley had gone down six steps on his chin, and half of Slytherin had laughed then.

Lyra found that she was looking at the Gryffindor table, and that so was everybody else.

Potter was eating porridge. Somebody nudged him, and he looked down the Hall at the boy on the end of the Slytherin bench, and his face filled up with honest, interested puzzlement, like a boy who has been shown a sum he cannot do.

“What happened to *him*?” he said, quite loudly. “Did he fall in the lake?”

A Hufflepuff girl laughed out loud before she could stop herself. Then several more did. At the end of the Slytherin table, the fourth-year put his toast down.

And for a quarter of a second, while the whole Hall was looking at the fourth-year, Potter looked at Lyra.

There was nothing in it at all. That was what she could not get past, all morning. It was not a gloat, and it was not a warning. It was the look a person gives a clock, to see what time it is. Then he went back to his porridge.

It had been three weeks since the Potions lesson, and in three weeks Slytherin House had learned to leave Harry Potter the whole width of a corridor.

It had not been one thing. It had been a great many small ones, and the trouble with each of them was that nobody could prove it. Two third-years had cornered him behind the greenhouses to teach him some manners, and had both spent the night in the hospital wing, and neither of them would say what had happened; Potter, who had a split lip, said he had walked into a door. Whatever you did to Potter, or to anybody near him, came back. It came back worse, and it came back without a single mark on it that a teacher could pick up and look at. And all the while he went about the castle with half the school at his elbow, laughing, as though the whole thing were enormous fun.

So the Slytherins had stopped. They went round him. They found things to look at in corridors when he passed. And since the whole of Slytherin had gone quiet, nobody was much frightened of Lyra any more either. She could take her wand out in a corridor now and people simply went on walking. Second-years had stopped stepping aside for her. And the worst of it, the part she would never forgive anybody for, was the game.

That afternoon the three of them were on the bench outside the library, under the window, when somebody called it from the far end of the corridor.

“*Look out—Potter!*”

It was a Ravenclaw, by the sound of it, and three or four voices laughed.

Lyra did not turn round. Goyle swung all the way round on the bench, and Lyra’s shoulders, if she was honest, gave a small jump of their own.

There was nothing behind them but a suit of armour.

“You jumped,” said Goyle, under her breath.

“I did not jump.”

“Your shoulders did.”

“My shoulders were *checking the corridor*. One checks a corridor. It is called being sensible.”

“It looked like jumping.”

“Well, it *wasn’t!*”

The name had started doing it to her too. That was the truth of it. In Slytherin they said *Potter* now very much the way grown-ups at her father’s dinners said the other name: quietly, and only when they had to.

“Malfoy,” said Crabbe, in the careful voice she kept for things she thought might be unwelcome, “couldn’t we just keep out of his way? Like everybody else?” She twisted the end of one plait round her finger. “My mum says you don’t argue with a wasp. You just go into another room.”

“Crabbe! Whose side are you *on?*”

“Yours! I only meant—”

“He is a boy with a *scar*,” said Lyra. “Somebody tried to kill him when he was too small to notice, and failed, and everybody has been clapping ever since. I do not go into other rooms for people who are famous for *not being dead*.”

She believed it, too. She very nearly believed it.

At the start of the year it had been different. At the flying lesson she had come off her broom at the top of an oak, which was not a thing she cared to think about, and Potter had come down out of the sky after her with his hand out and his whole face open, as though she were the only thing up there worth catching. She had been brought up to be looked at, and she knew what being looked at felt like. She had been falling off a broom in front of two Houses, and she had still been the whole point of the afternoon.

Now, on the rare occasions he looked at her, his eyes went through her like something thin and cold, and then he let out one short breath through his nose, the way her father did over a disappointing wine, and looked away. *You’d have to matter first*, he had said, in Potions, to his cauldron.

It was not a way she was used to being looked at. It was not, she was quite sure, a way anybody had the *right* to look at her.

“I am not frightened of him,” said Lyra.

Goyle said nothing. Crabbe said nothing, more loudly.

“I am *not*—”

And then Neville Longbottom came out of the library with an armful of books and went past them towards the stairs, walking in the anxious, hurried way he always walked, as if he expected the floor to give way under him.

Lyra watched him go. It had been her plan since before Christmas, after all, and she had simply not got round to it: leave Potter alone, the only person stupid enough to say the name, and go for the people round him. Potter might be awkward. Longbottom was what Longbottom had always been.

She took out her wand.

“Professor Flitwick says practice makes perfect,” she said. “Who remembers the Leg-Locker Curse?”

Neville came into the Gryffindor common room through the portrait hole with his legs stuck together, and fell flat on his face on the hearthrug.

He had hopped. He had evidently hopped all the way from the library, up seven floors, with his books clutched to his chest and his knees locked, and half the common room was on its feet laughing before anybody had worked out that it was not a joke. Hermione was the first to stop. She went across the room at a run, knelt beside him and said the counter-curse, and his legs sprang apart, and he got up slowly, shaking.

“Blimey, Neville,” said Ron. “There’s easier ways up the stairs.”

Harry did not say anything.

“Malfoy,” said Neville miserably. He sat down on the arm of the nearest chair; his knees did not trust themselves yet. “Outside the library. She said she wanted to see if she could still do it.”

“Go to Professor McGonagall!” said Hermione. “Tell her!”

Neville shook his head. “It’ll only make it worse.”

“You can’t just let her, Neville!” said Ron. “You’ve got to hit back sometimes, or they never stop. She walks all over people, and you lie down so she doesn’t have to step so high—”

Harry got up.

He took out his wand, and turned it over once in his fingers, and something in the way he did it made the Gryffindors nearest to him go quiet, and look at each other, and then look back at him with a nervous, hopeful sort of interest, as at a firework somebody has just lit.

“Outside the library,” said Harry. “That’s what you said, Neville?”

“Yes… why?”

“Well, it’s no good practising a jinx on somebody who can’t jinx you back. She won’t learn anything.” He closed his hand on the wand. “Somebody ought to give her a proper go.”

“But there’s three of them,” said Neville. “There’s always three of them.”

“Then she’ll have help.”

“Back in a bit,” said Harry to the room, and went out through the portrait hole.

He had got as far as the first staircase when he heard somebody scrambling after him.

“Harry, *don’t*.” Hermione had caught him up, out of breath, her hair coming down. “You can’t do magic in the corridors. It’s against the rules.”

Harry tipped his head back towards the portrait hole, behind which Neville was presumably still sitting on the arm of a chair, waiting for his legs to stop shaking. Hermione followed the look, and pressed her lips together, and changed her ground.

“She’ll tell. She’s clever, Harry, she’ll go straight to a teacher and make it look like it was all you.”

“Let her.”

“She’ll go to *Snape!* You don’t know what he’ll do!”

“Snape.” Harry’s face went cold, as though somebody had opened a window behind it. “I’d quite like a detention with Snape. Just him and me, in the dungeons, late, with nobody else about. I’d like to see what he does.”

“*Harry!*”

Hermione’s mouth fell open. She looked up and down the empty staircase, in case anybody had heard, and then took hold of his shoulder.

“You’re being *strange*. You’ve been strange for weeks.”

“Am I?” Harry spread his arms. “Everybody jumps out of their skin if you say Voldemort. Nobody jumps at the people who used to work for him. One of them marks our homework. Tell me which bit of that *isn’t* strange.”

“But everything you *do* is dangerous! You know somebody wants to—Harry, somebody might really try to kill you!”

“I know,” said Harry. “I keep hoping they’ll get on with it.” He smiled at her as he said it, and did not seem to know what his face was doing. “Then I’d know who’s who.”

Hermione let go of his shoulder.

She looked at him the way she had looked at the troll in the girls’ lavatory at Hallowe’en, which was not a way she had ever looked at Harry before. He pulled away from her and went on down the stairs.

He was not angry yet. He noticed that, as you might notice the weather. He was going down the moving stairs two at a time, and he was thinking, quite calmly, that there would be three of them and that they would be on the bench under the window, because that was where they always sat; that Goyle was the quickest of the three and Crabbe the slowest; that the library doors were behind the bench and the stairs at the far end; that if it went wrong, it would go wrong by the window. He did not ask himself where any of it came from. It seemed to have been there when he went to look.

“Oi! Harry! *Wait!*”

Ron came clattering down after them both, red in the face and grinning all over it. He had climbed out of the portrait hole behind Hermione and come down two steps at a time.

“You’re not having all the fun,” he said. “I want to see Crabbe hop.”

He fell in beside Harry, still grinning, and started describing at great length what Crabbe would look like going up seven flights with her ankles stuck together, and Harry laughed. It was his ordinary laugh. The face he had worn a minute ago on the stairs might never have been there.

Behind them, after a moment, Harry heard Hermione make a small, furious noise on the landing, and come after them.

They saw him coming from the end of the corridor: Potter in front, with his hands in his pockets, and Weasley at his shoulder grinning all over his face, and Granger a few steps behind, looking as though she had lost an argument on the stairs.

Lyra did not get up. Getting up would have meant something.

“Looking for me, Malfoy?”

He smiled as he said it. It was a very good smile, and Lyra knew good ones when she saw them; she had grown up among people who smiled for a living, at her mother’s parties, over the rims of glasses. Somebody had taught him that smile, she thought, and taught him well. It had nothing to do with being pleased.

“Leg-Locker,” he said. “On *Neville*. Honestly? My cousin used to do that sort of thing when he was seven, and he didn’t even have a wand. I thought you’d come up with something a bit better than that.”

People were drifting over from the library doors to see what was happening. Lyra could feel them arriving behind her, one and two at a time, and in a few words he had made her, for every one of them, a girl who jinxed people’s legs in corridors because she could not think of anything cleverer.

“Potter,” she said.

“That’s me.” He said it loudly, for the corridor. “Was there something you wanted to tell me? In front of everybody? Only you’ve gone a bit pink.”

“In your *dreams*, Potter.”

She put up a hand and swept her hair back over her shoulder.

It was the oldest thing she knew how to do. She felt every eye in the corridor go with it, as they always did, and she stood up at last, because now it would mean something. Chin up. Shoulders back. She was Lyra Malfoy, and this was a corridor in a school, and he was a boy with a scar.

“You want to be careful, Potter.”

“Of what?”

“Of *everything*.” There were twenty people round them now, and more coming, and she raised her voice to reach the back. “You fight with older students. You answer back in Potions. You haven’t any respect for anybody. If you carry on like this, things are going to start happening. To you, and to everybody round you.” Her hand had found her wand. “Longbottom was the polite version.”

Potter took a step towards her.

All three of them flinched. Lyra felt Crabbe do it on one side of her and Goyle on the other, as neatly as if they had practised it, and caught herself at once and turned it into a toss of the head; but the people at the front had seen.

“Hang on,” said Potter, and he looked honestly confused, like a boy asking for help with his homework. “*I* fight with older students? You mean the two third-years? Two of them, behind the greenhouses, waiting for a first-year? That’s the story you want to tell everybody? If I were them I’d be having my breakfast in the kitchens.”

Somebody at the back laughed. Then several people did.

“And respect.” He turned so that he was talking to all of them. “Go on, then. Which bit wasn’t true? That Snape used to be one of *his*? Ask anybody’s dad. That he takes points off you for breathing, unless you’re in Slytherin? Ask anybody.”

This time the whole corridor laughed: the Gryffindors, and the Hufflepuffs who had been on their way to the library, and a knot of Ravenclaws by the window with their books held against their chests. Everybody but the Slytherins.

Lyra had been laughed at before, a little, by people who did not matter. The laughter went round her at first, the way water goes round a stone, and then it was not going round her any more. She could feel Crabbe and Goyle going quiet beside her, and her own mouth doing something she had not told it to, and she bit her lip to stop it.

“Stop it! Both of you!” Granger had pushed her way to the front. She looked at the crowd and then at Potter, and pulled at his sleeve. “Harry, that isn’t why we came. We came about *Neville!*”

“Speak for yourself,” muttered Weasley, and stopped when Granger looked at him.

“You’re going too far as well, Harry, you know you are. But *you*—” She rounded on Lyra. “You can’t just go round jinxing people because you feel like it! You owe Neville an apology!”

Lyra had just been laughed at by half the school. She was not going to apologise to anybody for anything, and least of all because a girl with bushy hair had stepped out of the crowd and told her to.

“Who asked *you?*” Her voice went high, and she let it. “Go back to your Muggles, you filthy little *Mudblood*!”

It was a word from home. Her father used it about the Minister’s new secretary, and about the people who had bought the house at the end of the lane, over the soup. She threw it like the last card in her hand, the one that wins; and she waited for the gasp, and for the Slytherins to laugh.

The corridor changed.

The laughing stopped as if somebody had shut a door on it. Granger had gone white. Weasley, who a minute ago had been grinning about Crabbe’s legs, stepped in front of her with his fists clenched and his ears scarlet, and even the Ravenclaws by the window had gone pale, and not one of the Slytherins laughed.

And Potter did not look at Lyra.

He looked at Granger. He looked at her arms, first one and then the other, and at her hands, and her wrists, and then up at her face, quickly, one thing after another, the way Lyra’s mother went down the table after a large dinner to see that all the silver was still there. As though he had expected something to be missing. As though he could not believe it was not.

Then he turned round, and Lyra saw his face.

There was no smile on it. There was nothing on it. It had gone white and perfectly still, and it was not angry, or not in any way she knew; and it was looking at her as though she were a long way off, and as though it had come a long way to look.

He moved first.

“*Locomotor Mortis!*”

Crabbe’s legs snapped together and she went down sideways, and her wand skittered off across the flagstones. Goyle had hers out a moment later, quicker than Lyra had ever seen her do anything; there was a crack, and Potter’s wand went spinning out of his fingers and away under the bench. Weasley went for Goyle. There was a great deal of flailing. Goyle shoved him so hard he went rolling across the floor, but he had got hold of her wand on the way, and by the time the two of them were on their feet again both wands had been flung down the corridor in opposite directions, and Weasley and Goyle stood glaring at each other with nothing in their hands.

Which left Lyra.

Potter walked towards her. He had no wand. He did not seem to have noticed.

She had hers up. Her hand was shaking, and she did not care, because it was pointing at him, and it was a wand, and he had nothing.

“*Flipendo!*”

It hit him on the side of the head. She saw it hit. His head went over, and he took one step sideways to keep his feet, and then he straightened and kept coming. Something dark had started down from his eyebrow. He did not put up a hand to it.

She tried again. She had the next word ready behind her teeth, and her arm would not lift. It simply stayed where it was, at the height of her chest, with the wand at the end of it, as though it had been told something she had not, and the word stayed behind her teeth with nowhere to go.

He took the wand out of her fingers.

He did not snatch it. He took it as though she had been holding it out to him, and he did not throw it away. Then he had her by the wrist, and she went back against the wall with a thump that knocked the breath out of her.

“*Ow!*”

“Take that back,” said Potter.

“Let—go of me!”

He had her wand in his other hand. He was not pointing it at anything. He held it low and loose by his side, between two fingers and a thumb, the way her father held his across the dinner table when there was somebody at it he did not care for, like a man who has never once in his life needed to aim in a hurry. Lyra had seen a great many wizards hold their wands. She had never seen a boy do it like that. She had seen men.

“You’re frightened of his *name*,” said Potter, “and you’ll say *that*? You want something to be scared of, be scared of that!”

“I’ll say what I *like!*” She was frightened; she could feel it in her knees. She said it anyway, straight into his face, because it was the only thing she had left that was hers. “*Mudblood!*”

“*Voldemort!*”

People flinched all round them, as if he had thrown something into the crowd. The dark line from his eyebrow had reached his eye, and gone into it, and he did not blink it away. Half his face had gone red, and he was looking at her through it.

“He came for me when I was one,” said Potter, “and he couldn’t do it. A *baby*, Malfoy. He lost to a baby, and then he ran off and hid. And you can’t even say his name?”

He was not shouting any more. He had leaned in close, and his voice had gone quiet and level, the voice of somebody explaining a thing he had explained before, to other people, in other places; and her wand was coming up in his hand, slowly, in no hurry, until at last it was pointing at her.

“That isn’t scared, Malfoy. That’s manners. You want to know what scared actually is—”

His lips came apart on the next word.

Her face went.

At home, crying came with a moment first, to decide about it in. This came up from somewhere under her ribs, all at once, like being sick, and her mouth opened by itself and the noise came out of it, high and wet and enormous, and went off the stone and came back to her, so that she could hear exactly what she sounded like. She sounded like Draco, at three, when he was lost in the garden. Her eyes had shut. She did not remember shutting them. She could feel the wall against her shoulder blades and his hand on her wrist and her own breath going in and out in great dragging pieces, and she could not stop any of it, and in the middle of all of it she heard her own voice, a long way off, saying things.

“Stop it,” she said. “I hate it, I hate both of them—Vol-Voldemort, and that—that word—I hate all of it—”

“—and *you*, I hate *you*—”

Harry stared at her.

She had said the name. He did not think she knew. She was crying like a very small child, with her eyes screwed shut and her mouth wide open, not caring who saw, because she had not yet remembered that anybody could; and somewhere in the middle of it the name had come out of her as easily as any other word, and gone, and she had not heard it.

He had been going to say something. It was still there, behind his teeth: a short, hard shape, all ready, like a sneeze. He did not know what it was. He shut his mouth on it, and felt it go back down to wherever it had come from, and did not try to follow it.

Then he looked at his own hand.

It was still round her wrist, and there were red marks coming up on her skin at the edges of his fingers. That would hurt. He had not thought about that. He let go of her as fast as if she had burnt him.

*I hate you.* It was the most babyish thing he had ever heard anybody say, and it knocked the breath out of him in a way the jinx had not. He actually stepped back from it, as though she had pushed him.

Behind him, Crabbe got her legs apart. It had only been a first-year’s jinx, and it had given out. She came across the flagstones like a small round cannonball and hit him square in the chest with both hands.

“*Get off her!*”

He went over backwards. He was not expecting it, and he had nothing left to stop himself with; he sat down hard on the stone with his head ringing. By the time he had sorted out which way was up, Crabbe had Malfoy by one arm and Goyle had her by the other, and the three of them were going away down the corridor as fast as they could go, with Malfoy still sobbing between them.

Afterwards the corridor went very quiet.

Harry lay on the flagstones and looked at the ceiling. By rights it ought to have been cheering. The Slytherins had run; that was how these things were scored. Nobody cheered. It turned out that nobody, from any House, could watch a girl cry like that and feel they had been on the winning side, and somehow, without anybody saying a word, the whole corridor had decided that the person in the wrong was the one on the floor. People looked at him with uncomfortable faces, and looked away, and drifted off in twos and threes towards their own common rooms.

Ron came and crouched beside him. “You all right? Sorry, mate. Crabbe was mine. I lost her.”

“Doesn’t matter.” Harry waved a hand at him. It was better this way, really. If she had gone on standing there in front of him, he did not know what he would have done; and he found, lying there, that he meant it exactly.

Hermione had not come over. She was standing by the window where the Ravenclaws had been, with Ron’s wand in one hand and Goyle’s in the other, and she was not looking at him. She had stopped being white. She had gone back to being Hermione, nearly. She still did not come over.

“*What* is going on here?”

Professor McGonagall came round the corner at a speed that did not seem possible in a woman her age, and stopped dead at the sight of Harry on the floor with blood all down one side of his face.

“Potter! You’re *bleeding!*”

“It’s nothing, Professor.” He did not feel like being funny.

McGonagall’s nostrils went thin and white.

“Fighting. *Again*. I warned you, Potter, in so many words. Five months, and I have had more trouble out of you than out of the Weasley twins in their first year, which I should not have thought possible. Twenty points, and a detention, and you may thank your stars it is not worse—”

“Why’s it only m—”

“Twenty points from every student involved in this, Mr Weasley and Miss Granger *included*, and detentions all round! Hospital wing, Potter, this minute.” She turned on a tall Hufflepuff who had been edging away towards the stairs. “Mr Diggory. Who was Potter fighting?”

So Malfoy would get a detention too. Harry was glad, and ashamed of how glad.

He got himself up off the floor. McGonagall was looking at him again, and then lower down, at his hand.

“And whose,” she said, “is that?”

Harry looked down.

He was holding a wand. It was not his; his was still under the bench, where Goyle had put it. This one was lighter than his, and paler, and his fingers had settled round it as they settled round his own, without his having to think about it. He had held it all the way through. He had held it lying on the floor. He had not noticed.

“Malfoy’s, Professor,” he said, and held it out to her, handle first.

McGonagall took it, and looked at it, and at him, for longer than he liked. Then she told him again to go to the hospital wing.

He went. His head had gone cold and clear, now that all the heat had gone out of it, and things were coming back to him in order, one after another. Neville on the hearthrug. The bench under the window. Her face. All of it came back in order except two things, and he kept turning them over on the stairs and could not make them fit.

He had never heard that word before today. Nobody at Privet Drive had ever needed one like it. And he had known what it was before he saw a single face; he had known it like a smell, and he had turned round to look at Hermione’s arms, and he could not have said, if anybody had asked him, what he had expected to see there.

The other thing was the word he had not said.

He could still feel the shape of it. He did not know what it was. He did not want to know, and that was the part that frightened him: not that he did not know, but that something in him did.

He had decided, on a broom at the start of the year, that he was going to change her. He had put his chin up and told himself so.

“I was going to change her,” Harry said, to nobody. “I made her cry.”

Madam Pomfrey mended his eyebrow in about a minute, with a great deal of tutting, and in the morning there was nothing to show where it had been. That was the last anybody saw of it. The Gryffindors did not tell the story, because it was not the sort of story that made Harry look good. The Slytherins did not tell it, because it was not the sort that made Malfoy look good either. Nobody repeated what he had said about Snape. Snape himself, the next time Harry came into the dungeon, did not so much as raise his eyes, and Slytherin took its lead from him. By Easter nobody spoke of any of it.

Crabbe came up to the dormitory after lights out, when the others were asleep, and sat down on the end of Lyra’s bed in the dark.

Lyra had the hangings drawn. She did not open them.

“McGonagall gave me this,” said Crabbe, very low. “For you.”

Something came through the gap in the curtains, handle first. Lyra took it. It was her own wand, and for a moment, in her hand, in the dark, it did not feel like hers: it felt like a thing that had been somewhere without her, and had come back, and was not going to say where. Then it was only her wand again. She put it under her pillow.

“I didn’t cry,” she said.

There was a pause on the other side of the curtain. Crabbe, who was the worst liar in Slytherin, and possibly in the castle, took a long time over it.

“No,” she said at last. “You didn’t.”

She went on sitting there. After a while Lyra put her hand out between the hangings, without meaning anything by it, and Crabbe took hold of it and held on, and neither of them said another word until Lyra was asleep.

She did not say the word again that year. The next time was the following autumn, on a Quidditch pitch, about some brooms, and that time it was Weasley who went for his wand. She went on flinching at the other one, the name, as everybody she had grown up with flinched at it. But there was a particular face that she flinched from more: a boy’s face, white and set, with blood running into one eye and the Dark Lord’s name in its mouth.

She was very good, by the summer, at not thinking about it, or about the cold weeks before it. She was so good at it that, a year and a half later, when she watched that cold face turn on her father at the foot of the marble staircase, she thought she was seeing it for the first time; and when, that same September, a mild man in patched robes sat in a shabby armchair and asked her whether Harry Potter had ever given her a really bad fright, it did not so much as occur to her that there might be an answer.
