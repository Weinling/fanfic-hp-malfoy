# Chapter 93: What I Found

The eye went on looking at him until Snape’s sleeve came down over it.

That was the first thing anybody did. Snape had got one arm under her knees and the other behind her shoulders, and he came up off the floor with her as if she had been a wet cloak, with the water running out of her skirts and his own robes black to the thigh; her head went back against his elbow, and her left hand swung out, palm up, with the small flat head lying on the wrist. Then the sleeve fell across it, and Harry could move again.

He was still on his side on the stone. He got up as far as his knees, and that appeared to be as far as it went.

Snape did not look at him. He was looking at the basin. The box sat where Harry had put it, squared with the rim, the lid beside it and one pleated paper empty in the corner; and Snape considered it down the length of his nose, with the face he kept for a cauldron that was going to have to be thrown away. He knew what it was. He had known, Harry thought, a good deal longer than since Sunday.

“That,” said Snape, “is Miss Malfoy’s property.”

His voice was exactly as usual, and it was pitched to be heard, and not only in this room.

“It isn’t, sir,” said Harry. His own came out in pieces; he had sung most of it away. “It’s the Headmaster’s. It says so on the card.”

Something went across the long yellow face and was put back where it belonged.

“Then by all means deliver it, Potter. I’m sure he’ll be touched.” He shifted her weight, as carefully as he had ever decanted anything. “You will come to the hospital wing when you are able to stand. You will not come further than the door.”

He went without hurry and without a sound, and the door swung to behind the two of them and left Harry kneeling in the middle of the floor.

Every tap in the place was running. He hadn’t noticed, until there was nothing else to hear. He got himself up by the edge of the basin and turned them off one at a time, all down the row, as far as the one with the little snake scratched on its side, which had never worked and wasn’t running now. The water on the floor had gone the colour of the squash you were given at children’s parties, and lapped at the feet of the cubicles. Her shoe was under the third sink, on its side, and a yard beyond it lay a pair of grey woollen gloves, soaked through, with one thumb a good deal longer than the other. He picked those up as well, and held all three things against his chest, and couldn’t think where they went.

“You’ve made a dreadful mess,” said a voice.

She had come up out of the end cubicle and was sitting a few inches above the cistern with her chin on her fists: a squat, glum, pearly girl in thick glasses, who had been crying in that room for fifty-three years and had never, until tonight, been given anything fresh to cry about. She looked delighted.

“I saw,” said Myrtle. “You needn’t think I didn’t. I see everything that goes on in here, and nobody ever asks. She’d put her wand *down*, and you opened her up like a parcel.” She gave a long pleasurable shudder. “It’s the most interesting thing that has happened in this bathroom since I died in it.”

“Yes,” said Harry.

“She comes on Sundays. She stands just there and talks to her own arm. I thought she must be like me, I thought she hadn’t got anybody; I quite took to her. She has never once thrown a book at my head.” The glasses turned on him. “She was saying something, before you came barging in. Over and over. *It’s a nice one. It’s a nice one.* I don’t know what was. I shouldn’t think you do either.”

He didn’t. He stood with a shoe in his hand and felt it go in all the same, like something that was going to be explained to him later.

“I’m going to tell,” said Myrtle.

She said it with her chin up, as somebody says it who has been waiting half a century for the chance. “I told about Olive Hornby, and they said I was being hysterical. I told about the eyes, and I was dead by then, so that didn’t count. This time I’ve got a floor to show for it. I shall go straight through the wall to Madam Pomfrey and say that a boy cut a girl open in my bathroom, and then I shall say which boy.”

He ought to have minded that. He looked for the part of him that was always three moves ahead, that would already have known what a ghost wanted and what it would cost to give it to her; and it was sitting on the floor somewhere with its hands over its head.

“Tell her it was me,” said Harry.

“I *know* it was you. I’ve known you since you were twelve. You stood at that sink and hissed at it.” She drifted up a foot or so, to get the height. “Boys are always doing something at that sink.”

She went into the wall, feet last.

He took the box from the edge of the basin and put the lid on, and pressed down each corner. Then he went out, with the box under one arm and a girl’s shoe in his hand, and left a trail down two corridors that somebody was going to have to see to.

The ring had gone green on the stairs. He looked at it, on the hand that was holding the shoe, and would have liked to be able to take its word.

Madam Pomfrey had the doors of the ward shut. He stood outside them, as he’d been told, and dripped. Through the glass he could see a screen round the third bed and two shadows on it, a tall thin one and a short quick one, and from time to time the short one said something sharp and the tall one failed to answer. When the door opened at last it was the matron who came through it, with her sleeves rolled above the elbow and a basin in her hands that she had put a cloth over, and Snape behind her, drying his fingers one at a time.

She saw the state of him, from the knees to the wrists. She saw the shoe.

“Miss Malfoy,” said Snape, across her shoulder, “has had a fall.”

He said it to the corridor at large, in the voice of a man reading out a notice. Nobody could have supposed that he expected to be believed; and it came to Harry that he wasn’t saying it to either of them. The screen was twenty feet away. That was the length of a room, near enough.

Madam Pomfrey set the basin down on the windowsill.

“I wrote down a heart on Sunday,” she said. “I’ll not write down a fall.”

She was a small woman, and she didn’t raise her voice. “That child has been laid open from the angle of the jaw to the middle of the arm, as clean as a draper’s scissors, and somebody had the closing of it who knew the counter before ever he knelt down. I’ve been thirty years at this, Severus, and I couldn’t have shut that with a month’s notice. One of these days I should very much like to be told how you came by it. Tonight I want the Deputy Headmistress.” Her eyes had gone to Harry’s cuffs, and they stayed there. “And I’ll have the truth from somebody, if I have to get it from the dead.”

The dead obliged. A pair of thick spectacles came through the wall beside the door-frame, followed by the rest.

“It wasn’t a fall!” said Myrtle. “It was *him!* I saw it! She’d put her wand down, and he—”

“Was it you, Potter?” said Madam Pomfrey.

“Yes,” said Harry.

He had expected it to be harder. He had been lying to the staff of this castle for most of six years, and had got so that he could do it with his mouth full; and the true thing turned out to be one syllable long, and to need no arranging at all.

She did not say anything to that. She looked at him with her sharp, tired, reddened eyes, as she had looked at him across a great many beds, and whatever she had been keeping for him behind them since his first year, she now took back. She held out her hand. He gave her the shoe, and the gloves on top of it.

“You’ll stand there,” said Madam Pomfrey, “until Professor McGonagall comes for you, and you’ll not set a foot over that sill. She’ll live. She will have that on her until she’s an old woman, and she’ll never again wear what she likes, and that’s what *live* is going to mean.” She picked up her basin. “Thank you, Myrtle. I’m obliged to you.”

Myrtle went quite silver with pleasure; very possibly nobody had ever said so before.

The door shut. Snape had not gone back in. He stood a little way along the passage, where the wall turned, a good forty feet from the screen; and when he spoke it was low and rapid, and not the voice he had used before.

“Thirteen weeks,” he said. “Thirteen weeks of my Tuesdays, Potter, and when it was wanted you sang like a boy drowning a cat.” His left hand was closed round his right wrist. He seemed to become aware of that, and took it away, and under the cuff there was a mark as though a hot wire had been wound there. “She will be asked what happened. She will say something. Whatever it is, you will be discovered to agree with it. Do you understand me?”

“Yes, sir.”

“And take that upstairs before somebody offers one to a first-year.”

He glanced once at the box, and once, for no longer, at the shut doors. Then he went away down the corridor with his wet robes slapping at his heels, and for the first time since Harry had known him he did not walk like a man who owned the floor.

Professor McGonagall kept a tartan tin on the corner of her desk. In five and a half years he had been offered a biscuit out of it on four occasions, every one of them a day on which he had expected to be expelled. She did not open it.

“Sit down,” she said. “What have you got there?”

“Chocolates, Professor.” It was perfectly true. “They’re for Professor Dumbledore.”

“Then you may put them on the floor. I am not going to conduct this conversation with a boy who is holding a box of sweets.”

He put them on the floor. He set them down by the leg of the chair, an inch at a time, and kept the side of his foot against them; and Professor McGonagall watched him do it over the top of her spectacles, with her mouth gone to a line, plainly adding it to the account.

“There has been a ghost in that lavatory,” said Professor McGonagall, “since before I came to this school as a girl. I was warned about her in my first week. I have not believed one word she has said in fifty years, and it has taken the evidence of my own eyes to make me begin. She tells me that you cut Miss Malfoy from the neck to the elbow. I should be very glad, Potter, to hear that she has exaggerated.”

“She hasn’t.”

“With what?”

“A curse.”

“I had not supposed that it was a butter knife. Which curse?”

He told her. He had never said the whole of it aloud in this life, and it lay on the desk between them, among the inkwells, four syllables long.

“I don’t know it.”

“No, Professor. It isn’t in anything. I had it out of a book that somebody had written in.”

“And Miss Malfoy. Had she her wand in her hand?”

“She’d had it up. She took it down.” He made himself go on in a straight line. “She had started something, and she stopped herself. She’d got it right down by her side; I watched her do it. I had time to watch. Then I cursed her.”

“Why?”

He had a number of answers to that; he’d had most of them for years. An arm had gone up on a landing, and in a pink room, and over a plate of porridge, and something a little behind his right ear had been so pleased. He could have laid the whole of it in front of her, joint by joint, and every word would have been true, and she would have had to go and find somebody else to be angry with.

“I haven’t got one that’s any good,” said Harry.

Professor McGonagall took off her spectacles. Without them she looked older, and a good deal less safe.

“I have taught you for five years and a term,” she said. “In that time I have watched you do a number of things that I’d have given a great deal not to see, and on every occasion I have said to myself: he’s a child; he has had a wretched time of it; there is more to this than I’ve been told. I have grown very tired of being told that there is more to things than I’ve been told.” She laid the spectacles down. “I find I have come to the end of that, Potter. If it rested with me you would be on the train tomorrow morning, and you would not be coming back in January. It doesn’t rest with me. I’m informed that there are reasons. I am not informed what they are.”

“No, Professor.”

“Don’t agree with me. It’s insufferable.” She drew a sheet of parchment towards her and did not write on it. “You are off the Gryffindor team, from tonight until you leave this school. You’ll tell Weasley yourself, before breakfast. I don’t see why I should do that for you, and he has a Cup to win with no Seeker. Every evening of term, from six o’clock until nine, you will be in that chair, with your work on that desk and both your hands on top of it, where I can see them. That includes Saturdays. It will go on until the summer; and if I’m spared, until the summer after.”

“Yes, Professor.”

He had known what he was going to do next since the stairs. He took his wand out of his sleeve. A brown stain had dried into the grain near the handle, and would not come off on his robes. He laid it on the desk in front of her, in the clear space between the tin and the inkstand, and took his hand away.

Professor McGonagall looked at it. Then she looked at him, and it was the most frightening thing she had done all evening, because she was not angry at all.

“Pick that up,” she said.

“I thought you’d better—”

“I know perfectly well what you thought. You thought that if somebody took it away from you, you’d be let off having it. Pick it up. You will carry it, Potter, and you will know that you are carrying it, at every meal, in every corridor where she walks. I’m not going to make that easier for you. I can’t think why you supposed I would.”

He picked it up. It weighed what it had always weighed.

“I’ve written to her mother,” said Professor McGonagall, more quietly. “My owl has come back from Wiltshire twice since September with the letter still on its leg, and no notion of where it had been. Professor Snape tells me that he has other means. I haven’t asked him what they are.” She put her spectacles on again. “The Headmaster will see you at nine. Until then I don’t wish to know where you are. And take your chocolates.”

Neville had lit the lamps in Greenhouse Three and wedged the door.

Snow lay along the ridge of the glass, and where the heat got at it, it was sliding down the panes in long grey sheets. He was at the far bench in his outdoor cloak and a pair of dragon-hide gloves, tying back something that didn’t want to be tied, and he looked up when the cold came in. His eyes went to Harry’s face first, and then lower down, to the cuffs, where they rested for about a second and a half.

“He’s asleep,” said Neville. He kept his voice down. “I gave him the Mandrakes to count. I thought it’d take him till supper, and he’d done them in twenty minutes. There are forty. He went through them twice, because one of them had got behind a pot.”

Under the staging, where the pipes ran, somebody had made a heap of sacks; and on it, curled up very small, with his knees drawn in and his scarf for a pillow, lay Draco Malfoy. He had earth on both knees of his good grey trousers. A leaf had got into his hair, a big furry one shaped like a hand, and it was going gently up and down. The bow tie was still straight. Harry had seen to that himself, about three hours ago, and it seemed to have been done by somebody else.

“Is it hers?” said Neville.

He had not moved from the bench. He wasn’t looking at the cuffs now.

“Yes.”

“Did you—”

“Yes.”

Neville nodded. He took off one glove, a finger at a time, and laid it on the bench, and then the other. His round face had gone the dull bad colour that it had gone over a breakfast table in November, and he looked at the boy on the sacks as if he were working out a distance.

“I’ll take him down,” he said. “To the top of their stair. He knows me. You’re not to take him anywhere tonight, Harry.” He got it out with some difficulty, and without looking away. “I’m sorry. I do mean it, though.”

“I know. I’ve got to tell him first.”

“*Have* you?”

He did not say it unkindly. He was asking, as a person asks who was taken on Sundays, before he could read, to look at something that nobody ever tried to explain to him; and Harry thought about it honestly, for as long as it took the leaf to go up and down twice. This could be done so that a boy of twelve went to bed tonight believing that somebody had it all in hand. He knew how that was done. He’d had it done to him for seven years, with great gentleness, by an expert.

“Yeah,” said Harry.

He got down by the sacks. He didn’t touch him. “Draco.”

The eyes came open at once, pale and entirely clear, like the eyes of someone who has only been pretending. They went to the lamp, and to Neville, and then to Harry; and after that they went where everybody’s did.

“You haven’t fixed it,” said Draco.

“No. Listen. Your sister’s in the hospital wing. She’s going to get better; Madam Pomfrey says so. Professor Snape mended her.” He had worked out the order of this on the way down the lawn, and it was no use to him at all. “I hurt her. It was me. I did it with a curse. She’d stopped, she wasn’t doing anything, and I did it anyway.”

Draco sat up. He did it in stages. He studied Harry, with his head a little on one side; he had his sister’s way of going down a face as though it were a column of figures.

“How many?” he said.

“One.”

“How long?”

Harry showed him. He put two fingers under his own left ear and drew them down across the collarbone and the point of the shoulder, and along the arm, and stopped a little above the elbow. It took longer than he would have thought.

Nothing happened in the small clean face. The hiccups had stopped, some time in the last three hours, and he rather missed them. Draco got down off the sacks on the far side. He brushed at his knees, which made no difference; he took the leaf out of his hair, and looked at it, and gave it to Neville, who seemed to know what one did with those. Then he put his hand in his pocket.

He brought out the coin. He held it on his palm, under the lamp, and considered it; and Harry, who had once knelt on a staircase and said *I’ll come wherever you are*, knelt on the floor of a greenhouse and waited to have it handed back to him.

It went back into the pocket. Draco patted the place once, as he had done outside the Charms corridor: a ticket that one might not use, and would not throw away.

“I should like to go down now, please,” he said, to Neville.

He walked to the door by himself. Neville got the cloak round him and took the lamp in his other hand; and as they went out into the snow a small hand came up, without anybody’s having asked for it, and took hold of a fold of Neville’s sleeve, just above the wrist.

“Twenty-three,” said Harry.

He put the box down in the middle of the desk. Nowhere else would have held it; every other inch was taken up, as it always was, with the spindly silver things, and letters, and a bowl. Professor Dumbledore looked at the pale wood, and at the card in its slot, on which somebody with a very good hand had written out his title, and for a little while he did not touch it.

“There were twenty-four, sir. Greer Goyle had the other one on Sunday.”

“Yes,” said Dumbledore. “Severus came to me that night.”

He lifted the lid. He did it with his right hand; the left lay in his lap, half closed, and the ends of three of the fingers had gone the colour of a candle-wick. He looked for some time at two rows of chocolates, each with its small dark curl. Then he replaced the lid, and moved the bowl of lemon drops a few inches along the desk to make room.

Harry had not been asked to sit. He was glad of it.

“You knew,” he said.

“On Sunday night I knew what had killed her.” The voice was as gentle as ever, and as unhurried, and he had turned the whole of it on Harry, like a lamp. “I shall tell you what I knew before that, since you have earned the question and will not thank me for the answer. I have known since July that Miss Malfoy had been given something to do, and to whom it was to be done. I did not know what she had been given to do it with, or where in this castle she kept it. I took some pains not to find out.”

“*Why?*”

“Because a thing that I had found, I should have had to be seen to have found. There are three people called Malfoy, Harry, one of them twelve years old, who are alive this evening on the understanding that she has not yet failed. There’s a fourth person, in this building, who told me of it. I weighed those four against a risk to the school which I judged to be small.” He did not look away. “I judged wrong. I thought the danger was to me, and I have never been able to take that as seriously as I should. It didn’t enter my head that a child who had nothing to do with any of it would come up from the lake on a Sunday afternoon and eat a sweet.”

Neither of them spoke. One of the silver instruments let off a small puff of steam, and went on turning.

“I let Severus’s account stand. I shall go on letting it. Madam Pomfrey has written down what she found, and it’s in that drawer; it says *poison*, in capitals, and my name is on the cover, so that whoever opens it need waste no time wondering who shut it. I shall go to Mrs Goyle myself, on the first day that I can do it without burying two more children on her account. I don’t expect to be asked to sit down.”

He had not raised his voice or lowered it. He had simply set the thing on the desk with the rest. Harry thought that he had never heard anyone admit to so much in such good order, and that it was not one ounce the lighter for it; and he heard his own voice come out quite calm.

“You did it last time.”

The blue eyes sharpened.

“In the other one. It wasn’t her, it was him, and he was no good at it. There was a necklace. It got a girl in my team, in the lane coming back from Hogsmeade; she was in St Mungo’s till the spring. Then there was a bottle of mead, and that was Ron. I got a bezoar down him on a hearthrug.” He could see the hearthrug. “You knew all that year, too. You let it run. And they both lived, so nobody ever had to think about it.”

“No,” said Dumbledore, after a moment. “I don’t suppose that I did.” He folded his good hand over the other. “Thank you. That’s a thing I couldn’t have learnt from anybody else.”

He waited for that to be satisfying.

“And you, Harry?”

“What?”

“You have seen this year before. You’ve just told me so. You knew what was asked of the Malfoy in your form at sixteen; you had the necklace, and the mead, and a hearthrug. I understand that there is a notebook.” He said it without any edge at all. “Is it in the notebook?”

He opened his mouth.

*Mon. Left 10.10, alone.* He could have recited it. He had put sleeves in it, and half a roll, and the hours of an east staircase; he had filled forty pages with a girl going from one room to another, in a very small hand, with the curtains drawn. He had written *Long sleeves* and underlined it. He had known since August what was on her arm, and for a great deal longer than that what the heir of that house was given to do with his sixth year; and he had never once put the two things on the same line.

“She isn’t him,” said Harry.

“No. Was that the reason?”

It was the gentleness that did it. He would have liked very much to be shouted at.

“I didn’t want it to be her,” he said. “And I was busy.”

“With what?”

“Minding.”

Dumbledore nodded slowly, as at a sum that had come out.

“Then there are two of us in this room who looked away,” he said, “and one girl under the ground in the north. I shan’t insult you by dividing it.”

He let that lie. When he went on, it was about something else.

“It was not *Diffindo*.”

“No.”

“I had hoped that you would come and take that back before you had need to.” He glanced at the cuffs, once; he was the only person all evening who had left it at once. “There is something in you that was pleased tonight. I don’t ask. I know what you carry.”

“It sounded like me.”

“I expect it did. It has lived with you a long while. I am going to say two things, and I shall ask you to carry both of them, since neither will carry the other. It is not you. And it did not learn that curse out of a book, or keep a notebook through the autumn, or go up two flights of stairs this afternoon with a speech ready. That was a boy of whom I am very fond.”

*Fond.* He had counted that word once, on a floor in Grimmauld Place, at three in the morning.

“It hears,” said Harry. “Her arm.”

“Yes.”

“You knew that as well.”

“Severus guessed in the autumn. He told me.”

“In the *autumn*.” It went up in spite of him. “I’ve had six months of— I thought she’d sold me. I stood up in the Hall and I— You could have *said!*”

“Would it have helped you to know?”

“*Yes!*”

“I’m sure of it. Would it have helped her?” The lamp had not wavered. “A month ago you opened a door that you had been taught to keep shut, in order to tell Tom what you thought of him; and he sent you the account at breakfast, and she paid it. What you know, Harry, you cannot yet be relied upon to keep. She has understood that since the summer. It is why she said nothing. I didn’t presume to know better than she did.”

He had nothing. He stood on the carpet with his wand in his sleeve, where he had been told to carry it, and looked at the box.

“I shall ask one thing of you,” said Dumbledore. “I shall ask it once, and I can’t give you the reason, and I know what it is to ask that of you tonight of all nights.” His left hand had moved in his lap. “When the time comes, it must be he who does it. Not you.”

The fire ticked.

He knew those words. He had never had them said to his face. He’d had them at second hand, in another life, out of a dead man’s memory, in this room, with his head in a basin: *and Voldemort himself must do it, Severus. That is essential.* He had been seventeen, and he had got up off this floor and gone downstairs.

“You said that in the other one,” said Harry. “I went.”

“And?”

“I don’t know. It stops.”

Dumbledore regarded him over the half-moons; and what was in his face then Harry would have given a good deal not to have seen, because it was not certainty.

“Yes,” he said. “So you have always told me.” He drew the box an inch nearer to the lemon drops. “Go to bed, Harry. And wash your hands.”
