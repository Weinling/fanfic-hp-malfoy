# Chapter 50: Pretext

Her father’s owl came on the morning of the match, and she opened the letter in the changing room, alone, with her Quidditch robes half on.

It was not a long letter. That was the first thing wrong with it. Her father wrote long letters, as he talked: with turns and returns, and asides about Draco, and a joke about the Minister in the third paragraph. This one was a single side, and the hand was his, the beautiful sloping hand, but it had been written fast, and twice the nib had caught and spattered, and he had not troubled to blot it.

*My darling,*

*I write in haste, and you will burn this when you have read it.*

*Our master has been obliged to leave the house in the north. You will know which I mean, and you will not name it, even to yourself. It was being watched. Dumbledore’s people, on the cliffs, in the fog, for some days before anybody saw them. He was very angry. We have all been very much questioned.*

*Somebody talked. There is no other way it could have been known; that house has been in no register for a hundred years. He has asked every one of us who could have known the word, and who we could have told. I have answered for myself and for your mother.*

*I tell you this because you are at that school, among Dumbledore’s people, and I would have you be more careful than you have ever been in your life. Say nothing. Write nothing. Trust nobody who is not of this family.*

*Your father.*

Under the signature, smaller, as if he had come back to it after the ink was dry:

*Draco writes that he is happy. Keep him so.*

Lyra read it to the end. Then she read the postscript again, by itself.

Outside the door, she could hear the stands filling, a great low roar like the sea, and somebody in the corridor shouting for the Slytherin team. She folded the letter along its creases, very neatly, and put it up the left sleeve of her robes, against the inside of her wrist, where she could feel the edge of it; and finished dressing; and tied back her hair, as she had tied it back for every match since the November she was twelve; and picked up her broom, and went out.

⁂

It was the first Saturday of November, cold and bright, with a high thin wind that came off the lake in gusts and set every banner in the stands cracking like a whip. The whole school was out. The Slytherin stand was a wall of green and silver; somebody had brought a great silk serpent that writhed along the front of it on poles. And at the Gryffindor end, in front of the left-hand goal, very tall and very red about the ears, Weasley hung in the air on a broom with the shine still on it, guarding the hoops for the first time in his life.

The Slytherins had a song.

Lyra had heard it in the common room on the Thursday, being practised: a sixth-year had written it over the weekend, and it was about Weasley, and his family, and a bin, and it had a chorus that everybody could join in, and it was quite clever in the way that very cruel things often are. By the time the teams were in the air, half the Slytherin stand was singing it. The words came across the pitch in the wind in pieces, and every piece found Weasley, and she watched each one land.

He let in a goal in the second minute. He let in another in the fifth. The chorus went round again, louder.

On the front of her robes, under the Seeker’s badge, there was a new one, silver, with an *I* on it, that Professor Umbridge had pinned there herself on the Thursday with a little speech about responsibility, and that made her a member of something called the Inquisitorial Squad. She had not yet worked out what the Squad was for. She suspected that was the point of it.

Lyra hung above the pitch on her Firebolt, high, in the cold, where the Seekers hung, and looked for the Snitch, and listened to it.

She did not sing it. She had never sung anything in a stand in her life. She did not stop it either. She was the Slytherin Seeker, and a prefect, and Lucius Malfoy’s daughter, and she had stood up in front of the whole common room in June and told them that in this House nobody lowered his voice; and they were not lowering it. She could have flown down to the front of the stand and said one word, and it would have stopped. She knew it would. She stayed where she was, and let it go round, and round, and told herself that it was only Weasley, and only a song, and that she had more important things up her sleeve.

On the other side of the pitch, level with her, Potter was not looking for the Snitch. He was looking at the Slytherin stand, and then at Weasley, and then at her.

⁂

She saw the Snitch first.

It was low, by the Slytherin goalposts, a little gold glint in the grass-light, and she was diving before she had finished seeing it, flat to the Firebolt, with the wind tearing at her tied-back hair and the stands going past in a long green blur. She heard the crowd see it too. She heard the roar change. And she heard, behind her and to the right, the other Firebolt.

He was faster. He always had been, on the same broom; he had the lighter build, and he did not mind dying. They went down the last sixty feet side by side, so close that their robes cracked against each other in the wind, so close that she could see his face, set and white, with his eyes on the Snitch and his good hand stretched out in front of him; and the Snitch jinked left, and they both went with it, and his shoulder came against hers, hard, and her sleeve rode up on her arm.

The letter shifted against her wrist. Its folded corner showed above the cuff.

Then his hand closed on the Snitch a foot in front of hers, and he pulled up so steeply that the Firebolt stood on its tail, and the Gryffindor stand went up like a firework, and it was over.

She pulled out of the dive at the bottom, a yard above the grass, and sat there on her broom for a moment, breathing, with the noise coming down on her from all sides. Her sleeve had fallen back over her wrist. She put her hand on it.

Potter came round in a slow loop above her with the Snitch fluttering in his fist, and did not look like a boy who had just won a Quidditch match. He looked down at her sleeve, and then at her, as he had looked at her in a dark classroom when she said *No*; and then the Gryffindors reached him, and he was gone under a pile of red.

⁂

Weasley caught her outside the changing rooms.

He came across the grass at her with his broom still in his hand and his face the colour of the Gryffindor banners, and Granger behind him, half running, saying “Ron—*Ron*—” and not catching up.

“Happy?” said Weasley.

Lyra stopped. She had her broom over her shoulder and her father’s letter up her sleeve, and she had just lost to Potter in front of the whole school by a foot, and she was not happy.

“About what, Weasley? You won.”

“*Harry* won. I let in four.” His voice cracked on it. “Your lot made sure of that. That song. You heard it. You were right *above* it. You could’ve—”

“I don’t write songs. I leave that to people with time on their hands. You might try it yourself. It would give you something to do in the evenings. I’m told they’re very long at the bottom of the league.”

It came out quite easily, in the same voice as the sixth-year in the common room. Weasley’s hand went to his wand.

Granger got between them.

She did not do it gracefully. She came round Weasley’s side at a run and stopped in front of him, with her back to him and her face to Lyra, a foot away, with her bushy hair coming loose in the wind and a Gryffindor scarf wound three times round her neck; and Weasley’s wand, half out, stopped against her shoulder blade.

“You didn’t sing it,” said Granger.

Lyra looked at her.

“I was watching. I watch you at matches; I have done since second year. You didn’t sing it, not one word. You just hung there.” Granger’s voice was very level, and her face was very pink, and she did not look away. “You could have stopped it. You know you could. You did it on the train with Parkinson in about a minute; half of Ravenclaw had heard about it by breakfast. You didn’t, today.” She took a breath. “I don’t know which of those is worse, Malfoy. I really don’t. I’ve been trying to work it out since the second goal.”

Nobody said anything.

Then Granger turned round, and took Weasley by the arm, wand and all, and walked him away across the grass towards the castle, talking to him low and fast; and he went, looking back once, with an expression Lyra had not seen on him before and did not want to see again.

She stood there with her broom on her shoulder.

Granger had watched her at every match since second year. Lyra tried to find something wrong with what she had said.

⁂

Draco had sung it.

She found that out in the common room before dinner, where the whole House was still in the warm loud glow of a match lost by a foot and a Weasley humiliated by four goals, which was nearly as good as a win and in some ways better. Draco was on the hearthrug in the middle of a knot of first-years, with his face scarlet from the cold and a green-and-silver rosette the size of a dinner plate pinned to his jumper, and he was singing the chorus, all of it, at the top of his voice, a little behind everybody else and very slightly out of tune.

He saw her come in, and broke off, and came across the room at a run.

“Did you *hear* us? Did you hear the end bit? We did it right by the hoops, every time he—” He stopped, out of breath, grinning up at her. “You didn’t sing. I looked. Why didn’t you sing?”

*Keep him so.*

Lyra looked down at him. At the rosette, and the gap in his teeth, and the cold-red face, and the happiness coming off him like heat off a fire; at her brother, who was eleven, and had a list folded against his heart from an old man who ran errands for the Dark Lord, and had just spent an afternoon learning that it was a fine thing to sing about a boy and a bin until the boy let in four goals.

She could have told him. She could have knelt down on the hearthrug, in front of the whole House, and told him what Granger had told her on the grass. He would have listened. He always listened to her, in the end.

“I was busy. Looking for the Snitch.”

“You lost.”

“I know, Draco.”

“By *that* much.” He held up a finger and thumb, very close together. “Next time you’ll get him.” He was already turning back to the hearthrug, to the first-years and the song. “Next time, Lyra.”

He went back to singing. She let him.

She stood by the door of the common room with her broom still over her shoulder and her father’s letter still up her sleeve, and watched her little brother be happy, at the top of his voice, about a thing she would not have sung for a hundred Galleons; and kept him so.

Snape was waiting for her at the foot of the dungeon steps that evening, after rounds, with no candle, in the dark, so that she did not see him until he spoke.

“Miss Malfoy.”

She stopped on the bottom step.

“It is not Thursday,” said Snape. “But I think we will not wait for Thursday. Come.”

His office was cold; he had let the fire go out. He did not light it. He shut the door, and stood with his back to it, in the dark, with only the faint green glow of the jars along the walls to see him by.

“Your father has written to you,” he said. It was not a question.

She did not answer.

“I was in a room on Tuesday night,” said Snape, “in a house that is not the one in the north, and I heard a good many people asked a good many questions about a word. *North.* A word that perhaps a dozen people in the world could have said aloud and had it mean anything. He asked each of us, in turn, who had known it, and whom we might have told.”

The jars glowed. Somewhere under the floor, the lake moved.

“Your father answered for himself. And for your mother. Most fully. He did not answer for you.” He paused. “He did not think to.”

Lyra stood very still in the dark.

*I have answered for myself and for your mother.* She had read it in the changing room and thought: *of course*. He had trusted her to give him Potter’s secrets and never thought she might give Potter his.

“Five names were given,” said Snape quietly. “By various people. Mine was among them.”

She looked up.

“Do not concern yourself.” His voice was dry as paper. “I have been answering questions in that room since before you were born. I shall answer these. But I should like you to understand, Miss Malfoy, what has been spent, and by whom, and on what.” He looked at her across the dark office for a long moment. “I do not ask whom you told.”

“Sir—”

“I do not *ask*.” He opened the door behind him. The torchlight from the passage fell in across the floor between them. “I shall add it to the account. The lake. The window. And now this.” He stood aside to let her pass. “I keep very careful accounts, Miss Malfoy. You would be surprised how long I can wait to be paid.”

She went past him, out into the torchlight, and up the passage, and through the wall into the common room, where the sixth-year who had written the song was singing it again by the fire, to a great deal of laughter; and she went straight past him, and up the stairs, and did not stop.

In the dormitory, behind her hangings, she took the letter out of her sleeve and burnt it, as she had been told, with the tip of her wand, a corner at a time, and watched her father’s hand go brown and curl and go.

The postscript went last. *Draco writes that he is happy. Keep him so.*

⁂

By the Sunday morning she had looked at it from the other side.

*He did not answer for you. He did not think to.* On the Saturday night she had held on to that as one holds on to a rail. But five names had been given in that room, and there was somebody in it who asked everybody everything, in turn, and was in no hurry. Sooner or later he would get to the schoolgirl. He would ask what she had been doing for him since September. And the answer was that she had told Dolores Umbridge a handful of true things not worth a Knut; and that Professor Umbridge, who wrote to Wiltshire on the first of every month, had begun to call her *reliable* in the tone one uses of a quiet horse.

She needed to have done something. It had to be real, and it had to have cost somebody, and there had to be a letter about it on her father’s desk by the first of December.

There was Diggory’s group, which two sixth-years had been arguing about on the stairs. She looked at that for some time and put it back. It would bring the castle down on half the sixth year, and she might yet need it.

And there was what Crabbe had told her on the Friday, in the dormitory, in her low reasonable voice, looking at the floor. Her cousin again. One of the Hufflepuff boys had started keeping a list: who had been in that office, and for how long, and what was on their hands. He had nine names so far. When it was long enough he meant to send it to the Governors. *I just thought you’d want to know*, Crabbe had said, and had very nearly smiled. *Being a prefect.* She had seen the jar. She thought she knew which side of this Lyra was on.

It was a very small thing. That was what recommended it. It was one boy and a notebook. He would get a week of lines and a letter home. People got lines.

She knew what lines were. She knew it with the ends of her fingers, from the knuckles to the wrist. She noticed that, and went on doing up her buttons.

⁂

“Wayne Hopkins,” said Lyra.

It was ten minutes to eight on the Monday morning, and the kittens were at their breakfast. It took no time at all. She had wondered whether it would.

“Hopkins, dear?”

“A fifth-year in Hufflepuff, Professor. He is keeping a list of the pupils who have been in detention with you, and of their injuries. I understand that he means to write to the Governors. It may be nothing.”

Professor Umbridge put down her little pink cup. She looked at Lyra with her head on one side, and her wide mouth spread very slowly, until she looked like somebody who has opened a parcel and found exactly the right size of cardigan.

“Oh, you *are* a good girl,” she said. “I shall write to your father this very evening. One never likes to say so, but I had begun to wonder.” She patted Lyra’s hand. Hers was slightly damp. “You’ll sit with me, won’t you, dear? Tomorrow at five. I do think it’s so important to see a thing through.”

“Of course, Professor.”

She said it at once.

⁂

He said sorry when he came in.

He had caught the door with his bag, and it bumped the leg of the second chair, which was pink, and which Umbridge had had brought in and set beside her own with a small table between them. He said “Sorry” before he had seen who was in it. He had grown a foot since the queue outside Charms. He had a long, mild, rather spotty face, and his tie was done up properly, and in five years she had spoken to him perhaps twice. Then he saw her, and her badge, and the plate on the small table, and he looked at Umbridge, and she watched him work out what kind of room he was in.

The notebook was already on the desk. Umbridge turned its pages with the tips of her fingers. She read some of the names aloud, with little sighs, beginning at the top: *Potter, H.* Something slid out from between the leaves and fell on to the lace cloth, and she picked it up, and held it out to him across the desk.

“Yours, dear. What a funny bookmark.”

It was a Chocolate Frog card. It had been bent in half, a long time ago, and somebody had flattened it out again as well as could be done. Agrippa was frowning. There was a mark across his face about the size of the heel of a small shoe.

Hopkins took it and put it in his pocket. He did not look at her. It would have been easier, she found, if he had.

“*I must not tell tales*,” said Professor Umbridge. “Shall we say until it sinks in? Do have a wafer, dear.”

They were pink. Lyra had not eaten anything to speak of since the Saturday; Goyle had been trying for two days. She took one, since she was being watched, and bit it. Across the room, at the table under the window, Wayne Hopkins picked up the quill and began.

She knew the sound now.

It was thin, and wet, and it went on. At about the fourth line he stopped, and turned his hand over, and looked at the back of it, with his mild face gone slack and puzzled, like somebody who has put his foot on a stair that was not there. Nobody had told him. Nobody ever told them. He looked up at Umbridge, who smiled at him and lifted her cup. He bent his head again.

Lyra finished the wafer. She ate all of it. It seemed that one could.

He came on the Wednesday, and on the Thursday. By the Wednesday he was holding the quill in his fist. By the Thursday he had to lift it with his other hand and fit it between his fingers, and when it dripped on the pink cloth he said “Sorry,” quite quietly, to the cloth. There was a perfectly good third shelf in the Potions storeroom. She walked past the door twice. It would have gone in the book, in her hand, and there was a woman with a black bow sitting eighteen inches from her elbow, and she had told the only other person in the world who knew about that shelf to keep the jar.

At six o’clock Umbridge took the quill away herself, out of fingers that had stopped closing on it, and said she really thought it had sunk in.

On the Friday morning Cedric Diggory was cutting up Wayne Hopkins’s sausage.

He did it without any expression at all, very neatly, in the middle of the Hufflepuff table, with the Head Boy’s badge on his chest. Hopkins’s right hand was under the table. Perhaps a dozen people had noticed. Lyra noticed Potter notice. She saw him put down his toast.

He got up and came across the Great Hall, not quickly, between the tables, and the noise went down a little behind him, as it had done all his life. He stopped behind Hopkins. He felt in his robes and took out a small stoppered jar.

She knew it from where she sat. Third shelf on the left; one ounce. It was about a quarter full. He had been carrying it for five weeks.

He set it down on the table, a little way from Hopkins’s plate. Then he put one finger against it and pushed it along the wood, an inch, and another inch, until it stood exactly at the edge of the plate; and he took his finger away.

There were two people in that room who knew what they had just seen. He lifted his head, and looked along the length of the Hall, past Ravenclaw, and found her.

He did not look angry. She had told him once, in an Owlery, that he did not look at any of them as he looked at her. He had always looked at her as though he had no idea what she would do next. He looked at Snape like this. He looked at Umbridge like this. He looked at her for about as long as it takes to read a line one already knows, and then he went back to his toast.

Lyra buttered hers. It wanted doing. Beside her, Crabbe had put down her fork.

“I told you that,” said Crabbe.

It was very low. It was not a question. Lyra went on with the butter, right out to the corners. After a moment Crabbe picked up her plate and her cup, and moved three places down the bench, and sat down next to a second-year with her back very straight.

Goyle looked from one of them to the other. Her big soft face had gone quite frightened. She took hold of the toast rack and pushed it nearer to Lyra.

“Eat something,” said Goyle.

He was in the window on the sixth floor that night. She had not moved her rounds. It would have looked like something.

He was not sitting. He stood in the embrasure with the moon behind him and his hands at his sides, and she got out her book, because it was something to hold.

“You’re out of bed, Potter.”

“He can’t hold a spoon.”

“I saw.”

“I know you saw. He told me. He says you were in the other chair, all three nights.” His voice was quite quiet. “He says you had a biscuit.”

She had got ready for shouting. She had been ready for it since breakfast. She opened the book, and looked at the page, and heard herself.

“Three,” said Lyra. “She’s very pressing.”

It took a moment to go in. She watched it. It was one of the worst moments of her life, and she would have given a good deal to say it again.

“Who told her?”

“I should think you know.”

“I want you to say it.”

“I told her.” She looked up. “On Monday, before breakfast. His name, his year and what he had in his notebook. Should you like it in writing? I have a very clear hand.”

“*Why?*”

She had not meant to tell him. She had meant to have a great deal of dignity and no reasons whatever. But he had looked at her that morning down the length of the Great Hall as if she were something he had read before, and she found that she was so angry she could hardly see.

“Because somebody gave a house in Northumberland to Albus Dumbledore,” said Lyra, “and his people went and stood about on the cliffs in the fog, like a party of bird-watchers, and were seen. Did you imagine that that would be the end of it? He has been going round the table, Potter. *Who knew the word? Whom did you tell?* There are five names. My father answered for himself and for my mother. Would you care to guess who he didn’t answer for?”

She saw that reach him. He had not known; of course he had not known. It went across his face, and for an instant he looked as he had looked on the third floor with his hand held out.

“You should’ve told me.”

“So that you could do what? Keep me safe inside it?” Her voice had begun to shake, and she flattened it. “You wanted the north. You said *please*. I gave it to you, and this is what it cost, and it has a name, and it’s in Hufflepuff. What did you think it cost? Did you suppose it was free?”

“Don’t you put that on me.”

“Why not? You’ve plenty of room. You let a girl write to a book for a year so that you’d know what came next. How many was it, all told? Have you got a list? I should get one, they’re all the rage. You’ll forgive me if I don’t take lessons in who can be spent from *you*.”

“He hadn’t done anything to—”

“He’s *nobody!*”

It rang along the corridor and came back.

“He’s nobody, Potter. He’s a boy with a notebook and a hand that will heal, and I have a brother—”

She did not see him move. That was what she remembered afterwards. There had been a yard of moonlit floor between them. Then the book was on the flagstones, and her back had met the stone beside the window with a thump that knocked the breath out of her, and he had her by the wrist.

“Take that back,” said Potter.

He said it very quietly.

She was eleven. There was a corridor, and Crabbe on the floor, and her own wand in his other hand, held low and loose; and she would have told anybody that she had forgotten about it years ago. Her wrist had not. It seemed that it had been waiting. The blood went out of her face all at once, and she could not find her knees, and she could not get any air. She was fifteen. She was a prefect, with a wand up her sleeve. She stood against the wall and looked at him, and she could not have lifted a finger.

It was the same voice. They were the same three words.

His hand was right round her wrist, and the moon was on the back of it. *I must not tell lies*, from the knuckles down; and underneath it, smaller, the other three. They were about an inch from her pulse. She could read them perfectly well.

She saw him see her face.

It took him a moment. He looked at her. Then he looked down at his own hand, slowly, as if somebody had brought it to him; and there were red marks coming up on her skin at the edges of his fingers.

He let go of her as though she had burnt him. He went back a step, and another, until the far wall stopped him, and he stood there holding the hand a little away from his side. She had seen him after the dragon. She had never seen him that colour.

“Lyra,” said Potter.

There did not seem to be any air on the sixth floor. She could hear him looking for it.

“I didn’t— I wouldn’t ever—”

She bent down and picked up her book. It took some time. She found the page, and took out her quill, and stood with it over the line, as she had stood on a good many nights that term with her hair coming down. The ink dried on the nib while she looked at it. There did not seem to be a word for it on page nine.

She shut the book.

“Aren’t you—” It had hardly any voice in it. He had got his back against the wall, and his eyes had gone to the book in her hand, and she understood that he was asking her to. He actually wanted her to. “Malfoy. Aren’t you going to—”

She drew down her cuff and did up the button. She went along the corridor to the stairs, at the ordinary pace of a prefect on her rounds, with her wrist held in her other hand, inside the sleeve, where she had once carried letters.

Nobody lost any points that night.

Nobody lost any on the Saturday, or on the Sunday. On the Monday morning the rubies stood exactly where they had stood on the Friday, for the first time since the start of term; and she heard Weasley explaining to Thomas, all down the length of the Gryffindor table, with his ears pink with quiet pride, that it just went to show what you could do with a graph.
