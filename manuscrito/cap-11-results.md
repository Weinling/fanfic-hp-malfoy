# Chapter 11: Results

Professor Lupin was indisposed, Professor Snape informed them on Monday afternoon, and in his absence they would have to make do with him.

There was a small murmur of disappointment from the back of the room, which surprised Lyra and seemed to surprise Snape rather more. The Slytherins liked Snape. He was their Head of House, and in living memory he had never taken a point from any of them for anything short of arson. But Lupin’s lessons had turned out to be the best Defence lessons any of them had ever had, which after Quirrell and Lockhart was admittedly not saying much, and in two months of boggarts and Red Caps and a tank of Grindylows nobody had once had to take refuge on top of a desk. Snape let the murmur die of its own accord. Then he opened the textbook at the very back.

“Professor Lupin, I am sure, means to work through this book in order,” he said. “I see no reason to wait for him. Page three hundred and ninety-four.”

It was werewolves. Nobody had read it, since nobody had expected to reach it before the summer, and Snape did not appear to care. He taught it for the next hour as Lyra had never seen him teach anything, not even Potions, with a cold, exact relish that seemed to come from somewhere a good way below the lesson. A werewolf changed at the full moon and at no other time. Its bite passed the condition on. There was no cure and there had never been one. It could be told from a true wolf, by anybody who kept their head, by the snout, which was a shade narrower, and by the eyes, and by the set of the skull, which was a little more like a man’s than any wolf’s had a right to be. He drew the two skulls side by side on the blackboard and made them copy both. He asked Millicent Bulstrode to tell him the difference between them, and took no points when she could not, but looked at her in such a way that Millicent went on not knowing for the rest of the afternoon.

By the end of the hour several of the Slytherins, who had spent two years honestly puzzled by the things the other Houses said about Snape, were wearing the faces of people to whom something had at last been explained.

“A roll of parchment,” said Snape, as they packed their bags, “on how a werewolf may be recognised. For Monday.”

“A whole roll, sir?” said one of the Warrington cousins. “It’s the last chapter, we haven’t even—”

“It is necessary.” Snape let that sit long enough for a mutiny to start gathering at the back, and added, “The Gryffindors have two.”

The mutiny dispersed. “Well, one’s not so bad,” somebody said, and somebody else said, “Library tomorrow, after Herbology?”, and the room broke up into its usual knots and began to drift out.

Lyra took her time over her bag.

It had been a very satisfactory weekend, on the face of it. Slytherin had won by two hundred and forty points, which Flint had informed the whole common room was the most beautiful number he had ever seen, and Parkinson had dried his banner out in front of the fire and hung it over the mantelpiece, where it still said MAIFOY, and she had been congratulated by people who had not spoken to her since first year. Madam Hooch had had to prise the Snitch out of her fingers. Outside the common room, though, the match had turned into something else. By Sunday lunchtime it was a story about Potter, who had faced a hundred Dementors at thirteen and conjured a Patronus at them, a real one, people said, though nobody below the clouds had seen more than a flash of white; and the Gryffindors went about the corridors looking less like a team that had just been thrashed than like one that had been robbed by the weather. Nobody had seen what happened at the top of the stands, in that rain. Lyra had told nobody. She found she did not want to.

When the last of them had gone she went up to the front, where Snape was wiping the skulls off the blackboard in short, hard strokes.

“Professor.”

“Miss Malfoy.” He did not turn round. “If this concerns the length of the essay, I am not open to negotiation.”

“It’s about Sirius Black.”

The cloth stopped. When he turned, there was a look on his face she had never seen there before, not even for Potter: a hatred so old and so complete that there seemed to be no room in him for anything else, and under it a kind of disgust, as if the name had a smell. Then it was gone, and he was only Professor Snape again, sallow and tired, setting the cloth down on the desk.

He put two fingers to his temple and stood for some seconds as though weighing something up. Then he gathered his books. “Not here,” he said. “Come with me.”

His office was down in the dungeons, a cold stone room lined with shelves of jars in which pale, soft things hung suspended in green and amber, and Lyra had been in it perhaps three times in two years and liked it better each time. Snape looked out into the corridor before he shut the door. Then he drew his wand and murmured something at it, and a faint buzzing set in at the edges of the room, like a bee trapped behind a curtain. Lyra knew that sound. Her father’s study made it, on the evenings when he did not want to be overheard.

Snape did not sit down. “Now,” he said. “Why do you want to know?”

“Why wouldn’t I?” Lyra kept her voice light. “He broke into the school. He walked up to Gryffindor Tower on Hallowe’en as though he lived there. I should think anyone in Slytherin might like to know whose side he’s on.”

It was true as far as it went, and she let the rest stand behind it. The Malfoys had been where everybody knew they had been, Imperius or no Imperius, and a Malfoy asking whose side Sirius Black was on was a Malfoy asking whether he was one of theirs. Snape would take it that way. She meant him to.

“Is that all?” He was watching her very steadily. “I advise you to tell me the truth, Lyra. If this is something your father should know about, I shall be obliged to write to him.”

In lessons he called her Miss Malfoy. In here, it seemed, she was Lyra.

“My father knows already,” she said. “I asked him. He said you might know more than he does.”

Snape looked at her then, properly, straight into her eyes, and she looked back, because she had been brought up never to be the first to look away, and kept her face as still as she had kept it in front of her father in the summer. There was the smallest sensation imaginable, like a fingernail flicked against a wineglass somewhere at the back of her head, and it was over at once. Snape’s brows drew together.

“You have a natural—” he said, and stopped.

“Sir?”

“Nothing.” He was still looking at her, but differently, as though she had turned out to be heavier than she looked. “Remember how that felt. One day it may be of use to you.”

She did not know what he meant, and he did not seem disposed to tell her.

“Your father named me,” he said.

“Yes, sir.”

“Then he will hardly object if I write to him about this conversation.”

“Of course not,” said Lyra.

He considered her a moment longer. Then he went and stood by the shelves, half turned away from her, and when he spoke again it was in the level voice he used for a method on the board.

“Do you know what the Fidelius Charm is?”

“No, sir. It isn’t on the syllabus.”

“It would not be. It is old and very complex, and few people alive could cast it. A secret is hidden inside a single living soul, the Secret Keeper, and until the Keeper chooses to give it away, nobody can find it. Not by spying, and not by force.” He paused. “Twelve years ago the Dark Lord decided that the Potters must die. Why is not your concern. Dumbledore learned of it, and hid them under the Fidelius, and the Keeper they chose was Sirius Black.”

Lyra had known for years that Black had sold them; her father had told her so, in the voice he kept for other people’s scandals. She had never asked how it was done. Nobody had ever said that there was no one else who could have done it.

“Black was James Potter’s closest friend. They were in the same year here, and they went about together, the two of them and their little gang, making other people’s lives a misery and being thought enormously amusing for it.” His mouth twisted slightly. “You may have noticed that the son has inherited the vocation.”

Lyra had noticed.

“The Potters trusted Black with their lives. A week later the Dark Lord came to their house in person.” His voice had gone perfectly flat, as if everything had been drained out of it and only the words left behind. “He killed them both. And then he was gone.”

He said it without a flicker. But his right hand, down at his side, had closed so hard that the knuckles stood out like stones, and Lyra, watching it, cast about for a reason and found one. He had been loyal once; everybody knew that, though nobody said it. The Dark Lord had fallen that night and taken a great many things down with him. She had never thought of Professor Snape as one of the ones who minded. Evidently he was. And if it ever came to minding again, if the Dark Lord ever came back and began asking after the things he had left in other people’s keeping, men who minded like this would be the ones who came and asked her father. Her father still thought he had lost an old book. He would answer like a man who had lost a book.

“The rest you know,” said Snape. “A day later Black was cornered in a Muggle street, and killed twelve Muggles and a wizard with a single curse. The Ministry took him there in the street. He was laughing. There was no trial; nobody saw the need of one. They sent him to Azkaban for life, and it appears that was not enough. The Dementors ought to have been given him that same day. Had they—” He stopped. “Lyra?”

She had been on a broom above the stands again, just for a moment, with the cold coming up at her. “Nothing, sir.” She put it aside. “Then he was the Dark Lord’s man? He betrayed his best friend and handed him over?” And, because her father had written it too: “Some people thought he might have laid a trap. That he led the Dark Lord there on purpose, to—”

She had expected one answer or the other, and either would do. If Black had betrayed his friends, then he was a traitor; if he had betrayed the Dark Lord, he was something cleverer and worse; and either way she would know.

“What does it matter?” said Snape.

She had not expected that.

“I will tell you something worth more than the answer to that, Lyra. What a man meant, and what was in his heart while he did it, matter a great deal less than people like to believe. What matters is what came of it.” He had not raised his voice. He did not need to; the anger coming off him was cold and controlled and very nearly visible, and if Black had walked in through the door just then she thought Snape would have killed him where he stood. “The Potters are dead. The Dark Lord is gone. Whatever Black intended, neither of those things is altered by a hair.” And then, more quietly, as though he were saying it to somebody else: “So there will be no forgiving him. No seeing him in a kinder light. Whoever asks it of me.”

Lyra could not think who would. There was nobody alive who could give Professor Snape orders about anything, so far as she knew, except possibly the Headmaster, and the only other person who ever had was dead, or as good as. She was one of very few people in the castle who knew how much weight *as good as* was carrying.

She did not have the truth, then. But she had a great deal, and she turned it over with something very like glee. Potter knew that Black had betrayed his parents; everybody knew that. He did not know how. He did not know about the Fidelius, or that there had been nobody else in the world who could have given them away, or that it had taken a week. He would want this more than he had ever wanted anything from her, and for once she would not have to take what she was given; she could name her price. She kept her mouth straight with some effort.

“That is all I mean to say on the subject,” said Snape. “I trust there is nothing else.”

“Only one thing, sir. Why didn’t Dumbledore keep the secret himself?”

“He offered.” Snape said it with a contempt that was very nearly relish. “Potter would not hear of it. He had a friend he trusted more, and he was never a man to take advice from anybody he could not laugh at. The Headmaster let him have his way. He generally did.”

“And the wizard Black killed?”

A pause. “Peter Pettigrew. Another of their friends.”

“Their friend? But why would he—”

“How should I know what went on in Black’s head?”

She let it go. He wanted it finished; it was in every line of him. She had meant to ask about the brother as well, but her mother had written *you will not ask anyone else* in that small upright hand, and Lyra found she could still see the words.

“You will not go near Black,” said Snape. “You will not try to find him. If you see him, you will run, and you will come straight to me. Whatever side he may be on, we have no reason on earth to welcome him.”

“I understand.”

“And you will repeat none of this to anyone.”

“Do you take me for an idiot, sir?”

“I take you for thirteen. It bears saying more than once.” He went to the door. “Very well. You may go.”

Lyra picked up her bag. At the door she stopped, because it seemed only fair, and because she wanted to see his face.

“I ought to tell you, sir, I thought it might have been you.”

“I beg your pardon?”

“The person who let him in.” She said it pleasantly. “On Hallowe’en. He got all the way to the Fat Lady without anybody seeing him, and you can’t do that from outside, not with Dementors on every gate. Somebody inside must have helped him. One of the staff, I thought. And you know the castle better than anyone.”

To her astonishment, Snape smiled. It was a thin smile with a good deal behind it, but there was real pleasure in it too: the look he gave a potion that had come out exactly as it should.

“Clever,” he said. “And now? Whom do you suspect?”

“Nobody, now. I’m back where I started.” She shrugged. “Perhaps there isn’t anyone.”

“There is.”

She stared at him.

“I have thought so since Hallowe’en.” His lip curled. “I have suspicions, Lyra. I cannot prove them. You might look over what you were taught this afternoon.”

“Werewolves?”

“Werewolves. Write me the roll of parchment I asked for, and write it properly, and I think that whatever the rest of them make of it, you will notice something.”

“Couldn’t you simply tell me?”

“I have a position in this school. I cannot go about repeating a suspicion I am unable to prove.” He opened the door for her, and the buzzing went out of the room like water out of a basin. “But if you should arrive at it by yourself, that would hardly be my doing.”

She understood perfectly well what she was being asked to do, and that whatever she found, it would not be Professor Snape who had found it. It was very flattering. She suspected that was the point.

“Go on,” said Snape. Then, as she stepped out into the corridor: “Lyra.”

She turned.

“You did not let the weather decide it. On Saturday.”

The door shut. Lyra stood looking at it. Half the school had congratulated her since Saturday, and Flint had shaken her hand until it hurt, and not one of them had said that.

She was halfway up the dungeon steps before the rest of it caught up with her. Her father had written that Severus might know more, and in the very next line that he would much prefer she put the whole matter out of her mind. She had just given Snape the first half. One letter from him, and her father would know she had done nothing of the kind; and whether that letter was ever written was now entirely up to Snape. She had gone in to collect something, and she had come out owing.

She went down to the dormitory first. Crabbe was there on her own, lying on her bed with a Transfiguration book propped on her chest, and she looked up as Lyra came in and then, being Crabbe, looked down again.

“Where does Greer keep her chocolate?”

“Bottom of her trunk. Under the jumpers.” Crabbe turned a page. “Take the dark. She’ll notice if the milk goes.”

Lyra had never seen the point of dark chocolate, which was bitter and tasted faintly of medicine, but it was what Professor Lupin had handed round on the train, after the Dementor. She found it, a large slab of Honeydukes’ best in gold paper, under three jumpers and a tin of Fizzing Whizzbees, and straightened up with it.

“It isn’t for me,” she said.

“I didn’t think it was,” said Crabbe, and turned another page.

Lyra decided not to dignify that, and went.

Her plan, on the way up, was a good one. Potter had been in the hospital wing since Saturday, and Madam Pomfrey, who had watched him carried in behind the Headmaster in person, was said to be keeping him one more night on principle. Lyra would go up as the winner. She would stand at the foot of his bed and look down at him, and drop the chocolate on his blankets, and remark that she understood it was what people ate after Dementors, and ask how he was feeling in a tone that made it quite plain what she thought of people who fainted. Then she would mention, carelessly, that she had spent the afternoon talking to Professor Snape about Sirius Black. She would watch his face while she said it. After that she would wait, as long as it took, for him to ask.

The door of the hospital wing stood a little open, and a great deal of noise was coming through it. Potter had visitors. She heard one of the Weasley twins saying something about *a hundred of them, Harry, a hundred*, and Wood’s voice over the top, hoarse and earnest, explaining to nobody in particular that it wasn’t over, that if Slytherin dropped points against Ravenclaw and they beat Hufflepuff by enough—; and somebody, Granger by the sound of it, saying that he might have been killed, did none of them understand that, he might have been *killed*; and then Potter laughing, the ordinary careless laugh she had heard across a hundred classrooms.

Lyra stopped outside with the chocolate in her hand and went over it once more.

Potter knew what Black was. He had said it himself in Hogsmeade, *one cowardly traitor*, and on Hallowe’en he had gone looking for him and come back saying he had just missed him. What he did not have was what she had: how it had been done, and that his father had been offered Dumbledore and had chosen his friend instead. She tried to imagine his face when he heard that, and found that she could. She had seen it twice already, the cold, quiet face he had turned on her father at the foot of the marble staircase and on her across a library table, and she did not think it would be shock and she did not think it would be grief. He would not stop to ask whether it was true. He never stopped to ask. He had walked into a paddock full of hippogriffs at Hagrid’s first lesson and bowed to the largest of them as though he knew exactly how it would go, and perhaps he had; she had wondered since, more than once, whether he had known how it would go for her as well. He had gone down into the Chamber of Secrets with a wand and nothing else.

He hated Black already; anyone would. What she was carrying up the stairs would make it certain, and personal, and Potter had never needed more than that. Tell him, and he would not miss Black again. He would go and find him, on his own, with his wand, a man who had killed thirteen people with one curse and laughed about it, and whom nobody, according to her father, understood at all.

It was nothing to her. If Potter wanted to go and get himself killed that was entirely his own affair, and she could think of several respects in which it would be very convenient.

She stood there. Her hand closed on the chocolate and opened and closed again, and the gold paper crackled. It seemed to have grown heavier on the way up. Behind the door Potter was still laughing, and she did not know how much he remembered of the top of the stands, whether he remembered her there at all or only the falling. She remembered her own fist, shut tight round the Snitch, not opening. She found she did not want to be standing at the foot of his bed when she found out.

She turned round and went back down the stairs.

Knowledge was power. She had never once known it let her down. She had more of it now than she had had at breakfast, more than Potter, more than anyone in that noisy room behind her, and all it had bought her was the long walk back down three flights of stairs with a bar of dark chocolate she did not even like.

She did not go back to the common room. She went out through the oak doors instead and down across the lawn towards the lake, with her cloak pulled round her, because she wanted to think and could not think with Parkinson’s banner looking at her. It was nearly dark. Saturday’s storm had blown itself out and left the grounds sodden and the air very cold and still, and mist lay on the water in long flat sheets with the last of the light going out behind it. She walked along the shore path with her head down and argued with herself, and lost, and started again.

She had been walking for some time before she felt she was being watched.

There was a dog on the path ahead of her, at the edge of the trees. It was black and very large, and it was standing perfectly still and looking at her, and for one cold instant Lyra thought of the Grim, which Professor Trelawney could talk about for a whole lesson at a time with her eyes swimming behind her spectacles. Her wand was in her hand at once.

Then the dog shifted its feet, and she saw it properly, and lowered the wand.

“Don’t be absurd,” she told herself aloud. “It’s a dog.”

It was certainly that. It was enormous, nearly the size of a pony, but it was so thin that it was hard to be frightened of it; she could count its ribs from where she stood, and its coat hung off it in matted ropes. Lyra had been brought up among peacocks and owls, not dogs, but she knew that anything with fur on it that still looked thin was in a very bad way.

She flapped the chocolate at it. “Shoo. Go on. Filthy, bony creature. Don’t you come near me.”

The dog did not shoo. Its eyes had gone from her face to the gold paper in her hand and followed it when she flapped it, left and right, and behind it its tail began, very slowly, to thump against the wet bracken.

“It isn’t for you,” said Lyra.

It was not for anybody, now. She looked down at it. She had carried it up three flights of stairs for somebody who did not need it, and here was something that very plainly did, and it seemed a waste to carry it all the way back down to the dungeons. She tore back the paper and broke off a large square, and then stopped with her arm drawn back to throw.

“Can dogs even eat chocolate?”

She had no idea. The dog barked, once, sharply, in a tone that suggested it had been kept waiting quite long enough.

“Fine,” said Lyra. “It’s your own lookout. If it kills you, it is not my fault.” And she threw it.

The dog took it out of the air and it was gone in one swallow, and then it stood licking its chops and looking at her with such frank and hopeful greed that she very nearly laughed. Watching it, she thought it was a good deal more likely to die for want of chocolate than from having some.

She looked at it a little longer. Then she bent and laid the rest of the slab, paper and all, on a flat stone at the side of the path, and stepped back.

She was halfway back up the lawn, with the lit windows of the castle ahead of her, when it came back to her: Potter, on the train in September, unwrapping a Chocolate Frog that nobody had offered him. *If a big black dog turns up, be nice to it. Feed it something. It’ll be starving.* She had taken it for one of his jokes. She stopped on the wet grass and looked back.

The dog had not touched the chocolate. It was standing over it at the edge of the water, a black shape against the mist, with its head up, and it was watching her.
