# Chapter 75: You Were Missed

Harry came down to breakfast on the morning of the Charms examination with both his socks on, whistling.

He had no ear for it. It was a thin, wandering, tuneless noise, somewhere in the neighbourhood of “Auld Lang Syne”, and it preceded him through the doors of the Great Hall and the whole way along the Gryffindor table. People looked up from their notes. He sat down by himself at the end of the bench, in a bar of sun, and helped himself to four sausages. Two hundred white-faced fifth- and seventh-years were moving their lips over cold toast; and Harry Potter, who had come fifth from the bottom in every Charms test since Easter, shook out his napkin and looked about him with shining pleasure, as if somebody had just that minute finished building the room.

Lyra watched him over the rim of her cup. She had not been to bed. There had seemed no point in it. She had walked her rounds and then walked them again, and at some time after three she had sat down on the top step of the fifth-floor landing, in the dark, with her chin on her knees, and had simply stayed there until the window went grey, doing nothing, feeling like a house with all its doors open. She supposed this was what people meant. She had always assumed it would be more restful.

*The worst thing I have ever done.* She had looked at that, on the step, from a number of sides. It had not gone away, and it had not got any smaller, and it stood in the corner of the landing with its hat in its hands. She found that she could look straight at it and go on smiling. She would have to see about that. She would see about it presently, when she had finished.

He had seen her. He did not look round. He picked up a sausage on the end of his fork, and regarded it, and his lips moved, and from across forty feet of hall she had no difficulty at all in reading the word.

She looked into her cup. She drank some coffee, in order to have something to do with her mouth.

The post came in at eight, with a noise like a great many wet umbrellas. There was nothing for her from Wiltshire. She had been braced for that all night: for her father’s hand, going downhill, and whatever it might have to say. It was Crabbe who pushed the thing along the table, without looking up from the Goblin Rebellions, with one finger, as one moves something that has been found in the butter.

“That was on your plate,” said Crabbe.

Lyra had not seen it arrive. No owl had brought it. It lay beside the marmalade, a single sheet folded once, and she knew what it was before she touched it, by the edge. There was a long drawer in her father’s study. The paper was thick and cream-coloured and faintly ribbed, and when she opened it, the morning went on around her exactly as before. Someone was saying something about Wit-Sharpening Potions. She could smell toast.

The writing was not one she had seen. It was small, upright and very clear; a good plain school hand, the hand of a boy who has won prizes for it, with every letter finished. There were three words in the middle of the page, and no signature.

*You were missed.*

She held it a little longer. Then she did what she had been brought up to do. She tilted it to the window, and ran her thumbnail along the fold, and looked for the gap of two fingers, and felt for the second skin of the paper; she touched it under the table with the tip of her wand. There was no gap. There was nothing underneath. It had nothing more to say to her, and it was perfectly polite.

She folded it. She put it in her sleeve. She found that she had finished her coffee.

Draco came back at noon.

They had got through the theory by then. She had written for two hours in a very good hand, about Substantive Charms, in a hall full of small square desks; and she came out with everyone else into the sun and the din of people telling each other what they had put, and went in to lunch with the Practical still to do. He was already at the table. He was not at the far end, with the first-years. He had come up the whole length of the bench, in his clean robes, with his hair combed flat, and he was sitting directly across from her place.

He had not sat across from her since the thirty-first of December. He hadn’t spoken to her since, in any sense one could use. He had passed her the salt.

She stopped. She had her hand on the back of the bench. Goyle, behind her, stopped too, and she heard Goyle draw a long breath in through her nose, and go away and sit somewhere else.

Lyra sat down.

He looked very well. That was the first thing. She had been afraid of a good many things all night and all morning, in the part of her mind that had not been sitting on a stair, and she had gone through them one at a time; she knew what a person looked like who had been at that table, and she knew the colour of it. He wasn’t any of those colours. He had a good clear skin and a clean collar. There was a boiled egg on his plate, and he had taken the top off it. He was cutting a slice of bread and butter into strips, each the same width as the last, and laying them side by side. His feet were on the floor. They hadn’t always reached.

“You weren’t there,” said Draco.

She had not heard his voice, to speak to her, since January. It had got no deeper. It was a clear small treble, and it was quite friendly.

“No,” said Lyra.

“I thought you would be. There were eight places.” He dipped a strip of bread in the egg, and ate it, and chewed it the right number of times. “I got there first. Mother did my tie. I sat down, and then Father did, and Grandfather came in with Pobble, and we waited. There was a clock. I kept looking at the door.”

The noise of the Hall had drawn off somewhat. She could hear it, a long way down the table, and the small exact sound of a knife.

“I told them you were coming,” said Draco. “I said you’d be late, because of your hair.”

She didn’t say anything. There was a place under the ribs where something had been put, very lightly, and left. She kept her face as it was. She knew how that went.

“He came in at half past. He looked at your chair. He looks at you when he comes in, and he sort of counts, doesn’t he? And he said good evening to me. He said, where is your sister?” He took another strip. “I said I didn’t know. That was true. I’d thought about it, I’d been thinking for quite a long time, and I didn’t. So he looked at me. You know how he does it; you think somebody’s come into your room. It doesn’t hurt. I don’t know why you made such a fuss about it. He looked for ages. Then he said I was a very truthful boy.”

“Draco—”

“He said I would do.”

There it was. He said it with a slight lift of the chin, and for an instant, in the middle of that clean incurious face, she saw something pleased; the look of a child who has at last been handed the correct size of cup. It went away. He dabbed his mouth with his napkin.

Her father said that. He said it at Doncaster, walking round something with his cane. It was what you said of a yearling, when you had finished running your hand down its leg.

“Do for what?” said Lyra.

It came out quite well. It had a little frown in it.

Draco shrugged. She had not seen that shrug on him before. One shoulder rose by half an inch, and sank. She had seen it across a chessboard, on the end of a very old arm.

“He didn’t say. He shook my hand. It wasn’t cold; I’d thought it would be.” He considered his egg. “We had the fish. Then we all went up to see Grandfather’s hands, and I held the lamp.”

“Grandfather,” she said. “How is he? How long—”

“How is he what?”

“He was dying. Father wrote.”

For the first time he looked at her properly, with faint surprise, and a sort of patience, as you might at somebody who has got the wrong year in a date.

“No, he wasn’t,” said Draco. “He had two helpings. Why would he be dying? He just wanted us all to come.”

The knife went down on the plate, exactly parallel with the edge. She had taught him that. He had been six.

“I asked him who Tom was,” said Draco. “You said it, that night. Well, Grandfather did. I’ve been wondering. I thought I’d ask him, since I was going to be sitting there. Father made a noise. But he didn’t mind at all. He laughed. He said Tom was a boy who had nobody, and it was the best thing that ever happened to him. He said it’s very good for one.” He pushed back the bench a little, and slid out, and stood. He picked up his bag. His tie was perfect, and he looked down at her, for a moment, from a height of about four foot six. “I’ve got to go. I’ve got Herbology. You’ve got egg on your sleeve.”

She looked. There wasn’t any.

When she raised her head he was walking away from her, down the length of the Slytherin table, not fast, with his hands at his sides. He had always run. He had run everywhere, since he was two; they had given up telling him. He went on past the first-years and he didn’t turn in there. He went straight on to the doors.

A large shape had come back along the bench, and stood behind her, looking down the Hall.

“He’s not been down,” said Goyle. “Not since Sunday. He used to come at six.” She looked at the plate, and the strips of bread laid side by side, and she picked up the one that he had left, and held it in her big fingers, and did not eat it.

She took the Practical at two. She made a wineglass sing, in tune, and she Banished a pincushion through a very small hoop, and Cheered a fretful-looking toad until it lay on its back and waved. The old witch with the ear trumpet told her it was the prettiest wandwork she had seen since Dumbledore’s.

Afterwards she went down to the bathroom with the broken tap, because nobody used it, and held her wrists under the cold water. She looked in the glass. Her face was doing what it had been told.

*He just wanted us all to come.*

Nobody had been dying. There had been a sheet of notepaper, in a shaking hand, and a horn button off a child’s coat. Somebody had laid eight places and written out the invitations and sat down to see who would come, as you might tap on a glass; and they had come, every one of them, in their good clothes. They had gone in to dinner.

She had been eleven. She knew how long a clock could go on. There was a way that you listened to corridors. She could have told him how you did it, she could have told him what you did with your hands, and at what time you stopped telling people that they were coming, and that it got to be very quiet, and that afterwards you were a different person, and rather better at things.

She tried to think how small he had been. It seemed important. She went into the room, the one she kept, with its breakfast table and the sideways light, and she looked for him going up to the Hat in September, with his feet not reaching. She knew it had happened. There it was on its shelf, a little to the left. It was a very good likeness. She could do him at four, with a hoop. That would have to serve.

Very well. She turned the tap off.

She would have to look at it. That was only sensible. She had burnt a letter, on a Sunday, on a rug. She had thought the bill would be sent to her; she had thought about that a good deal, on the step, with a certain grandeur, and she’d had her chin ready. It had simply not occurred to her that one could order a thing and have it charged to somebody who was eleven. He had seen to that, with three words on a sheet of paper, and he had known she’d look for the second layer.

And if it were Sunday, and twenty to six?

She stood with her hands on the cold edge of the basin, and she made herself ask it, in so many words. She had never in her life told herself a lie about a sum. There was a boy on a bald rug, with his nose running, holding up her coat.

The water dripped. A long way overhead somebody was running down a staircase, shouting about wineglasses.

She’d do it again.

There. That was the thing itself; that was what had been standing in the corner of the landing with its hat in its hands. She would do it again tomorrow. She’d do it knowing. She had looked at her brother across a boiled egg, and something in her had gone on quietly singing the whole time, like a wineglass, and she had not been able to make it stop, and she could not make it stop now.

That could not be said to anybody, of course. There was nobody to say it to. There was just the one person she told things to, and he was upstairs.

She dried her hands.

“*Wessex*,” said Harry, with deep satisfaction.

He was on his knees on the rug at the time, with his head on one side. The chair stood propped on *Some Lesser Houses of Wessex* and most of the first volume of something by Bagshot. It had settled a little to the left and acquired a certain raffish tilt, and when he sat in it, as he now did, it gave a groan like a dowager being helped from a carriage, and held.

“There,” said Harry. “I always knew that book was good for something. Come here.”

It was the Wednesday. He had got through Transfiguration without turning anybody into anything. He had been like this for three days. He had said good morning to Filch. He’d been heard asking Peeves how he was. He sat alone at meals, and went up alone to bed; Weasley had passed him in the corridor outside the Hall that very morning without a flicker, with a badge on his chest that was bent in the middle and had been pinned there all the same, and she’d seen Harry’s face go, and she had seen it come back. It came back like a cork. It was the most astonishing thing she had ever watched. He’d slept. There was colour in him. He looked about fifteen, and he ate as though it were a discovery. On the way out of Transfiguration, in a crowd of people, on the stairs, he had gone by behind her with his bag on his shoulder and said *Mine*, in passing, without moving his lips, in the tone of a person mentioning the weather; and she had walked straight on, with her nose in the air, and taken ten points from Dean Thomas for breathing.

She sat. There was less of the chair than there had been. He made room, and she put her feet on the fender, and for some time he didn’t say anything. He just looked at her, with his glasses crooked and his whole thin face lit up from inside like a turnip lantern. She didn’t think she could stand it. She didn’t think she could stand it if he stopped.

“What happened?” said Harry. “On Sunday. You never said. When you didn’t turn up.”

She had known he would get round to it. She had had three days. She had not known it would be today, with the window open and the smell of cut grass, and she took a breath, not a large one.

“Oh, I am a disgrace to the name,” said Lyra. “I’m to have no allowance until Michaelmas. There were four sides of it. I have broken his heart, it seems, and my mother’s, and he has been obliged to apologise for me to the whole of the south wing, and he hopes I enjoyed my Arithmancy.”

“That’s all?”

“I told them I’d fainted in Potions. It’s the first thing I have ever been known to do in Potions without a mark.”

He had stopped smiling. It was only for a moment. His eyes went over her face; she knew that look, she had taught it to him. She felt it reach the place below the ribs, and she stayed exactly where she was.

“And Draco?” said Harry. “Is he—I mean, did he have to—was he all right?”

There was a blackbird somewhere. It was going over the same four notes.

He would get up. That was what he would do; she could see it, as you can see the whole of a game. He would stop whistling. He’d go white and quiet, and go and stand on the stairs, and he’d start to count. He had had three days. He had had three days, in two lives. She had never given anybody anything; she had lent, and she had let, and she’d kept the receipts. And it turned out that there was this one thing she had to give, and that it was three more days, or a week, of a boy eating toast.

She reached out, and put his glasses straight.

“He’s perfectly all right,” said Lyra. “He had an egg. He told me I had some on my sleeve, the little beast. He’s started speaking to me again.”

She saw it go in. She saw him believe it. He let out his breath, and his shoulders came down. “*Has* he?” said Harry, and that terrible, happy, lopsided grin came up over his face like the sun coming up over a midden. “Lyra, that’s— That’s *brilliant*. I told you. I told you he’d come round.”

“You did.”

“You see? Say it.”

“I’ve said it. It’s in writing.”

“Say it anyway.”

“Yours,” said Lyra.

That was true. That was what she kept noticing. It was the truest thing she had. He kissed her, with his eyes shut, and she kept hers open, and looked past his ear at the small square window, where a piece of perfectly blue sky was going on and on.

*I am writing to you about the grey*, she wrote, *which has come back from Madam Malkin’s with the hem let down.*

She did that one on the Thursday night, behind her hangings. She took her time. There was a good deal about the hem. It filled a side of second-best paper, in her neatest hand, a dutiful and rather tiresome letter from a girl in the middle of her examinations, who was sorry about Sunday, and it had a gap in it, between *your* and *obedient*, of the width of two fingers.

It would be read in the south wing. She took that for granted. It would go up the back stairs on a salver and be looked at over a cup of something by somebody with very clean boots; and he would tilt it to the light, because he had had that morning out of her at Easter, and looked under it. So she had given him the second skin. There were six lines on it about what Harry Potter ate for breakfast, and he was welcome to them. But he had not grown up in that house. There was a thing that her mother had shown her when she was nine, about the garden door, and about lemon and the backs of seed catalogues. She pinned in a paper pattern for the hem, and she turned it over, and she wrote on the back of it very small.

She had thought about it for three days. She could not get her brother back; she had gone through that as she would go through an account, and there was no entry anywhere. Very well. But there was a date. She’d had it given to her on a bald rug, by a person with a handkerchief over his nose, and she had said a thing about it, with her chin up. *We shall not have that.* Seven weeks ago she had meant it as one means things, in a chair.

He had said *I’ll see to that*, and she had not asked him how, and whatever he was doing, it had a boy’s handwriting all over it: it had an atlas in it, and a great many sums. Somebody was going to be standing in that Ministry on the eighteenth of June, and she did not intend it to be Sirius Black. She could do that. That she could do. There was a big untidy laughing man with his boots on a table, who was the only father Harry had got, and she was going to reach out, in her own hand, over the top of both their heads, and move him.

It needed only somebody he would come for. He wouldn’t come for her; and he’d walk through a wall before he would come for a Malfoy. He’d spit in the owl’s eye.

*Mother. Your cousin must be a long way from London on the 18th of June. I can’t tell you why. I have never asked you for anything. I know what you have round your neck. He stood in that cave, and he has never had a line of it; Dumbledore kept it, and then it came to you. Write to him. Ask him to meet you, wherever you think best, and I think you will find that you have something to show him.*

She read it over. She added a line.

*He’d come for Regulus.*

The juice sank in, and went pale, and was gone. There was nothing on the pattern but the hem.

She didn’t tell Harry. She thought about that as well, and it took rather less time. *Nothing moves without asking*, she had said, on a hill, in the snow. It was a very good rule. He’d stopped keeping it some while ago, and so had she, and they had neither of them mentioned it; it lay about between them, in the room, like a good many other things that they had got used to stepping over.

It wanted twenty minutes to midnight. She put on her cloak, the grey one; it had a smell of marmalade that would not come out. Goyle would not carry her letters any more. She took it up herself.

She had not been up to the Owlery at this hour since she was thirteen. It was a long, cold climb for June, and the stairs were slippery with what stairs in that place were always slippery with. The moon had got in at the western windows. There was straw, and a great soft shifting on all the perches, and small round eyes that opened and shut, and she stood for a moment at the head of the stairs with her hand on the wall, and got her breath; and somebody said, “Oh.”

He was over by the sill. He had a white owl on his wrist, and he was in the act of tying something to its leg, with his tongue between his teeth, in his pyjamas, with a coat on over them.

Neither of them moved.

She saw him look at her hand. She saw him not look at it. He’d put his body, without appearing to think about it, a little way between her and the bird; and she noticed that hers had gone behind her, with the letter in it.

“Hello,” said Harry.

“You’ll catch your death.”

“Couldn’t sleep.” He grinned. It was a very nice grin. It was nearly all there. “Who’s yours to?”

“My mother. It’s about a hem.”

“Oh. Right.”

“Who’s yours?”

“Sirius,” said Harry. “Just—saying hello. You know. He likes getting post.”

The owl looked from one of them to the other, and shut one eye.

“Give him my regards,” said Lyra.

She crossed the straw, and picked out a dull-looking brown bird, a good way down from him. It took her a little while to tie the knot, because of her fingers. She carried it to the window. He had got there before her. They stood side by side at the sill, where somebody had once stood with his leg bleeding, with their shoulders just touching; and they put out their arms, and two owls fell away from the stone, and opened, and went off over the black trees, together for a little way, and then apart, without a sound.

They watched until there was nothing to see.

“All right?” said Harry.

“Perfectly,” said Lyra.

He felt about in the dark, and he found her hand, and they went down.
