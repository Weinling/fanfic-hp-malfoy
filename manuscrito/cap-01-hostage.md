# Chapter 1: Hostage

The night the Chamber of Secrets was closed, Harry Potter took Lyra Malfoy into an empty classroom, put her father’s diary on the desk with a hole punched through it, and informed her that she was now his hostage.

Until then, she had been having a perfectly good year.

Lyra Malfoy had many virtues, and she saw no reason to be modest about any of them. She was beautiful, for a start. She came from one of the oldest pure-blood families in Britain, and one of the busiest.

“Board of Governors. The hospital fund. My mum’s garden society, and she is *furious* about it. They’re like dry rot, the Malfoys. You find them in the walls.”

And she was clever and quick and very, very good on a broom, which was the part that seemed to upset people most.

Lyra took remarks like that as compliments. As far as she was concerned the school had a natural order, and she sat very comfortably near the top of it. Her real smiles she saved for people who had earned them, which at present meant her mother, her father, her little brother when he was behaving, and, on particularly good mornings, her own reflection.

There was, however, one flaw in the arrangement, and it wore glasses.

Lyra had taken against Harry Potter before she even knew who he was. He had been standing on the stool next to hers in Madam Malkin’s the summer before first year, a scrawny boy in clothes several sizes too big for him, and she had made perfectly friendly conversation about Quidditch and Houses and the sort of people one ought not to mix with, and he had answered in mumbles, edging away along his stool as though she might be catching.

Then, on the Hogwarts Express, she had found out his name, gone along to his compartment, and offered him her hand in front of the Weasley boy. He had looked at it and told her he could work out the wrong sort for himself, thanks.

Nobody had ever refused Lyra Malfoy’s hand before. She had not enjoyed the novelty.

In fairness, and Lyra was always fair when it cost her nothing, he wasn’t bad-looking. Somebody who had never heard him open his mouth might even have called him handsome, which was a purely objective assessment and said absolutely nothing about her. His hair was a lost cause. The rest of him was merely wasted.

What she couldn’t forgive was everything else. He had been raised by Muggles, and it showed: he wandered round the castle with his shirt untucked and his tie at half-mast, eating as though he expected somebody to take the plate away. Nobody called him harmless, though; Potter repaid grudges patiently and in full.

His marks were respectable. Lyra had a theory about that, and the theory was Granger, that know-it-all Mudblood; and since the theory had spent the last month lying petrified in the hospital wing, Lyra had been watching Potter’s marks with great interest.

Then there was Quidditch. This year Lyra had joined the Slytherin team as Seeker, opposite Potter, and her father had very kindly equipped the entire side with Nimbus Two Thousand and Ones, which had nothing whatsoever to do with anything. Granger had suggested otherwise, which was why Lyra had called her what she had called her, and why Weasley had spent that afternoon belching slugs.

The November match she did not discuss: not the Snitch, which had apparently hovered beside her left ear for several seconds while she was busy telling Potter exactly what she thought of him, and not the broken arm he had caught it with, which people talked about as though it were heroic rather than simply careless.

And he kept ending up at the centre of everything. Last June he had snatched the House Cup out from under Slytherin’s nose, and Lyra had chewed a thumbnail very nearly to ruin over the pudding, until Crabbe, without a word, took her by the wrist and put her hand back down on the table.

This year half the school had called him the Heir of Slytherin, until he announced over dinner that they were welcome to, provided the Slytherins started kneeling; and Lyra, who knelt to nobody, had found herself in the absurd position of defending him.

Hogwarts without him, she sometimes thought, would be very nearly perfect. Quieter. Better run. Considerably less likely to involve giant snakes.

The news reached the dungeons a little before midnight, the way news always reached the Slytherin common room: sideways, in pieces, and from somebody’s cousin.

Ginny Weasley was alive. Dumbledore was back. Something enormous had been killed underneath the school, and Potter and Weasley had been involved, because naturally they had. A fifth-year said basilisk, and everybody laughed, Panther Parkinson so hard that he fell off the arm of his chair.

Then Professor Snape appeared in the doorway with the expression of a man who had been ordered to be festive and was taking it personally, and announced a feast, now, in whatever they happened to be wearing. Nobody laughed about the basilisk after that.

Lyra refused to go up with her hair the way it was, and took ten minutes she didn’t really have in front of the mirror. When she came down, in her dark green dressing gown with the silver embroidery at the cuffs, she looked, if not her best, then at least deliberate. Her hands had not been entirely steady with the brush. The dungeons were always cold at night.

Her father had been in an excellent mood all year. At Christmas he had written, in the careful way he wrote anything that might be read by the wrong eyes, that she should keep her head down and let events take their course; she had asked what events, and he had answered with three pages about her Transfiguration marks.

Lyra was not stupid. She knew he despised Arthur Weasley and wanted Dumbledore gone. She had been standing beside him in Flourish and Blotts last August when he picked the youngest Weasley’s battered Transfiguration book out of her cauldron, sneered at it, and dropped it back in, and she had been at school a few weeks later when the first message appeared on the wall.

She had not asked herself any questions. Questions were for people whose fathers weren’t Lucius Malfoy, and her father had spent her entire life making sure that consequences happened to other people. Whatever had gone wrong tonight could be tidied up. It always was.

Crabbe and Goyle were waiting at the foot of the dormitory stairs in nightdresses that had clearly been bought with growth in mind, and they fell in behind her without being asked. The three of them had just reached the entrance hall, which was full of noise and candlelight and Hufflepuffs in nightcaps, when someone stepped out of the shadow beside the marble staircase and said, “Malfoy.”

It was Potter.

He hadn’t changed. Everyone else in the castle was in their nightclothes, and Potter was still in his school robes, and they were filthy: streaked with something grey and slimy down one side, torn at the hem, and stiff along the right sleeve with what Lyra realised, after a moment, was dried blood.

There was a fresh scratch along his jaw. His hair was worse than usual, which she would not have thought possible. He looked, in short, exactly like somebody who had spent the evening under the school killing something, and Lyra decided on the spot that she was not going to be impressed by it.

“A word,” he said.

“I’m busy.”

“It’s about the diary.”

Behind her, Goyle made a confused noise. Lyra didn’t turn round. Something inside her had gone very still.

“Go on ahead,” she told them. “Save me a seat. Somewhere away from the Gryffindors.”

Crabbe looked at Potter, then at her. “You sure?”

“Did I ask for a second opinion?”

They went.

Potter led her down a side corridor off the entrance hall and into a classroom nobody seemed to have used in years. The desks had been shoved against the walls, the blackboard still held half a sentence about the uses of moonstone, and the only light came from the window, where the moon sat fat and pale above the grounds. It smelled of chalk dust and damp. Potter shut the door.

“If you’ve dragged me out of a feast,” Lyra began, “in my dressing gown, to—”

“Malfoy. Shut up for one minute.”

She was so surprised that she did. People did not tell her to shut up. Weasley did, but Weasley didn’t count.

Potter reached into his robes and dropped something on the nearest desk. It was a small black book, or what was left of one.

A hole had been punched straight through the middle of it, ragged at the edges, as if something long and sharp had been driven in through the cover and out the back, and the pages round the wound were soaked with ink that seemed to have bled out of the paper rather than spilled onto it. It had dried in thick, glossy streaks. Lyra had the unpleasant impression that until very recently the book had been alive.

She knew it at once.

Last summer, in Borgin and Burkes, her father had laid out a row of objects on the counter for Mr Borgin to make an offer on.

Lyra had been bored, and had been told twice to stop poking at the withered hand in the glass case, and she had watched him take a small black book out of his inside pocket, turn it over once in his fingers, and put it back. *Not that one*, he had said, mostly to himself.

“That’s Father’s,” she said, before she could stop herself. “He had it at Borgin’s—”

“I know,” said Potter.

She barely heard him. She was looking at the hole.

“Your dad,” Potter said, “has done something very stupid, and you’re going to have to help him live through it.”

That brought her back. “Do you have any idea who you’re talking to?”

“Yeah. That’s why I’m talking to you and not him.”

“Where did you even get that? If you’ve been going through my father’s things—”

“He chucked it on the floor in Dumbledore’s office.” Potter’s mouth did something complicated. “He was a bit upset. I’d just freed his house-elf.”

“You did *what*?”

“Dobby. He’s free. He’s got a sock.” He said this as though it explained anything at all. “Your dad worked out what had happened and nearly cursed me, and then he stormed off. I don’t think he noticed he’d left this behind. He had a lot on his mind.”

Lyra opened her mouth, found she had about eleven things to say and no idea which mattered most, and shut it again.

Dobby was theirs. Dobby had been theirs since before she was born. You couldn’t simply go around freeing other people’s house-elves; it was like walking into someone’s house and giving away the furniture. Her father must be livid. He might even be frightened, though she had never once seen him frightened, and she found she did not want to picture it.

“Why,” she said carefully, “are you showing me a ruined book?”

Potter looked at her for a moment. Then he pulled a chair away from the wall, sat on it backwards, and folded his arms along the top.

“It’s not a book,” he said. “It’s a Horcrux.”

Lyra waited for the rest of the sentence. It didn’t come.

“I don’t know what that is,” she said at last, and resented every word.

“It’s a bit of Voldemort’s soul.”

She flinched. She couldn’t help it; she had been flinching at that name since before she could read. Potter noticed. He didn’t say anything about it, which was somehow more insulting than if he had.

“He tore his soul up and put a piece of it in there,” Potter went on, “so that if anyone killed him, he wouldn’t die properly. Your dad’s had it for years. Then he slipped it into Ginny’s things so she’d be the one opening the Chamber, and Mr Weasley would get sacked, and Dumbledore would get chucked out. And it nearly worked. Ginny nearly died.”

“No,” said Lyra.

“Yes.”

“You’re making it up. You’re making all of it up, you can’t possibly—” She stopped. “Prove it.”

Potter gave her the look you might give someone who had just tried to post a letter in a fireplace.

“Prove it to who? Fudge? I walk into the Ministry with this under my arm, somebody looks at it and says *Horcrux*, and then half the Ministry knows. Including the half that used to wear masks. Your dad’s mates, for a start—”

“My father’s friends are respectable people!”

“—and they all find out Voldemort made one, and that it’s been destroyed, and who had it last.” He flicked the diary with one finger, and it slid an inch across the desk. “You want me to do that?”

“Then hand it in and don’t tell them what it is.”

“And when someone works it out?”

“They won’t.”

“Your dad worked out what it could do,” said Potter. “It’s not that hard.”

Lyra folded her arms, mostly so he wouldn’t see what her hands were doing.

“The Ministry’s the easy part,” Potter said. “The Ministry has trials. Your dad knows everybody, so he gets a fine, or kicked off the board, or at the very worst a few months somewhere damp. That’s the good version. Honestly, if he stood up tomorrow and said he’d opened the Chamber himself, he’d probably come out of it all right.”

“He did *not* open the Chamber!”

“I know he didn’t. I’m telling you how much better that would be. Because if Voldemort comes back and finds out a bit of his soul got killed so your dad could have a go at Mr Weasley, there won’t be a trial. There won’t be anything. There’ll be your dad, and then there won’t.”

“My father,” said Lyra, and her voice climbed in a way she couldn’t stop, “knows everyone at the Ministry. He has friends on the Wizengamot. He’s on the Board of Governors—”

“And none of them,” said Potter, “are going to be in the room.”

For most of Lyra’s life, the Dark Lord had lived somewhere between history and a ghost story. Grown-ups lowered their voices when they spoke of him, if they spoke of him at all. Nobody at the Manor said out loud that her father had served him. Nobody needed to.

Lyra had grown up with the sense of an enormous locked room at the heart of the house, and she had been raised, like any sensible Slytherin, to respect whatever was kept in locked rooms. It was why she had always hated the careless way Potter said the name. He tossed it about like a Chocolate Frog card, as if it were just a word.

She was looking at the diary now, at the ink that had bled out of it, and trying to take in that a piece of the Dark Lord had been inside. Had been in her house, in her father’s pocket, on the counter at Borgin’s while she poked at a withered hand in a glass case.

And now it was dead, and the thing that had killed it was a fang, and the person who had driven the fang in was sitting backwards on a chair in front of her with blood on his sleeve, looking tired.

“Technically,” she heard herself say, “you destroyed it. Not my father.”

“I don’t think Voldemort’s going to care about technically.”

It was true, which annoyed her almost as much as everything else.

“Your dad’s just lost Voldemort a seventh of his insurance,” Potter said. “So your family wants to go very quiet. Properly quiet. No more favours for his old friends, no more—”

“Seven?”

Potter stopped.

“You said a seventh,” Lyra said. “A *seventh*. He made seven of these things?”

“That’s what he was going for.” There was a pause before he added, “Seven’s a powerful number. Everyone knows that.”

It was a short pause. Lyra had grown up at a dinner table where half of what mattered went unsaid, and she knew what a pause like that was for.

“Everyone does *not* know that he tore his soul into seven pieces. I didn’t know he’d torn it into one. You just said nobody can know about any of this, and you’re twelve.” She stared at him. “How do you know?”

“Does it matter?”

“Yes!”

“Not tonight, it doesn’t.”

“Did Dumbledore tell you?”

Potter looked at her very steadily and said neither yes nor no, and Lyra understood that he had no intention of saying anything at all.

It was the look her father wore when a dinner guest asked too directly about his investments. She had never seen it on a boy who couldn’t knot his own tie, and it sat on him oddly, like something handed down from someone much older. She would think about that later, she decided, when she was not standing in a freezing classroom in her dressing gown.

“The houses,” Potter said.

“What houses?”

“You lot have got houses all over the place. Cottages, and places on the estate, and somewhere up north. And people stay in them sometimes, don’t they? People who got let off after the war because they said they’d been Imperiused.”

Lyra’s face went hot. “That is a disgusting thing to say.”

“I didn’t say anything. I asked.”

“You didn’t ask. You never ask anything, you just *say* it, as if you—” She made herself breathe. “We do not harbour criminals.”

Potter shrugged.

It was a perfectly ordinary shrug. But there were guest cottages on the Wiltshire estate that Lyra had been told since she was small not to go near, and there had been men who came up the drive after dark and were gone by breakfast, and there had been one winter when a soft-spoken man lived in the east cottage for a month and her mother had asked her, very gently, never to mention him at school.

There were names that stopped conversations. There were friends of her father’s who had spent the entire war under the Imperius Curse, poor things, and turned out to be such excellent company afterwards.

Lyra had never needed to ask about any of it. That had always been the comfortable thing about being a Malfoy: some doors stayed shut, and behind them the grown-ups dealt with whatever needed dealing with, and you were allowed not to know.

Potter had just kicked one of those doors in, and he had done it with a shrug.

“If Voldemort comes back,” he said, “those people aren’t going to look after you. They’ll be first in the queue to prove they were loyal all along. And the easiest way to do that is to hand him whoever lost his Horcrux.”

She could see it far too clearly: the drawing room at the Manor full of her father’s old friends, every one of them smiling, every one of them so terribly sorry.

“So you lot stay out of it,” Potter said. “All of you. And if they can’t...” He hesitated, and for the first time that night something in his face looked twelve. “Then you, at least.”

Lyra stared at him.

“Me?”

“Yeah.”

“Why on earth would you care what happens to me?”

He seemed to hear the question and decide it was in a language he didn’t speak. “I’m blackmailing you, by the way,” he said instead. “In case that wasn’t clear.”

“Oh, are you?”

“If anybody ever learns what this was, and who had it, and how it died, that’s the end of the Malfoys. The Ministry’ll want to know why Lucius Malfoy was carrying round a piece of Voldemort. The Death Eaters’ll want to know why he lost it. Between the two of them, there won’t be a teaspoon left at the Manor with your name on it.” He picked the diary up again, gingerly, by one corner. “So if you don’t want that, you’re going to be our hostage.”

For a second Lyra genuinely didn’t understand the words.

“Your *what*?”

“Hostage.”

“Our,” she said. “You said *our*. Whose? Yours and Weasley’s? Is Granger in on it, is that what she’s been doing in the hospital wing, lying there plotting—”

“Not your problem.”

“It is entirely my problem, I’m the hostage!”

“Look,” said Potter, and rubbed his forehead just beneath the scar, as if it ached, “I’m not going to lock you in a cupboard or anything.”

He said it in an odd, flat voice, like a joke that wasn’t one. Lyra had no idea what to make of that, so she ignored it.

“How extremely comforting. As a rule, that is what happens to hostages.”

“It’s not like that. You’re...” He hunted for the word. “Insurance.”

It was the word he had used for the diary, and she did not care for the company.

“Against what?”

“Your dad. And anyone else who reckons the Malfoys ought to go back to Voldemort when he turns up.” He held her gaze. “I don’t need you to spy on anyone. I’m not asking you to nick letters out of his desk or listen at doors. Your dad’s not going to tell his twelve-year-old his plans.”

“Excuse me?”

“Well, he isn’t.”

“*You’re* twelve!”

“Yeah, and nobody tells me anything either,” said Potter, with feeling. “That’s not the point. The point is, you’re with us now, and your family’s going to know it. Your dad’ll think a lot harder about helping Voldemort if it means helping him against you. And you’re going to make sure he thinks about it.”

“And if I say no?”

Potter turned the diary over in his hands.

“Then I stop keeping this quiet.”

Lyra looked at him. Not five minutes ago he had spent a great deal of breath explaining why he could never tell anyone what the diary was, and now he was threatening to tell everyone. It was probably a bluff. She was fairly sure it was a bluff. She was not, however, prepared to bet her father’s life on it.

The classroom was very cold. Somewhere down the corridor the feast had started. She could hear it faintly through the walls, a roar of voices and the scrape of hundreds of benches, and somebody cheering. Gryffindors, probably. Probably for him.

That morning the world had been simple: good families and embarrassing ones, Slytherins and everybody else, and a father who could fix anything. She had never given much thought to the war, if there was ever to be another one. Wars, as far as she understood them, were things that happened to other families, and the Malfoys sent flowers afterwards.

It had not occurred to her until tonight that a world might arrive in which the Malfoys were simply in the way.

“Fine,” she said.

It came out very small. Potter raised his eyebrows, and she could have hit him.

“Fine,” she said again, louder. “I’ll do it. Whatever *it* is. Are you happy now?”

“Not really,” said Potter. “But thanks.”

She had not been expecting that, and it wrong-footed her.

“I thought there’d be hexes,” he added.

“The night,” said Lyra, “is young.”

“You’re cleverer than I thought, though.”

“Than you *thought*?”

“Don’t push it. That’s the nicest thing I’ve ever said to you.”

He got up off the chair and held out his hand.

Lyra looked at it. It was a thin, grubby hand with dried blood in the creases of the knuckles and a raw scrape across the palm, and she knew the shape of the moment, because she had been on the other side of it.

Two years ago on the Hogwarts Express she had stood in a compartment doorway and held her own hand out to this exact boy, and he had looked at it and turned it down, and she had carried that around ever since like a stone in her shoe.

Now the hand was his, and she was the one expected to take it. In the moonlight the blood in the creases of his knuckles had gone black, like ink, and Lyra, who had watched her father sign a great many things, thought it looked uncommonly like a contract: one that somebody had already signed, and that was waiting for her name underneath.

Everything she had been brought up to believe told her to turn round and walk out. Potter was a Gryffindor. Potter was a jumped-up, Muggle-raised nobody with a scar and a legend and no manners, who had never in two years done anything but get in her way.

But she was a Malfoy, and Malfoys survived. That was rather the point of them.

She took his hand.

His grip was warm and dry and a good deal firmer than she had expected. He shook once, as though sealing something, and let go.

“Go and have your feast,” he said. “Act normal.”

“I always act normal.”

“Act like you, then. Same thing.” He tucked the diary back inside his robes and went to the door, and stopped there with his hand on it. “And Malfoy? Don’t write to your dad about any of this. Not by owl. Tell him when you get home.”

Then he was gone, and Lyra was alone with the moonstone sentence and the smell of chalk and the distant roar of the Great Hall.

She stayed there longer than she meant to, going back over all of it: the Horcrux and the number seven and the cupboard, and the way he had said *then you, at least*, as if it had cost him something to say it. She went over it line by line, looking for the mistake. It was only when she reached the door herself that she found it.

*That’s Father’s*, she had said. *He had it at Borgin’s.*

And Potter had said, *I know.*

Lyra stood with her hand on the cold brass handle and thought back to Borgin and Burkes last summer: the dusty counter, the withered hand in its case, Mr Borgin bowing and scraping, her father taking the little black book out of his pocket and putting it away again.

There had been nobody else in the shop, nothing but junk and that hideous black cabinet by the door. Potter could have heard about it somehow, she supposed. Knockturn Alley was full of people who would sell anything, gossip included.

But he had also said *somewhere up north*, as carelessly as if he were remarking on the weather.

The house in Northumberland was not in any register. Her father had made very sure of that. Lyra had been there exactly once, when she was seven, and in the five years since she had not been allowed to mention it even to Crabbe and Goyle.

Potter knew it existed.
