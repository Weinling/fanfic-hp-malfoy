# Chapter 79: Who Sent for Him

She told it with dates.

She told it standing up, with her back to the window and the wand in her hand, and she didn’t raise her voice. On the sixth of June she had written to her mother in lemon juice. Her mother had written to Sirius Black, without one word of affection, about a piece of paper in Regulus’s hand, and the owl had been three days finding him. He had told her to go to hell. He had said he’d be at the end of the Cobb at Lyme on the eighteenth, at noon, and that she had better bring it. Lyra had read that on the eleventh, in bed, off the back of a seed catalogue.

“Noon,” she said. “A hundred and forty miles from that room. Nobody could have used him for anything. That was all it was for. You could have gone or not gone, it couldn’t have mattered, there’d have been nothing there but a—”

It stopped. He saw her throat move. She looked at the wall, where a kitten in a bonnet had gone to sleep with its mouth open, and she got her breath in, and when her voice came back it had a crack in it from one end to the other.

“He said he’d feed her to the crabs,” said Lyra. “And he *went*. We told him it was about his brother, and he went. I did that. I got it right, Potter, I had every single line of it *right*—”

Then she went. She bent in the middle, and a noise came out of her that he had never heard, high and ugly, like a child who has run too far. His hand went out. He hadn’t sent it. She saw it come, and her whole body went back against the window-frame, and her wand came up; and they both looked at it. He put the hand down.

“Go on,” said Lyra, when she could.

Harry hadn’t sat down. Nobody had asked him to. He told her the moor, and it took less time than he’d have thought: page 71, and the letter about the seven, which had been rubbish, and forty-one sheep. He told her what he had put into Hermione’s hands. He told her about the owl every morning, to the bothy and nowhere else. Then he felt in his robes, and it was still there, gone soft along the folds. He held it out by one corner, and she took it by another.

*Still bored? H.*

He watched her read it. He watched her go down it, as he had gone down it on a flight of steps in the sun. A bird had flown to a hillside and found nobody. There had been nobody because a man had got up that morning and gone to be rude to her mother.

“He left at dawn,” she said. “He’d have had to.”

“Yeah.”

“And she came back with it.”

“Yeah.”

“And that was when you—”

She didn’t finish it. She stood holding the bit of parchment, and they had it all out on the carpet between them now, the two halves of it. They had each looked at the only other person in the world who could have helped, and thought of something very kind. On the last night they had sat in that chair with her hand in his and their mouths shut, and the two things had fitted together so exactly that you’d have thought it had taken years of planning.

“You could have told me,” said Harry.

“Yes,” said Lyra. “Do go on.”

He found that that was the end of that.

“Is that all of it?” he said. “Lyme. Is that everything?”

She looked at him. It was only for an instant. He had made a study of that face, and he saw her eyes go down the column and stop.

“Yes,” said Lyra.

*By four o’clock*, somebody had said, by a fountain, with blood on his collar. Harry had that in his mouth. He held it there for a moment. It tasted of nothing yet. He let it lie.

“I put you down,” he said instead.

His voice had gone again, and it came out scraped. He looked at the pale square of the carpet, because he couldn’t do this at her. “That’s what happened. Nobody had me by the arm. I want— I had loads of time. I took your wand off you and I looked at it. I put your hands straight. I told Hermione it wears off.” He had to stop, and start again. “It doesn’t.”

“I know,” said Lyra. “I was there.”

“You were trying to say—”

“I didn’t say anything. That was rather the point.”

He took that. It went in and it stayed where it was.

She looked at him for a while. Her eyes were red round the rims, and she was holding them not quite open, as if the light had edges.

“You smiled at me,” she said.

“I know.”

“No,” said Lyra. “You don’t.”

She did not go on. She turned her wand over in her fingers and looked along it, at the place where his hand had been.

“Will you do that again?”

He would have given a good deal to say no. It was the obvious answer; she had left room for it. He thought of his arm coming up on a landing, and in this room. He thought of a line opening on a woman’s face, as neat as the crease in a letter, and something very pleased behind his right ear. He looked at it, and he put it where he kept things. It wasn’t her business. It wasn’t anybody’s. He had lied to this person about nearly everything for five years, and he noticed he was going to go on, and that he couldn’t, all the same, get the one small word out past his teeth.

“I don’t know,” said Harry.

She nodded. It was about the figure she had expected.

“Pockets,” said Lyra.

He took his hands out and showed them to her, both sides. It was what you did. He was halfway through it before he saw her face.

“No,” she said. “In.”

He put them in.

“That’s where they stay. You don’t take hold of me. Not my arm, not my wrist, not my hand, not to move me out of the way of anything, until I tell you. I shall tell you. I don’t know when. You don’t touch my wand.” It had gone very quiet and very exact, and she was going through it quickly, like someone crossing ice. “I was going over on to that fender, and you caught me. I’ve had some time to think about that. Next time I’ll take the iron.”

That was all of it. She stood with the morning behind her, and he could see the pulse going in the side of her neck. She looked at the door.

He had never been so aware of his hands in his life.

“Don’t go,” said Harry.

He heard it as it came out. He had said that once, with his hand round her arm. He had heard her say it yesterday, in this room, with her back to a fire. So had she. The corner of her mouth moved, and it was terrible to see.

“*Go?*” said Lyra. “Go where? I signed it, you stupid boy. I wrote it out and dried it and put the date.” Her voice went. She stood and waited for it, and got it back, and it was very nearly level. “It says *not repayable*. It doesn’t say I have to like it.”

She picked up the long pin from the fender, and she walked past him, with a good yard of carpet to spare. At the door she stopped. She did not turn round.

“You’ve got one sock,” she said. “See to it.”

“You will want to know what it said,” said Dumbledore.

“I know what it said.”

“Yes.” He had his long hands flat on the desk, and he looked at them. “I do beg your pardon. I keep forgetting.”

It was Wednesday afternoon, or it might have been Thursday. All the silver things were going round and puffing on their spindly tables. Harry sat in the chair that he’d been put in. The bowl of lemon drops was where it always was. He had thought he might want to break something in this room, and he looked about him for that, in an interested way, and it didn’t seem to be there.

The prophecy was gone, Dumbledore said, and nobody had ever heard the whole of it but the two of them; Tom had the first line and a quantity of abuse. Cornelius Fudge had stood in his own Atrium and seen what there was to see, in front of six of his Aurors and a lift attendant. It would be in the *Prophet* tomorrow. It would be a great deal of use, for about a fortnight, to a number of people who were no longer there. The Headmaster had gone to London at noon on the eighteenth because the Minister had sent him a note. He said that. He didn’t add anything to it. He looked a hundred and fifty.

“You will go back to Privet Drive at the end of term, I’m afraid, until your birthday.”

“Right.”

“And there is this.”

It was a single sheet with a goblin’s seal on it, and it came across the desk on two of his fingers. The hand went all over the place. It had been done in purple ink, on the second of January, by somebody who was laughing. *Being of sound mind, whatever Moony says.* There was a house in it, at number twelve, Grimmauld Place, with everything in it, and there was a line about the elf that had been crossed out, and written in again, and crossed out. Harry didn’t read that. He went on down until he found what he was looking for, to see what it called him.

*My godson, Harry.*

He read that three times. It didn’t seem to settle anything.

“One thing more,” said Dumbledore gently. “Mrs Lestrange was seen this morning, in Knockturn Alley, by a person who is sometimes useful to Alastor. She has been to two Healers. She has a cut from here to here that neither of them can close.” He drew one long finger down his own cheek, through the beard. “I don’t know of a curse that does that. What did you say to her, Harry?”

Harry looked up. He looked into the very blue eyes, with the half-moons in front of them. He knew how it was done. You didn’t look away; you let it come out tired, and a bit ashamed, and you let them see you were trying to remember. He was tired. He was ashamed. It was one of the best he had ever told.

“I don’t know, sir,” he said. “I was throwing everything I’d got. *Diffindo*, I think. I don’t really remember. It all went a bit—”

He moved his hand.

Dumbledore looked at him for some time. Then he reached out and took a lemon drop from the bowl, and he held it, and he didn’t eat it.

“I see,” said Dumbledore.

The Hospital Wing smelt of burnt things and fish pie.

Madam Pomfrey had taken one look at what had been done to Neville’s jaw on a bench and done it again from the beginning. He was sitting up in the end bed in striped pyjamas, strapped from the crown of his head to the point of his chin, so that he looked like somebody who has died in a Victorian novel, and on no account was he to open his mouth before Saturday. The pot was on his locker. It had dropped its flower. He had a small slate, of the kind they kept for people with no jaws, and when he saw Harry in the doorway he took up the chalk and wrote on it for some time, with his tongue moving behind the bandage, and held it out.

*ALL RIGHT?*

“Yeah,” said Harry.

Neville looked at him, with his round mild eyes. He rubbed the slate on his sleeve, and considered, and wrote again. It squeaked.

*ALL RIGHT*

He held it up and went on holding it. Harry found he needed to look at the window.

“Don’t encourage him,” said a voice from the next bed. “I’ve had that for two days.”

Cedric Diggory was lying flat with his hands outside the blanket. They had taken him off the potion that morning, and he had a grey look and a bandage under his pyjama jacket, as broad as a saddle-girth. He turned his head on the pillow an inch at a time. His trunk was at the foot of the bed. He had nine days left at Hogwarts, and he was going to spend them looking at that ceiling, he said, and then he was going to be an Auror, if they’d have anyone who couldn’t lift his arms.

“Good,” said Harry. “That’s— Good.”

“The key’s on the hook behind the flour bins,” said Diggory. He wasn’t talking to Harry. “There’s a list in the drawer. They’ll let you have the room on the same nights; I’ve spoken to McGonagall. Don’t let Finnigan near the first-years.”

Neville’s eyes had got very large. He looked behind him. There was a wall there. He seized the chalk, and it broke, and he wrote with the longer piece, and he thrust the slate across the gap between the beds at the full length of his arm.

*I CANT*

Diggory took it. It took him a while to get his hand up. He read it, and he licked his thumb, and he rubbed it out.

“There are thirty people in that room,” he said, “and I’ve been watching since February, and nobody in the corner you run has ever been frightened of you. You’ve no idea how few people I can say that about.” His eyes moved. They came to rest on Harry, and they were still very pleasant. “Yes, Potter?”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were going to ask what happens to you.”

He had been, as a matter of fact. It was absurd. He had stood at the front of that room for seven months; he had done this before, in another life, with a lot of mirrors. His name had been on the wall.

“Not you,” said Diggory. “You’ll teach the Patronus. You’ll stand at the front, where he can see you.” He shut his eyes. It seemed to have cost him something. “Nine seconds,” he said. “I told you to stay behind it.”

Somebody went by the end of the bed. It was Hopkins, long and mild and spotty, carrying a tray with his left hand and the edge of his right forearm, as he’d worked out how to; and on the tray was a dish with a crust on it, which he put down on Diggory’s locker with a good deal of care. It had come up from the kitchens. Goyle had sent it, he said; it was soft. She’d said to say there was a nutmeg in it, and to tell her if that was wrong.

“Sorry about your godfather,” said Hopkins, to Harry. He looked at him with his light tired eyes. “I don’t know what you’re meant to say.”

“No,” said Harry. “Nor do I.”

Something touched his ankle. He looked down. Under the empty bed by the wall lay a big black shape with a splint on its leg and its chin on Madam Pomfrey’s second-best slippers. It had brown eyes and a torn ear. It thumped its tail on the boards, once, in case.

He went over. He didn’t know why. There was somebody sitting on the bed. He hadn’t noticed that; you wouldn’t have. Lupin had his patched elbows on his knees and his hands hanging down, and he was looking at the floor between his shoes, and he had got very small. Nobody had asked him to sit there. He looked up, after a time, and took a moment to work out who it was.

Harry had come to say something. He had thought it would be something else.

“Who sent for him?” he said.

Lupin’s face did not change. He had been waiting for that, Harry saw. He had been sitting on that bed since Tuesday night, waiting for somebody to ask.

“I did,” said Lupin.

The dog sighed, and put its chin down.

“Ron got to McGonagall at ten past four. She got to Moody. We were at Grimmauld Place inside ten minutes, those of us who could be. We’d been looking for Sirius for twelve days, you know; he left a note on the kitchen table saying that he’d gone to see a man about a sheep.” It was a quiet, rusty voice, and it went on without any particular expression. “Moody said, get Black. He said he’d take every wand he could lay hands on, and he didn’t care where it had been. I’d sent four owls. He sent them back with drawings of a sheep. There’s an old thing we used, in the last war. It isn’t in the books. You can give a Patronus something to say, and think of a person, and it goes. It doesn’t need an address. It also shows for ten miles and tells anybody watching just where he is, so we’d kept it back. It’s the fastest thing I’ve ever owned.” He looked at his hands. “It found him on a wall in Dorset. He told me that himself, going down the stairs at the Ministry, at a run. He seemed to think it was funny.” He stopped for a little. “*They’ve got Harry. The Ministry. Come now.* That was all it said. It says it in your own voice. That’s how they know it’s you.”

He had seen that done, at a wedding: a lynx, landing among the chairs. It hadn’t once crossed his mind.

Neither of them said anything. Down the ward, a spoon rang on a plate.

*Sit down, Harry*, she had said. She’d held it up in front of his face, in his own writing, with a reference.

So that was the sum. He stood by an empty bed in the smell of fish pie, and he did it, and it was very short. If he had sat down on that step, Ron wouldn’t have run. If Ron hadn’t run, nobody would have gone to Moody. Nobody would have needed a wand. Nothing silver would have gone over Salisbury Plain looking for somebody, and at about the time that Harry Potter was handing in a History paper with one sentence not quite finished, a man in a plum-coloured waistcoat would have been sitting on a wall by the sea with his boots hanging over, swearing at his cousin, and eating an ice.

He had been safe. He’d been safe twice. Harry had seen to it, and she had seen to it, and it had worked. It had all worked. And then the one thing in the whole of England that could reach him had got up off a step and run.

He did it again, to see. It came out the same.

“It isn’t—” said Lupin.

“Don’t,” said Harry.

His hand had found its way down into rough warm fur. It wasn’t anything to do with him. The dog pushed its head up under his fingers, and looked at him, hopefully, with its stupid brown eyes.

He met Ron on the stairs.

It was the Friday, on the flight below the Fat Lady. Ron was coming down, with a broom over his shoulder. He saw who it was. He slowed up, and he came on, because there was only the one staircase, and he’d got taller since May, or Harry had stopped looking. There was a graze along his jaw, going yellow. Ron stopped one step above him. He looked at the wall. His ears had gone red, and he stood breathing through his nose, like someone at the top of a high board.

“Sorry about Sirius,” said Ron.

It was the first thing he had said to Harry for twenty-seven days.

“Thanks,” said Harry.

Ron nodded. He hitched up the broom and went on down. His big feet went away round the turn of the stair, and on, and across the landing. He didn’t look back.

Harry stayed where he was. A boy who had once sat on a roof on Rannoch Moor with his tongue between his teeth and a mouthful of nails had said three words to him, and he thought he might have those. Nobody had said he couldn’t. He stood on the step and held on to them for a bit, in the pocket with his hand.

She still came.

He hadn’t been sure of that. He went up on the Thursday night, because there wasn’t anywhere else, and walked past the bare wall, and the door was there. There were the bald rug and the fender and the chair. The next evening, at about ten, she came in with her book of rounds and sat down in it with her feet under her, and she opened the book. She didn’t say anything. He stayed on the rug, on the far side, with his hands where he’d been told. After a while she turned over a page.

It was what they did. It went on for nine nights. It wasn’t bearable, and he couldn’t have done without it.

On the third night he brought a cauldron.

He had got the recipe by heart, in another life, out of somebody else’s handwriting, and he had got the first of what he needed from the third shelf on the left, at night, under the Cloak, and not written it down in any book. It wanted a low fire and a great deal of patience, and it would be ready, if he didn’t kill it, some time before Christmas. He had stopped having plans. He had lain awake and looked at a staircase, and a boy on it with a square of parchment in his hand, and he had thought: *It wasn’t that I didn’t know. I knew everything. I just didn’t know which foot.* There was a thing that did that for you. For about twelve hours, there was a thing that told you where to put your foot.

He lit it. He knelt there in the thin bright smell, stirring it the wrong way round, nine times, with his sleeves rolled up.

He looked up after a while, and she had put down her book. She had come as far as the fender. She stood over the cauldron with her arms folded, and she looked into it, and he saw her nostrils move; she had been top in Potions since she was eleven. She looked at the colour. She looked at him. He waited. She would know what it was, and how long it took, and what it was for.

She didn’t ask. She bent down, with her skirts held away from the fire, and moved the damper by half an inch with the end of the poker, and she went back to the chair.

King’s Cross was hot and smelt of soot and the insides of pasties.

He got down from the train at the back of the crowd, with his trunk and an empty cage. Hedwig had gone on ahead. Fred and George had a stack of something by the barrier, and Mrs Weasley had got hold of Ron and Ginny, one in each arm. She looked over Ron’s shoulder and saw Harry, and her face began to work. Hermione was walking towards a man and a woman in sensible raincoats, with one sleeve pinned over a dressing, and she didn’t turn her head.

The Slytherins had got out at the front.

He saw it from thirty yards away. It was like watching iron filings on a sheet of paper. There must have been forty of them on that stretch of platform, with their parents, and there was a space in the middle, about the size of a good carpet, and everybody on the edge of it was standing with their feet turned slightly inwards. Montague had taken his hat off. Parkinson had got hold of a trunk that wasn’t his. Harry saw old Nott bend from the waist, by about an inch, over a very thin white hand; and he saw a thickset woman in black, who had had a husband in the Ministry cells since a week last Tuesday, take a step forward and say something, low and warm, and press the thin hand between both of her own.

It had gone round. Of course it had. Any man in that line could talk. *You may tell my daughter it was very well done.*

She stood in the middle of it, in a long pale travelling cloak, with her hair up, and she inclined her head to Mrs Crabbe exactly as far as was correct. Her mother was behind her with one hand flat on the top of Draco’s head. There was a lot of green and silver, and it looked at her as if she had a crown on.

A little way along, by a pillar, someone was reading the *Prophet* with both eyes. Moody had a mug in his hand and his bad leg out in front of him. He wasn’t reading the paper. The blue one had come round in its socket, slow and steady, and stopped.

Harry put down his trunk.

He didn’t decide to go over. He’d have said he stood there. The crowd moved, as they do; the families began to break up and drift towards the barrier, in order of importance; and there was a moment when the space around her had thinned, and she had turned aside to say something about a hatbox, and she was three feet away. He had his hands in his pockets. He’d got rather good at it. Up close she was the colour of candle wax.

“Potter,” said Lyra.

“Malfoy.”

It was for anybody to hear. She looked him up and down, as you’d look at something that had been left on a platform. Her eyes came up, and they were dry and quite steady and they stayed on his.

“Have you anything you want to ask me?” she said.

It was very quiet. Her lips hardly moved. He thought he had never heard her say anything with so little in it. A long way down the platform a guard was shouting about a door, and Mrs Crabbe was still looking at her, with that warm grateful stupid face.

He could have done it. He had it ready. It was only three words long. He’d had it since the fountain, and he had got up with it and cleaned his teeth with it, and he looked at her, and he thought of a green fire, and a breath going in. Of how she had stopped, in that room, and looked at Hermione. He’d seen that. He knew what he’d seen. He had not looked at it since, and he found he’d walked all round it, all week, as you walk round something in the road.

She had moved her hand. He looked down. She had taken a fold of his sleeve, just above the cuff, between her finger and thumb. No more than that. You couldn’t have seen it from a yard away. He could feel it all the way up his arm.

“No,” said Harry.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she let go, and smoothed the place, once, and turned to see about the hatbox.
