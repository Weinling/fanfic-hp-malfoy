# Chapter 59: In Public

“Malfoy,” said Harry Potter, stopping behind her at breakfast. “Did your father buy you that face, or was it left to you?”

He said it clearly, and he said it to the room. Forty Slytherins stopped chewing. It was the last Monday of January, and the Great Hall was full of grey light and the smell of kippers. Lyra laid down her knife. She turned on the bench, without haste, and looked him over from the boots up, as one looks at a stain on a good carpet and wonders whose fault it is.

“It’s an heirloom, Potter,” she said. “You wouldn’t know. Yours were all repossessed.”

Somebody at the end of the table made a noise like a kettle. Potter went white about the nostrils. He looked at her for a long, hating moment, breathing through his nose, and then he said “You’ll be sorry for that,” and walked away, with his shoulders very stiff; and Parkinson thumped the table with both hands.

“Oh, *good* one,” said Parkinson. “*Repossessed.*”

“Thank you, Panther.”

She picked up her knife, and went on with her toast. Zabini, across from her, was shuffling a pack of cards without looking at them.

“Six,” he said.

“I beg your pardon?”

“Out of ten. It’s a nice line. He let you down on the exit. I don’t believe in *You’ll be sorry*. A person who has just been told that would say something else.” He dealt himself a card and considered it. “He was better on Thursday. There’s something rather fresh about the way he hates you this term.”

“You came in early,” said Lyra.

“I didn’t.”

“You came in on *left to you*, before I’d finished turning round. I told you. I need the turn. It’s the whole of the effect. And what in the name of Merlin was *You’ll be sorry for that?* We agreed on the thing about the peacock.”

“I forgot the thing about the peacock.”

“It was four words long!”

“I’d just seen your face,” said Potter, from the rug. “You were doing the nostrils. I nearly lost it.”

It was half past nine, and the snow was going by in the little window. They had the script on the floor between them, a sheet of the second-best paper with two columns on it and a number of crossings-out. It had begun as a precaution. Nobody could be seen to stop hating Harry Potter; not Lucius Malfoy’s daughter, not this year, with her father’s owl on the table every Wednesday; and since it had to be done in any case, it had seemed to her only sensible that it should be done properly. That had been three weeks ago. She gave him a mark every evening, out of ten, in red. He was currently averaging five and a half. She had found herself lying awake on the Sunday night, with her heart going, because she had thought of a thing to say about his glasses.

“Zabini gave you a six,” said Potter.

“Zabini gave *us* a six, and he’s a gambler with no ear. He says there’s something fresh about the way you hate me.”

“That’s nice.”

“It is not nice. I don’t want it fresh. I want it unanswerable.” She crossed something out. “Weasley has started a new graph.”

He put his arm over his eyes. His shoulders had begun to go.

It had been the great consolation of Ronald Weasley’s winter that the points had stopped. He had stood up in the common room in November, it was said, and made a short speech. Then, in the third week of January, on a morning when nobody had done anything, there had been a hole in the rubies. It was a larger hole than the old ones had been. Weasley had gone down to look at it in his pyjamas. He had been seen on the stairs with a ruler. She had passed him that very afternoon, on her way to Charms, standing in front of the hourglass with Granger at his elbow.

“*Tens*,” he had been saying, in a dreadful hollow voice. “She’s gone up to *tens*.”

Granger had not said anything. She had made a small neat tick, on a piece of parchment, and on the way past she had looked at Lyra for about a second, with no expression at all.

Then there was the timetable.

It had been in Lyra’s pigeonhole on the Friday, folded into a square. It was ruled in three colours. *Mon. and Fri., 8.30 to 10.30. Sun., 2 to 5, reports. NOT Weds. (Harry: Potions essays.) NOT Thurs. (You know why.) Both of you will go to dinner. I have spoken to Goyle.* There was a note at the bottom, in the same small upright hand. *He has O.W.L.s in June as well, whatever he may think. This is not a kindness. I want that written down.*

She had read it three times. She had never in her life been told what to do by anybody so badly dressed. She had pinned it up over the fireplace, because it was, when one looked at it, an extremely good timetable.

“‘Describe the causes of the goblin rebellion of 1612,’” Lyra read. “You have twenty minutes.”

It was the Friday. They were doing History of Magic. It was, she felt, an odd thing to be doing in that room; but it was on the timetable, and she had got into the habit of finishing things, and he had been so plainly astonished at being made to sit up and take a quill that she had decided to go on with it for the look on his face. She had been at it for an hour. They had not touched one another, except by accident, and once with a ruler.

He had his chin on his fist, and he was staring at the fire. He looked tired and thoughtful, and he had ink on his ear. She was sitting on the rug with her back against the chair and his knee beside her head. Nothing had been settled. He had not said anything clever. She was still angry with him about a crown, in a small way, when she remembered; he was, she knew, still looking at her hands; and it seemed that one could go on being angry and copy out the dates of the Goblin Wars, and that it was rather peaceful.

“It won’t be that one,” said Potter.

“I beg your pardon?”

“1612. It won’t come up. There’s one on wand legislation, and something on the giants. And the International Confederation.” He yawned. “I shouldn’t bother with the goblins.”

Lyra lowered the parchment.

It took her about as long as it takes to turn a page. She sat quite still. Then she got up on her knees, and turned round, and laid both her hands on the arm of the chair.

“You’ve sat these,” she said.

He stopped in the middle of the yawn.

“You have *sat* these. You’ve *taken* your O.W.L.s, you unspeakable fraud. You’ve been in that Hall. You have seen the paper!”

“It’s—that’s not— It won’t be the same! Everything’s different. Moody’s not— It might be different.”

“What did you get?”

He looked at the ceiling. His ears had started.

“Potter. What did you get in History of Magic?”

“D,” said Potter.

There was a silence.

“I fell asleep,” he said. “I had a dream. Quite a bad one. I’d only got half of it done, there was this whole thing with the—”

She had got hold of the front of his jumper. She found she was making a sound. It seemed to be coming from somewhere near her stomach, and she could not stop it, and she put her forehead down on the arm of the chair. She had known for seven months that this boy had lived his life before. He had stood in the straw and told her about it with the owls looking on, and she had lain awake over it till she was ill. And he had sat his History of Magic with all that behind his eyes, and he had got a D.

“It’s not funny,” said Potter.

“And *Divination?*”

“I’d rather not—”

“Twice! You’ve *failed* it, haven’t you? You, of all the people in the entire— You know the *future!*” She could not breathe. There were tears going down beside her nose. It was some time since that had happened to her, and it hurt a good deal, under the ribs, and she never wanted it to stop. “Oh, you poor *idiot*. Oh, I shall tell Granger. What was on the Charms paper? No. Don’t tell me. *Tell* me. I shan’t use it.”

“That’s cheating.”

“I know it’s cheating! What was question six?”

He looked down at her. He was scarlet, and furious, and it was starting to get away from him at one corner of his mouth. Nobody had laughed like that at him, she thought, in either of his lives. He seemed to find that he did not mind.

“Substantive charms,” said Potter. “Something about a goblet. I got it wrong.”

She kissed him. It seemed to be the only way of stopping. It did not work; she got the giggles halfway through, and bit her own lip, and he said “Ow” on her behalf.

There were, that term, a certain number of matters to be cleared up.

There was Theodore Nott, for one. He had given her a book. It was only *The Great Houses of Wessex*, in two volumes, with plates, and he had lent it to her in the most gentlemanly manner, at the end of Ancient Runes. She took it up to the seventh floor and left it on the shelf. On the following Monday she took down the first volume, to check something about a Selwyn, and she found that somebody had been writing in it. It was in pencil, in a small untidy hand. *No it isn’t*, it said, on page 40. *Who says?* And further on: *He’s just copied this out of Bagshot.* And on page 212, with a line under it, pressed so hard that it had gone through to the plate: *RUBBISH.*

“You read it,” said Lyra.

Potter looked up from his Potions essay with an open and innocent face.

“There are eleven hundred pages in this. You loathe the Great Houses of Wessex. You’ve never in your life read anything with plates.”

“He underlines with a ruler,” said Potter darkly.

“He does nothing of the kind. How would you know? It’s a perfectly civil book.”

“I bet he irons his socks. Have you seen his hands? I’ve never trusted anybody who had hands like that.”

She closed the volume and put it back. It was perfectly ridiculous. There was no reason on earth why anybody should feel so extremely well about it. She made him rub out *RUBBISH*, and it took him twenty minutes, and he did it with very bad grace; and she took the opportunity to raise the matter of Cho Chang, and a certain breakfast in the autumn of their third year.

“What breakfast?”

“You know perfectly well. She dropped her spoon.”

“I don’t remember any spoon! I was *thirteen!*”

“You were thirteen, and you picked it up. I was four tables away. I’ve got the date.”

It cost him a bad quarter of an hour. She kissed him at the end of it, to show that there was no ill feeling, and then again, on reflection, because there was.

There was the question of the Bishop of Bath and Wells, which he kept trying to bring back. There was the question of who had eaten the last of Goyle’s fruit cake; and of whether a person could be said to have a good singing voice if she had been told so only by her mother; and there was a very long dispute, on the first Sunday in February, as to whether or not a fox could climb a tree.

“They can’t,” said Potter. “They’re not cats. Their feet don’t work.”

“I think I may know more about foxes than you do. We have had them on the estate since the Conquest.”

“You’ve had people to shoot them.”

“They go up the yew by the east wall. I’ve *seen* them.”

“Prove it.”

That was how it came about that she took out her wand, at four o’clock in the afternoon, with her feet on the fender.

She had not done it since before the summer. It came to her as she lifted her hand. She had not done it since she had stood in the dining room, on Isfahan carpet, and found out what she could mean; she did not suppose that it would come, for that kind of person, and she stopped with the word in her mouth, and looked at the wand. He had gone quiet. He did not say anything. He had taken his hand out of his pocket and laid it on the arm of the chair, about an inch from her elbow.

She looked at the snow going by. She looked at the timetable over the fire, and at the bald rug, and at a boy with ink on his ear who had got a D in History of Magic.

“*Expecto Patronum*,” said Lyra.

It came out of the end of her wand like a cork. It was a good deal larger than she remembered. It hit the floor all legs, and skidded, and went twice round the rug with its ears flat, shedding light. It saw the shelf. It went up the side of the bookcase in four bounds, claws scrabbling, sending *The Great Houses of Wessex* to the floor, and it sat on the top with its brush hanging down, looking extremely pleased; and then it fell off.

Potter’s fox, which had trotted out of his wand a moment before and sat down on the hearthrug with its chin up, looked at it for some time. It got up. It crossed the rug, with an air of having been sent for, and it began to wash her fox’s ear.

“That’s a bookcase,” said Potter. His voice was not altogether steady. “It’s not a tree.”

“It’s a *precedent*,” said Lyra.

She did not look round. She found she needed a moment, on account of the smoke.

The card came at breakfast on the fourteenth.

She had known it would. She had told him that if he did anything of the kind she would have him killed, and he had looked at her with great sincerity. It came down the Great Hall in the claws of a very small school owl that could hardly keep it in the air. It was the size of a tea tray. It was pink. It landed in the marmalade in front of the entire Slytherin table, and it opened of its own accord, and began to sing.

*O Malfoy, Malfoy, ship ahoy!*
*Thy hair is like a Fanged Frisbee.*
*Thine eyes are grey as old lino—*

Nobody in the history of the school had ever been so happy as Panther Parkinson was at that moment. He had to be held up by Montague. Down the Hall, every head at the Hufflepuff table had turned. The verses went on. There were five of them, in a high fluting voice with a slight lisp. *Lino*, she was given to understand, had been made to rhyme in the fourth verse with *rhino*. It was signed, in letters of gold, *An Admirer*.

Lyra sat through to the end of it. She reached out, and shut the thing, with one finger. It gave a small strangled *ahoy*. She lifted her eyes, and looked along the length of the room, past Ravenclaw, to where a dark untidy head was bent very low over its porridge. Its shoulders were perfectly still. It was the stillest head she had ever seen.

“Thomas,” said Zabini thoughtfully, turning over a card. “Or a Weasley. It’s got that sort of finish. Nine.”

She was first in the room that night. It was a Wednesday, and not on the timetable, and neither of them was going to mention it. She had got the chair. She sat in it with her arms along its arms and her book open on her knee, and she had been composing what she was going to say since a quarter to eight that morning. It had an opening and three heads. She heard the door. She did not look up. He came round the side of the chair, with his hair on end, and an expression of holy calm; and he said, “Did you get anything nice?”; and without any further remark he folded himself up and sat down on her book.

“Get off.”

“*Thine eyes are grey as—*”

“Potter, I have been sung at in front of four Houses. I have had marmalade on my prefect’s badge. It said *lino*. It is going to be on the wall of the Hufflepuff common room by Friday. I’ve never been so humiliated in my life, and you have got your elbow in my Arithmancy, and I love you, you unspeakable oaf. Get off my chair.”

The fire went on burning.

She had gone on looking at the place where her book had been. She seemed to have stopped. It was very odd. There was a sentence still going on in the room, somewhere near the ceiling. She could hear exactly how it had sounded; it had come out in the same voice as all the rest of it, a little breathless, and rather shrill, and it had been going somewhere else. Nobody had asked it. It had simply got in among the others.

He was not grinning.

He had stopped, with his mouth a little way open. He had turned his head, and he was looking at her from about a foot off, and all the calm had gone out of his face, and the holy expression, and the *lino*. He looked as if somebody had dropped something on a stone floor in a very quiet house.

“Say that again,” said Potter.

“I shall do nothing of the kind.”

“You said—”

“I said *get off my chair*. It was a figure of speech. I meant *loathe*. It was the marmalade. I have had a very trying day.” Her face was on fire, from the collar up. She could feel it in her ears. “Stop *looking* at me like that. What are you doing? What’s that? Put that down!”

He had got out a quill. He had taken a sheet of paper from the fender, and was hunting about, with a hand that did not seem to work very well, for something to lean on. He settled on her knee. He wrote for a moment, breathing through his mouth, with his tongue between his teeth, and then he sat and looked at what he had put.

“What’s the time?” said Potter. It came out cracked. “I want it for the record.”

“You are not writing that down.”

“A hundred and thirty-three days,” he said. He did not seem to be saying it to her. “From Umbridge’s office. I’ve been counting. I wasn’t going to say.” He looked up. His eyes had gone very bright and he was starting, slowly, helplessly, to smile, all over his face, and he could not do anything about that, and he did not try. “*Hold still*, you said. A hundred and thirty-three days ago. It was a Wednesday as well.”

She looked at him. She looked at him sitting on her Arithmancy, with ink on his lip, holding a sheet of the second-best paper in both hands.

“Ten points from Gryffindor,” said Lyra, with what voice she could find. She took him by the ears. “For sitting.”

The owl was on the rack by her plate the next morning.

It was her father’s, the grey one. It held out its leg as it always did. She had come down late, and rather well pleased with the world. She had caught a glimpse of a dark head at the Gryffindor table, and it had lifted, as she passed, and very slightly mouthed the word *ahoy*. She gave the owl a piece of bacon. She slit the letter with her butter knife.

It was a single side. He had not put in anything about Draco, or the Minister. The hand was his, and it had got worse.

*My darling,*

*He has had your reports read to him. He is amused. He says that you have a pretty wit.*

*Nits will not do. He asks what you have brought him, and I have nothing to tell him. He has been very patient, and your grandfather’s hands are no better.*

*There is a man at the Ministry, in a department you will not have heard of. We want his name and his hours. Your Professor Umbridge will have them. She will tell you, if you ask her nicely.*

*Do this for me.*

She read it through, and she folded it along its creases, and she looked for some time at the marmalade. Somebody had wiped the table. There was still a little pink glitter down between the boards.

Goyle pushed the toast rack an inch towards her.

Lyra took a piece, and buttered it, right out to the corners.
