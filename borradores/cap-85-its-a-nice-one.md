# Chapter 91: It’s a Nice One

“That’s where the oven goes,” said Goyle.

She pointed. There was nothing there. It was a low dim empty room on the wrong side of Hogsmeade, between a cobbler’s and a shop that sold second-hand cauldrons, and it had a stone floor, a good many cobwebs, and a smell of mice and old apples. A long card in the window said *TO LET: Apply Rosmerta*. Snow was going by outside. It was the Saturday, the last Hogsmeade weekend of term, and Lyra stood on the bare boards by the door in her grey cloak and her grey lumpy gloves, wondering how she had come to be there.

She hadn’t agreed to it. She was fairly sure about that. She had been sitting at breakfast, looking at an egg. Goyle had come and stood behind her with a scarf on. She’d said, “I want to show you something,” and she had gone on standing there, large and mild and quite unhurried, like weather, until it had seemed less trouble to get up.

“There’s a flue,” said Goyle. “Look. It goes right up. Bricked in, but it’s sound, I went and looked. You could have a proper bread oven, in that wall, and a range along there. There’s a bit of a yard at the back.” She walked across the floor, heavily, with her arms held a little out from her sides, measuring it. She had a pencil in her hair. She stopped in the middle of the room and turned round, and she pointed with one thick finger at a patch of air about four feet off the ground. “Counter. Pies there, and your buns. Cakes at the end, where they’d get the light from the door. People come in, and it’s the first thing they’d see. And that’s the stool.”

“What stool?”

“For old ladies,” said Goyle. “They want to sit down. I’ve thought about that.”

The door banged. A thickset figure had come in backwards, shaking snow from a shawl, with a long narrow book held against her chest. She stopped when she saw who it was. She looked at Lyra, once. It went to the left sleeve, and the gloves. She didn’t say anything. She had not said anything to Lyra since the third of December. She walked across and laid the book on the windowsill, and opened it at a ribbon, and took out a stub of pencil.

“Eight Galleons a quarter,” said Crabbe, to the page. “I’ve been in to see her. She wants a year in advance, and she’ll take it in two lots. It’s the chimney; there’s nobody else who wants it. Flour’s gone up. You’re not to go above twelve on the marble, Greer, I told you. There’s a man in Tutshill.”

“It’s got to be Welsh.”

“It’s got to be paid for.”

“Zabini’s put in a Galleon,” said Goyle, to Lyra.

She said it with pride. She felt in her apron, and held it out, on the flat of her big pink hand. It was quite an ordinary Galleon. It had been polished. There was a very small square of card tied to it with a bit of thread, and on the card, in a slanting elegant bored hand, Lyra read: *As an investment. I expect nothing. B.Z.*

A very small movement had occurred at the corner of Crabbe’s mouth. Lyra saw it. She had never seen that book. It had a stiff black spine. It was ruled by hand. There were dates in it that went back two years, in the small round careful writing, a whole long patient history of butter, and all the way down the margin, in a much larger, slower, rounder script, somebody had been adding remarks in pencil. *Good. More nutmeg. Ness says no.*

Two years. She stood on the boards with the cold coming up through her soles. They’d gone quiet when she came in at the door. They had always gone quiet. She had thought that it was respect. She’d sat in that dormitory for five and a half years, at the centre of everything, with those two people on either side of her like a pair of good bookends; and all that time there had been this. It had got a yard at the back. It had a stool in it, for old ladies. She looked for the place that had been kept for her. She went over the whole of that small dim smelly room. She wasn’t in it. Nobody had meant anything by that. It had simply never occurred to anyone that she would want to be.

She had a slight sensation of having looked through a window, from the dark outside, into a house with people in it.

“What will you call it?” said Lyra.

She hadn’t known that she was going to say that. It didn’t sound like anything. It wasn’t the pleasant voice. It had come out a little hoarse.

They both looked at her. It was very quiet in the room. She saw Crabbe’s pencil stop. Goyle had turned her big soft face, with the snow-light on it; she looked at Lyra, for some time, as she’d have looked at something that had been in the oven and was just starting to rise.

“We don’t know,” said Goyle. “Ness wants *Goyle and Crabbe*. She says that people like to know who they’re suing.”

It got out before she could stop it. It wasn’t very much, a sort of sharp small breath down her nose; it hurt her somewhere under the ribs. It was a laugh. Crabbe had looked down at her book again. But she’d heard that, she must have heard, because Lyra saw the pencil start up, quite slowly, and she saw the flat pale ear above it go pink.

They went to Honeydukes, for the fudge, and to the Three Broomsticks; and somebody put a plate in front of Lyra with a thing on it, and she ate some. It was a good day. She noticed that at about four o’clock, on the road back, with the lamps coming on along the wall of Hogsmeade Station, and the three of them walking in a line, in the blue dusk, with the snow creaking. Goyle was singing something, under her breath, flat. Lyra had got a crumb in the wool of her glove. Her arm lay along her side. It listened, she supposed. It had heard about the marble. She hoped very much that he was enjoying that.

She thought that it had probably been the best day of her life. It seemed an odd thing to find out, in Hogsmeade.

He was on the stairs.

It was the second flight up from the Entrance Hall, a little after five. Everyone had gone in to get warm. She had stayed to shake out her cloak, and sent the others on, and she came up the marble steps by herself, with her boots wet and her face stinging; and there was someone sitting on the turn, with a book on his knee. Weasley’s sister was sitting on the step three above him, eating an apple, and looking at the wall. She got up when she saw who it was. She gave Lyra one long direct and rather puzzled look, as she’d been giving her for a month; and she wiped her fingers on her jeans, and went away up the stairs.

He stood up.

He had got very thin. He took his hands out of the book, and she saw them go to his pockets, one and then the other, and settle there. He didn’t say anything. It was the first time that they had been within ten feet of each other, without a Hall full of people, since the fifteenth of November. There was a white line by the side of his mouth. There was nothing whatever on his face. She had seen that face do a good many things to a piece of toast.

She stopped, two steps below him. She didn’t know why. She should have gone by. She had a line about his tie.

He looked at her. It was a slow, tired, careful look, and it went from her wet hair down to her boots, and back; and it stopped. It stopped at the corner of her mouth. She saw something happen in the back of his eyes. It was very slight. She’d have said that it was a sort of disbelief. She put up one woollen finger, and touched the place, and there was a bit of pastry there.

“You’ve eaten something,” said Harry.

His voice was hoarse. It didn’t have anything in it. He said it as a person might who has been walking for a long time, in the dark, and has come round a corner, and seen a light in a window. He didn’t seem to be expecting a reply.

There was a pause, of about the width of a finger and thumb.

She looked for the pleasant voice. She knew where she’d left it. It lay along the vein. It was a Saturday evening, with the snow going by. He might have put down his cup; he might have been reading. She thought about Herodotus. She thought about a boy on a stone seat, and a wall of faces, and a thing that had gone in under her breastbone in the Great Hall, and had not come out. She’d never forgive that. She had looked at it from every side. There was a small glass phial, about the size of a tooth, buttoned against her ribs. She’d had half a mince pie.

“Bacon,” said Lyra.

It came out rather low, and it had a crack down the side; and it was the first thing that she had said to him since a chalk hill that was for him.

She saw it reach him. He didn’t move. He didn’t do anything at all. His chest had stopped. He stood two steps above her with his hands in his pockets, and his eyes behind the mended glasses went very wide; they filled up, quite slowly, from the bottom, with something that she couldn’t look at. His lips parted. *Don’t*, she thought. *Don’t speak, you idiot. Don’t say anything, not one word. Oh, don’t.*

He didn’t. He’d shut his mouth. He pushed his hands down, as far as they would go, so that she saw his shoulders move.

She went up the two steps. It didn’t take very long. She took hold of the edge of his collar, between one grey woollen finger and thumb, and she stood on her toes. His mouth was chapped and cold, and it was shaking. It was hardly anything. It was a very small one. It lasted for about as long as it takes for a snowflake to go out on a hot stove. He hadn’t breathed. She could feel him not breathing. Neither of them made any sound. There wasn’t a thing to hear.

She let go. She smoothed that down, once. She went on past him, up the stairs, with her cloak dripping, and she didn’t look back. At the turn she noticed that she’d got her left hand pressed flat against her ribs. She was smiling. She could feel it on her face, like something that had been left out in the rain and had started, very stiffly, to dry.

There was a thin hard sun on the Sunday.

She got the box out at about three. She did that on Sundays. She’d have told you that it was only sensible. She drew the hangings on the door side, for the look of the thing. The dormitory was empty, with everyone up at the lake, trying to skate. She knelt down by her trunk, and moved the winter things, and felt in the left boot, and she lifted it out and set it on her bed. It was very clean and pale. It might have been the inside of an almond. She took off the lid.

There were twenty-four.

They lay in their two layers, in their pleated paper, each one with its small dark curl. She looked at them for a while. She didn’t touch anything. She only counted. There were five days. Nothing had changed. *To the Headmaster*, said the small card in its slot, *with the compliments of the Sixth Year*. She had written that herself, in the end. It had a nice plain sound to it.

She thought that she might write it out fair. She’d got a blot on the *c*. She looked in her bag, and found that she had run out of the good black. There was a bottle in the Prefects’ cupboard, at the end of the passage. It would take two minutes.

She left the lid on the blanket.

She wasn’t a careless person. She would like that to be understood. She would go through this, afterwards, a very great number of times; she’d stand at the door of it and look in. She had kept that box for four months and a half, in a boot, under a mouth full of lies. She’d never left it for so long as it took to clean her teeth. She went along the passage with her book of rounds under her arm. She found that she was humming. It was something flat. It wasn’t even a tune.

It was more like five minutes. There had been some question about the key.

She came back along the stone passage with the bottle in her right hand. The door was ajar. She pushed that with her foot. She had started to say something to no one in particular. She stopped.

Goyle was sitting on her bed.

She’d come in from the lake. She’d got her scarf on still, and her boots had made two wet marks on the floor, and her big face was bright pink with the cold. She had sat down on the edge of the mattress, which had sunk a good deal. She had a greaseproof packet beside her, that she must have brought up to put on the bedside table. She had the pale wooden box on her knees, in both her hands. She was bending over it. She had got her nose about two inches from the top layer, and her small eyes were shining. Lyra had seen that look. She’d seen it on the Thursday before last. She had seen it on people in cathedrals.

“Oh, look at *that*,” said Goyle.

It was a soft reverent thick sort of voice, and it wasn’t addressed to anybody. She hadn’t looked up.

“Look at that shine. That’s been done on marble, that has. By hand. You can’t get that with a wand. Look at the *curl*. I’ve been trying to do that for a year, it goes to bits on me. Who did these? They’ve got ever such a thin shell, you can see where he’s—” She had turned her head. Her whole great plain face lit up, slowly, from the chin. “There you are! Where’ve you been hiding these? You never said. I’ve been up and down them stairs. There’s been nothing you’d look at. I’ve tried everything, I’ve tried you with suet. I’ve been going mad. And you had these all along! I knew there’d be something. I said to Ness. I said, there’s got to be *something* that she fancies.”

The card had fallen out of the lid. It lay on the floor, by the wet mark of a boot. Goyle looked at it, and she stooped, with a grunt; she picked it up between finger and thumb, and read it, with her lips moving. She laughed. She had a very nice laugh. It was slow and rather breathy, and you could feel it in the floorboards.

“He’s got his lemon drops,” said Goyle. She let it fall. “He won’t miss one.”

There was a bar of sun across the room. It lay along the floor between them, and up over the end of the bed. It had a great deal of dust in it. It was going round very slowly. Lyra could see each of those. There was a bottle of ink in her hand.

She had not moved. Her heart didn’t seem to be doing anything. She heard someone speak. It was a very good voice. She’d had it since she was eleven; it had got her a bench, and a boy to carry her bag, and it had never failed her but the once. It wasn’t raised. It was cool and clear and bored, and it came out as it always came.

“Put that down,” said Lyra.

Goyle looked at her.

She looked at her across the sun, from under her pale eyebrows. She looked at the bottle of ink. Something had started to happen to her. It began at the corners of her eyes; it spread. She was sixteen years old. She had been told what to do by that voice since the first of September, 1991, and she’d got a ten, and a Galleon in her pocket with a bit of thread on it. It had been a very good weekend. She had shown someone an oven. She’d heard her laugh. Her shoulders had begun to shake. She sat on the bed with a box of chocolates on her lap, and she looked up at Lyra Malfoy, with her small eyes gone to slits, and her big soft chin trembling with delight.

“No,” said Goyle.

She was laughing. She could hardly get it out. It was the funniest thing that had ever happened. She had learnt that word. It had taken her five. She’d found out what it was for; and it worked, it had worked on the Wednesday, you just said it, and it didn’t matter a bit, and nobody died. She had been so looking forward to doing it again.

“You taught me that,” said Goyle. “Didn’t you? Oh, your *face*. Look at you. You’re not hungry. You’ve got butter on the Herodotus. No, I won’t, you can’t make me, I’m going to stand here till you’ve had one. They’re not going to poison you. I’ll tell you what. Look.”

She had put out one big careful finger and thumb. She knew how to do that. She had hands that could lift a sheet of pastry thin enough to read through. She chose. She looked along the row, with her tongue between her lips, and picked out a very small dark perfect thing, from the corner. She held it up, in the sun. It had a bit of a gleam on it.

“Look,” said Goyle. “I’ll go first.”

*There*.

It took no time. It was all there. She’d thought that it would be slower. There was a good deal of room in it, and it was perfectly clear.

There was a word. It was quite short. It was at the front of her teeth. It wanted only one breath; she had breath; she was standing in a bar of sun, four yards from a girl with her mouth open. She only had to say it. *Poison*. She could say *Don’t*. She could say *That’s poison, Greer. It’s poison, it’s for Dumbledore, put it down*. And it would go out, into the room, and it lay along her vein with its small round eye. It heard what was said. It didn’t sleep. It might be a Sunday afternoon, in a long room, with the shutters closed. *The box*. He would hear that it had been opened. He’d know who had been told. There’d be a very large soft girl who knew what was in it; who had a mouth, and a friend; and on credit; on *credit*, Narcissa, you’re a family who understands these things. *I shall tell you when it falls due.* There was somebody who had taken a sponge from an elf. There was a small boy with his hand on a bow tie, who’d said *There are twenty-four*. *I have no further use for him.* Nothing to put on his arm. She saw it. She saw the whole of the column, all the way down, in the most beautiful clear hand, and she had got the answer before she’d got to the bottom. She had always been so very quick. She’d stood on a stool. It was perfect. It came out. It would come out to the last Knut.

Her arm was at her side.

She looked at that. There was a very small part of her, somewhere up near the ceiling, that did. It had a bottle of ink at the end of it. She knew what it could do. It could go up, that arm, by itself, from the shoulder, without being told. It had gone up in a passage behind the Charms corridor, as smooth as you please. It hadn’t asked her anything. It had been so *fast*. She had a wand in her sleeve, there was a word for this as well, *Accio*, you’d only need to think it. A child could do that. She waited. She stood there, in the sun, and she waited for it to go. She waited with the whole of herself. *Go on*, she said. *Go on. Go. Go*—

It stayed where it was. It hung there.

It had only ever been quick to save her.

Goyle’s eyes were shut. She’d got her lips closed. She had her head a little on one side, and her pale lashes lay on her pink cheeks, and she was chewing, very slowly, with enormous attention. Nobody had ever looked so happy. Her throat moved.

The dust went on going round.

“Oh,” said Greer Goyle. She breathed out, through her nose. “Oh, that’s *lovely*. That’s orange.”

It took a little over a minute.

She knew that. She’d read the label. It had been made for a very old, very strong man. She found that she had crossed the room. She didn’t know what had happened to the ink. She was on her knees on the boards, and she’d got something black all over her skirt, and she had hold of a hand. It was large and warm and rather damp. There was flour in the creases. It closed round hers, in its grey wool, in a comfortable sort of way.

There wasn’t a bezoar in the room.

She had thought of that first. It had been the first thing; she’d been over every inch of it in July, on her father’s carpet, with a list. There was a shelf in the Potions store with a jar of them, on the left. It was six minutes there and back. She had carried a box of poison for a hundred and forty-two days, and had got her sleeves right. She’d thought of everything. It had simply never occurred to her that she might want to have one by her. She’d never been going to want to save anybody.

“You’ve gone ever so white,” said Goyle.

She had opened her eyes. They were looking at Lyra, from quite close, with a kind and thoughtful expression. There was a small frown between them, of the kind that you’d have if you had heard a noise in another room.

“It’s got a sort of a… It’s got a bit of a kick, hasn’t it, at the back. What’s he put in that? I can’t get it. It’s not cloves.” She ran her tongue round her teeth. She swallowed. “It’s gone to my feet. That’s funny. I’ve got pins and needles. Is it cold in here? You don’t want to sit on that floor.”

She couldn’t say anything. It wasn’t that she’d chosen. She had her mouth open, and nothing whatever came out of it. There was a pulse going, under her thumb, in that big soft wrist. It was very fast. It stumbled, and went on. She held on. She had both hands round it now. She knew what you did with your hands. *Hold it*, somebody had said. She’d said that. On a hill.

The colour was going out of Goyle’s face. It went slowly. She looked down at it, at Lyra’s hands, in those dreadful gloves. She looked very much surprised. She seemed to be thinking about something, with some difficulty, a long way off. Her breath had got short. She had let the box slide a little on her knee, and she moved her other hand. Lyra saw it go. It was shaking, rather. It went out to the box. It went along the row, with one thick finger and thumb, feeling its way, very carefully, because she’d always been so careful; it took one up, by the edges. It was the next along.

She held it out.

It wasn’t very steady. It came across the small space between them, with its bit of a curl, at the end of that big pale floury hand. It stopped about an inch from Lyra’s lips. She could smell chocolate. Behind that, there was something faint and sweet and rather sharp, like the peel of an orange on a winter morning.

“Go on,” said Goyle.

It was hardly a breath. She was smiling. She’d got that frightful great slow smile, like all the shutters in a house. Her eyes had started to go past Lyra’s shoulder, and she brought them back. It took her a good deal of trouble. It was a thing that had to be got done. She had been trying to do that for six months. She’d been at it, one way and another, since the train.

“It’s a nice one,” said Greer Goyle.

Her fingers didn’t let go. They just stopped. There was nothing that you could have said had happened. Her head had gone over a little to the left. She sat there on the edge of the bed, in her scarf, with her boots in two wet marks, looking at something that was slightly behind Lyra; and the sun lay across her knees.

It was very quiet. Somebody on the lake was shouting, a long way off, and laughing.

Lyra stayed where she was.

She didn’t know how long. She thought that it was probably about as long as it takes to count to twenty. She counted. Then she reached up. She took the small dark thing out of those big warm fingers; it had started to go soft, and she had to work it free. She looked at it. She got to her feet. She took the box from where it lay, and she put that one back, in its place, in its paper, so that the row was straight. There was one gap. There was a small brown mark on the pleat. She picked up the card from the floor, and put it in its slot. She found the lid, on the blanket. She pressed down each corner.

She went over to the trunk and knelt down. She moved the winter things. She put it in the left boot. She put back the jerseys, one at a time, with the folds to the front. She closed the lid, and she turned the key, and she took out the key. She went back across the room, and looked at the floor. She rubbed out a place with her foot. She stood there. She looked to see that there wasn’t anything else. It was all quite tidy.

She took off her gloves. There was ink on them. She laid them on the bed.

*Then* she opened her mouth.

It wasn’t a word. It wasn’t anything that she had ever heard. It came from a long way under the floor. It went on, and on, and she heard it go along the passage and up the stone stairs, and she heard doors. She stood with her arms hanging, in the sun, beside a girl who was still smiling at her. Against her ribs, in a small glass phial with a neat twist of wire, there were twelve hours of the most tremendous luck. She had been saving that for Friday.

They came. There were a great many feet.

There was Parkinson’s cousin in the doorway, with her skates round her neck, and then she wasn’t. There was somebody being sick. Madam Pomfrey had got there, in nine minutes, with her cap crooked and her breath whistling. She didn’t ask anybody anything. She had Goyle laid down flat on the boards, and her scarf undone; she’d got her wand at the big soft throat, and her ear against the chest, and she stayed like that for a long time. She said “Come along, dear. Come along, now.” She said that several times, in a sharp cross voice. It got slower. She sat back on her heels.

There was someone else at the door. She hadn’t seen her come. She had got cold air on her, and a shawl. She’d got as far as the threshold, and stopped, with one hand on the frame; and she stood there, a thickset girl, holding a long black book against her chest. She didn’t come any further. She looked. She looked slowly and carefully and thoroughly, at the boots, and the two wet marks, and the big pale hand lying open on the floor. Her face didn’t change. There wasn’t any change that it could have made. She went on standing there, and nobody told her to go.

There was a smell of cold stone.

He came in without any sound. The black robes went by Lyra’s elbow, and stopped. He stood looking down, with his long yellow hands hanging. He looked at Pomfrey, and at the bed. Then he got down on one knee, stiffly. He bent his head. He lowered his long nose until it was about an inch from the slack pale mouth; and she saw his nostrils move, once, as they had moved over ten thousand cauldrons. He stayed there for perhaps as long as it takes to read a label.

He raised his eyes.

They came up from the floor and they found her. She was standing at the end of the bed. She hadn’t moved. She had ink on her hands, and all down her skirt. He looked at that. They went on. They went into the dark behind her eyes, with no more sound than a key; she didn’t stop him. She couldn’t have found the breakfast table. There wasn’t any table. There was a bar of sun, and it was all just lying there. He’d only to look.

Something happened to his throat. It was very slight. It might have been that he swallowed. A small white dint had appeared on either side of his nose; and for an instant she saw him stand on the edge of something, as you might see somebody at the far end of a long dark room, stopped, with a hand on a door. She didn’t know what it was. He looked older than she had thought. It cost him something. She could see that, she’d been very well taught. She could have told you what it cost to the Knut.

He got to his feet. He drew his robes straight.

“It was her heart,” said Snape.

My mother always said that you can’t go on—”

A chair went back.

It didn’t go very fast. Blaise Zabini had got to his feet. He was at the far side of the table, by himself, with his long hands flat on the wood. He was perfectly dressed. He was as grey as a dishcloth. He looked at Panther Parkinson. There was something in the set of his head, and he went on looking; she had known him for five and a half years, and she’d seen him lose everything that he had at cards, with a yawn. His lips had drawn back very slightly from his teeth. He didn’t say a word. He felt in his top pocket, and took out a small square of white card; and he laid it on the table, and put one finger on it, and pushed it across the wood. It stopped against Parkinson’s spoon.

It was blank.

Nobody breathed. Parkinson looked at it. He’d begun to shake. Zabini stood a moment longer. Then he picked up his glass, and looked into it, and set it down, and walked out, between the benches, down the whole length of the Hall, and his footsteps went on for quite a long time.

Heads had begun to turn. It went along the table towards her, in the candlelight. There were a lot of pale eyes. Montague was looking at her, with a sort of awe. *She was with her*, somebody was saying. *She found her. She was holding her hand*. It seemed that she was going to have to say something. They were waiting.  She looked at her soup. She took up her spoon, and it was quite steady, and she laid that down, a little to the right. She raised her eyes.

“How dreadful,” said Lyra. “Poor Goyle.”

It came out quite well. It had a little frown in it.
