# Year One, Part 6: The Black Pieces

After the Forest, Lyra decided to beat Potter at everything.

She did not announce it. A Malfoy did not announce things; a Malfoy simply arrived at the end of them, first, and let everybody else work out how. But she knew the very morning after, sitting at breakfast with a cup of tea she could not drink and her hands still remembering how they had shaken, that she was not going to spend one more day of her life going round the other side of a room because Harry Potter was in it.

She had been frightened of him for eight days. She had found that out on a path in the dark, when he said so. She was not going to be frightened of him for a ninth.

So she competed.

In Charms she had her feather in the air before Flitwick had finished saying the incantation, and kept it there, turning slowly, for the rest of the lesson, while Potter’s lay on the desk like something dead. In Herbology she knew the uses of every plant in Greenhouse One before Professor Sprout asked, and said them, in order, in a clear carrying voice. In History of Magic, where nobody ever said anything, she wrote eleven inches on the goblin rebellions when ten had been set, and handed it in on top of his, so that Binns would have to read hers first.

In Potions she lost.

It was very annoying. Something had happened to Potter in Potions that winter, and nobody could say what. In the autumn he had been merely bad, the sort of bad that comes from not listening, and Snape had enjoyed him enormously. Since about the middle of January he had been something else: he chopped without looking, and stirred by the clock on the wall instead of the one in the book, and twice, when the instructions said one thing, he did a different thing, quickly and without asking, and his potion came out the right colour and hers came out the colour it said in the book, which was a shade less right. Snape stood over his cauldron with his face like a shut door and could find nothing to take points for. It was the only lesson in which Lyra had ever seen Professor Snape look as though he would have preferred to be elsewhere.

“You’re going to make yourself ill,” said Goyle, on the fourth evening, in the library, with her head on a pile of Potions books. Lyra had made both of them come. “Why do you *care*? He’s a Gryffindor. Gryffindors are supposed to be bad at things.”

“He is not supposed to be better than me.”

“He’s better than everybody in Potions,” said Crabbe reasonably, “now. Even the fifth years.”

Lyra turned a page with some violence. “Then I shall be better than him at everything else, and he shall be better than me at one thing, and that will be the thing everybody remembers about him. *Harry Potter. Not bad at potions.*”

Crabbe looked at her across the table with her quill in her mouth.

“You’re not frightened of him any more,” she said.

“I was never frightened of him.”

Crabbe went on looking at her. Then she took the quill out of her mouth and went back to her essay, and did not say anything else, which was a habit of hers that Lyra was going to have to do something about one day.

It was the following week, on a Thursday, very late, that she followed him.

She had not meant to. She had been coming back from the Astronomy Tower, where she had gone after hours to look at Mars through somebody’s abandoned telescope, because Professor Sinistra had said it was red and Lyra had wanted to see whether that was true; and on the fourth-floor corridor, at the corner, she had seen a door close. Only the door. Nobody had gone through it. It had closed, quietly, on a room that had been dark, and a strip of light had come up under it.

She knew who went about the castle at night without being seen. Everybody in Slytherin knew; they had all decided, at the start of the year, that Potter must have some way of doing it, because he turned up in places at hours when he could not possibly be there, and Filch never caught him.

She opened the door.

It was a classroom nobody used, with the desks stacked against the walls. A lamp was burning on the windowsill. And in the middle of the floor, propped against the far wall as if it had been put down there by somebody who meant to come back for it, there was a mirror.

It was enormous. It went nearly to the ceiling, in a heavy gold frame on two clawed feet, and there were words carved round the top of it that she could not read from the door. And in front of it, with his back to her and a cloak bundled under his arm, was Potter.

He turned round when the door creaked. His face did something very fast.

“Malfoy—”

“What is *that*?”

“Nothing. It’s a mirror. Go back to bed.” He moved in front of it. “It isn’t— honestly, it isn’t anything. Go away.”

That decided it.

She went straight past him. He put a hand out, not quickly enough, and she was round him and in front of the glass before he could do anything about it, and she looked.

It was the classroom.

Not this one. The other one, on the ground floor, with the desks pushed back and the half-sentence about moonstone on the blackboard: the one she had sat in every night of the Christmas holidays with one lamp lit, and her father’s chess set, and nobody. It was there in the mirror instead of the room she was standing in, as clear as a window. The lamp was lit. The board was set out on the desk in the middle of the floor. And she was sitting at it, on the near side, with the white, with her back to the blackboard.

Somebody was sitting in the chair on the far side.

She could not see who. The lamp was on the desk between them, and whoever it was sat just back from its light, so that there was nothing of them she could see but a shape, and a sleeve, and a hand. The hand came forward into the light and picked up a black knight, and hesitated over the board for a long, uncertain moment, and put it down on what was quite plainly the worst square available.

Lyra laughed out loud.

She could not help it. It came up out of her before she knew it was there, in the silent classroom, and in the mirror the girl at the chessboard was laughing too, with her head back, the way she had not laughed since September. It was such a *bad* move. It was so exactly the kind of move the black pieces had made all through Christmas, night after night, the wrong piece to the wrong square, so that the bishop had complained to her about it in the mornings: patient, and hopeless, and there.

She knew who it was. She had worked it out on the third day, on the dungeon stairs.

“It’s the Baron,” she said.

She had never seen him play, only the board in the morning, moved. And here he was, the Bloody Baron, playing the black badly and having the courtesy to keep his terrible face out of the light.

Behind her, Potter made a small noise.

She turned round. He was standing exactly where she had left him, with the cloak under his arm, and he had gone a deep, slow, astonishing red from the collar of his pyjamas to his hair.

“What?”

“Nothing.” He looked at the ceiling. “The Baron. Right.”

“He played with me,” said Lyra. “At Christmas. When there was nobody. Every night.” She did not know why she was telling him. It was the most private thing she owned, and she had just handed it to Harry Potter in a disused classroom at midnight as if it were a sweet. She turned back to the glass. In it, the shape in the far chair had sat back again, out of the light, waiting for her move. “I never thanked him properly. You can’t thank the Baron properly. He doesn’t answer.”

“No.” Potter’s voice sounded strange. “I don’t suppose he does.”

She stood in front of the mirror for a long time. Then she read the words carved round the top of the frame, which went backwards; and she worked them out, as she worked everything out, and understood what it was.

*Not your face. Your heart’s desire.*

She stepped back from it, fast.

That was not what she had expected her heart’s desire to be. She had expected, if she had expected anything, something with a great deal more gold in it: her father’s hand on her shoulder, perhaps, at the head of the Slytherin table, or Professor Snape saying *Faultless* in front of the whole class, or a Quidditch Cup. Not a chessboard in an empty room, and a ghost who played badly, and herself laughing.

She did not look at it again.

“What do you see?” she asked, without turning round. “In it. You were standing there before I came in. You were looking.”

There was a pause.

“Nothing much,” said Potter. “People.”

“Liar.”

“Yeah. Probably.”

She did turn round then. He had the bundled cloak under one arm and his hand on the door latch. He was looking at her as though she had said something kind, and he could not think why.

“Whatever it is, I hope it’s something stupid. I hope it’s a Chocolate Frog card you haven’t got.”

“It isn’t.”

He smiled at her, a small, crooked smile quite unlike the one he put on for the whole Great Hall.

“Night, Malfoy.” He opened the door and went out, closing it quietly behind him.

She lay awake for a long time that night, in the dormitory under the lake, with the green light moving on the ceiling.

She had been unhappy, more or less, since the night before Christmas, when the house-elves had cleared away the plates in the Great Hall and she had walked down to the dungeons alone. She lay there now and waited for the familiar ache. It did not come.

She thought about the hand coming into the lamplight, and the knight going down on the worst square on the board.

She wanted to sit down and make her move and have somebody there to answer it. Even badly. Even a ghost.

She would beat Potter at Charms tomorrow. She would beat him at everything, all term, and all of next year, and as long as she could, and it would be his part to lose, as it was hers to win.

But she would not tell anybody, ever, what the words round the top of the mirror said. Least of all him.
