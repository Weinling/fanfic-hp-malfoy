# Chapter 70: Cousins

Her mother’s answer came on the Tuesday, in a seed catalogue.

It was the summer list from a firm in Ross-on-Wye, and it arrived with a page turned down at the delphiniums and a note in the margin about staking. Lyra took it to bed with her. She drew the hangings, lit the end of her wand, and held the back of page thirty-one above the flame of the small brass spirit lamp she kept for her hair; and for some time nothing happened, and then, as a blush comes up in somebody who has been told not to, the writing rose out of the paper, pale brown, and very straight.

*He has answered. I wrote as he would wish to be written to, which is to say without one word of affection. My owl was three days finding him and came back looking as if it had been made to apologise. He says that I may go to hell. He says that if this is one of Lucius’s he will feed me to the crabs. He will be at the end of the Cobb at Lyme on the eighteenth at noon, where we were taken as children, and he says that I had better bring it.*

*He wants to see the hand. He said so. It is the only civil thing that he has said to me since 1977.*

*I have not been past the gates since Easter. I shall take the Portkey to my dressmaker’s at Charmouth, as I do every June, and I shall walk. If I am asked, I shall tell him that it was about Regulus. It will even be true.*

*Bella came in as I was answering him, to borrow the wax. She is so much better. She has taken to sitting with me in the mornings.*

*Don’t write again. Stake your delphiniums.*

She read it through twice. Bella had been there. Her mother would have covered the letter; she must have. Lyra held the lamp steady and read the time again. A thing she had been holding at arm’s length, for a good many days, without noticing the ache, came slowly down. *At noon.* He would be a hundred and forty miles from London, in a Muggle waistcoat, on a stone wall in Dorset, with his back to the sea, being rude to her mother. At noon on the eighteenth the only person that anybody could use for bait would be eating an ice, for all she knew; and he would go on being somewhere else all afternoon. Harry would turn over his paper in the Great Hall at two, and the dream would come or it wouldn’t. He could sit there with his quill and know exactly what it was. He wouldn’t go. And even if he went, there was nobody there.

She had done that. She had moved a piece on a board that neither of them could see, by a cousin, over the top of the Dark Lord’s head, with a lemon. She blew out the lamp. The ring on its ribbon had gone a soft contented pink. She lay in the dark with her hands folded on her chest, and for the first time in nine days she could feel her feet.

Page thirty-one went into the common-room fire at breakfast time, with the delphiniums.

She had seen him give it to Granger on the Saturday.

She hadn’t been looking for it. She had come up from the library with a stack of *Numerology and Grammatica*, and they were all out on the front steps in the sun, with their notes round them, fifty people murmuring Transfiguration to the grass. Hermione Granger sat by herself on the end of the balustrade, a little way from where Weasley lay on his back with a book on his face. She had got thin. She looked as though she had been left out in the rain and dried too quickly. Harry came down the steps with his bag on his shoulder and his hands in his pockets, whistling, and as he went by her, without stopping, without turning his head, he put something down on the stone beside her knee. It was a square of parchment, folded very small.

He went on. He didn’t look back. Granger sat perfectly still for about as long as it took him to reach the grass. Then, not looking either, she laid one finger on the square, and drew it towards her, and it went into the green-tabbed notebook, and she snapped the elastic band.

So there was something that one could put in writing. And there was somebody to put it to.

Lyra had stood at the top of the steps, and shifted the books to her other arm. She had felt, quite distinctly, under the ribs on the left-hand side, a small cold pang of a kind she had not had since the Yule Ball; and then she had laughed at herself, down her nose, without any sound. She had a paper pattern. She could hardly complain. *Who were you with?* She knew exactly how that went.

And on the Monday night she’d had the atlas in her hand.

He had fallen asleep; he did that now, all at once, like a child, with his mouth open. He was lying on the rug with Bagshot on his chest. His bag had fallen over by the fender, and spilled, as it always did, a tide of broken quills and Chocolate Frog cards, and she had gone over, for something to do, to put it back. It was a large floppy paper thing with a torn cover. *AA Road Atlas of Great Britain.* It had a brown ring on the corner, and it fell open a little as she lifted it, of its own accord, at a page that had been opened a great deal. She could see a line of pencil going across the fold. All that she needed to do was move her thumb.

She didn’t, in the end. She stood there and looked at her thumb for some time.

She could not have said that it was honour. She had a reasonable idea of what that was worth. But she was going home in a fortnight, to a house with a south wing, and whatever there was on that page, somebody would be standing just inside the door of her head with his boots wiped, asking if he might look at it. What she didn’t know he could not find. Harry had thought of that. She saw that he had, all at once, as you see the second half of a combination. It was why he had given it to Granger. He had looked at the only two people in the world he could tell, and one of them hated him and could be trusted. He had left the other one out, to keep her safe; and there was a sort of insolence in that, and it went to her heart.

She closed the atlas, and put it in the bag, with the Frogs on top. Then she stepped over him and turned down the lamp.

Well, she thought. That made two of them.

Arithmancy was on the Wednesday, in the small hall behind the Charms corridor, and there were only nine candidates.

There was no help for it: the desks went by the alphabet of something, and when she came in with her pens she found she had been placed one aisle over and half a row behind that thin straight back and that bush of hair. She had sat near that back for five years. She had looked up in the middle of a hundred lessons and found a pair of brown eyes resting on her, with a level, adding, unforgiving attention, as if she were a column that had come out wrong. She had complained of it. She’d said a number of things about it, in several common rooms.

Granger’s eyes passed over her in the corridor at a quarter past nine. They did not stop. They were perfectly civil; they went by as they’d go by a coat on a peg.

It had not occurred to Lyra that that was a thing one could miss.

“Your cuff’s in the ink,” she said, at the end of the second hour, when they had been told to lay down their pens.

Granger moved her cuff.

There were eleven questions on the paper, and the last had been set by a person with a grudge. Lyra got it out on the fourth sheet at seven minutes to twelve. It came down to a single beautiful line, as the very good ones do, with a sort of click, and she sat looking at it with her pen in her hand and her whole head ringing. She had got all of it. She knew. She had not got one thing wrong. She went out into the corridor walking rather quickly, past the people comparing answers, and she was halfway up the fifth-floor stairs before she noticed that she was going to tell somebody. It had never happened before. She had been top of everything for five years, and she had always gone and sat by herself for a bit, and then written to her father.

He had told her to come at four. He met her at the door of the room, with his glasses steamed up and a smear of something on his forehead, and he took her by the shoulders and steered her round so that she faced the wall.

“Don’t look. You can’t look yet. How was it?”

“I got it all,” said Lyra. “Every line. There was one at the end that nobody on earth could have—Potter, what is that *smell?*”

“All of it?”

“All of it.”

“I knew it!” He let go of her, and she heard him crash into something. “I *knew*— All right. All right, turn round. Don’t say anything. I did it myself. The elves only showed me where the oven was, and one of them had to go and lie down.”

She turned round.

There was a plate on the seat of the chair. There was something on the plate. She looked at it with attention. It had been intended, she thought, as a cake; it had been round, at some stage, and had sunk in the middle and risen on the left, and someone had tried to correct this with a thick grey-green icing, and the icing had set off down the slope and stopped. Across the top, with a very great deal of care, in letters of a violent yellow, leaning on one another for support, was a single word.

*TAU.*

“That’s Latin,” said Harry. “For yours. You said.”

He was looking at her with such a terrible hopeful pride that she had to bite the inside of her cheek. She walked round the plate. She took out her wand, and tapped the icing, and it rang.

“It’s a Greek letter,” said Lyra.

“What?”

“*Tau*. It’s the nineteenth letter of the Greek alphabet. It comes after sigma. You’ve baked me a cake, Potter, in honour of my Arithmancy examination, with a *constant* on it.”

She watched that go across his face. She watched him look at the cake, and he opened his mouth. And she began to laugh; she couldn’t help it, it got out from under her ribs, and she sat down on the rug with her hands over her face and howled. He stood over her saying, “No, but it’s—you *said* it— Is it really? But that’s even better!” in a rising voice, and then he had gone too, and they sat on either side of the plate, helpless, with their eyes running, not able to look at each other, or the thing between them, which had begun very slowly to subside.

She ate it. There was salt in it, and a certain amount of shell. It had the texture of a wet Sunday in Surrey. She held out her plate and she had a second piece, sitting cross-legged on the bald rug with the window open and the swifts going over, and she told him about the eleventh question, from the beginning, with diagrams, on the back of his Potions notes. He didn’t understand one word of it. He sat with his chin in his hand and his mouth full, nodding, and he looked at her the whole time; and when she got to the line, the beautiful one, she found that she had drawn it for him in icing on the edge of the plate, and he looked down at it, and up.

“That’s *you*, that is,” said Harry. “All of that.”

She kissed him. It was only a small one; he had crumbs on his chin, and she had to lean across the plate to do it. The sun lay along the side of his glasses, and she had a pain in her chest from laughing; and she thought, as clearly as she’d ever thought anything: *I got all of it. There isn’t one thing wrong.*

“The third flagstone rocks,” said Snape.

He said it the following afternoon, without raising his eyes from the essay he was marking, when she had sat in the upright chair for twenty minutes in the green underwater light and given him the breakfast table, the sideways sun and the silver, with nothing out of its place. She had thought they had finished. He had told her that there would be no lesson next week. She had got as far as picking up her bag.

“Sir?”

“It has rocked for fourteen years. I have had a number of offers to see to it. I find that it tells me when someone is walking in the passage, and when they have stopped.” His pen moved, and crossed something out. “You will have been revising your Herbology.”

Her hands had gone quite cold. She kept them on the bag.

She had rehearsed this, in one form or another, since the middle of May. She had a very good face for it. He lifted his long yellow head then and looked at her, across the desk, with his flat black eyes; and neither of them said anything. There was a jar on the shelf behind him with something pale in it, turning slowly. The whole of what she had heard through that door lay on the table between them, like a thing that a cat has brought in, and they both looked at it, without looking.

“You sit at that table, sir,” said Lyra. “You answer questions.”

“I do.”

“And he has not yet—”

“If he had, Miss Malfoy, you’d have read about it in the *Prophet*.” The thin mouth moved a very little. “I have been lying to the greatest Legilimens in the history of magic since before you could walk. I don’t propose to discuss how. You have one room in that head. I told you so. I suggest you find that there is nothing in it about flagstones.”

“Yes, sir.”

She had got as far as the door.

“Your report of the nineteenth of May,” said Snape, “has been very much admired.”

She stopped.

“It was read out. I was present. It is the only one of your productions that he has asked to hear twice.” He had gone back to the essay. His voice had no more expression than the scratch of the nib. “He laughed. In seventeen years I have heard him laugh on two occasions, and I’d advise you to think about what was happening on the other. He has not asked you anything since, I believe. No. He wouldn’t. I should be careful, Miss Malfoy, of supposing that a man who has stopped asking questions has stopped wanting to know. In my experience, it is what they do when they have started to enjoy themselves.”

She stood a moment with her fingers on the cold iron of the latch. A hundred and forty miles, she thought. The end of the Cobb, at noon.

“Thank you, sir,” said Lyra. “Will that be all?”

He turned over the page.

“I have no idea,” said Snape.

It came on the Monday, with the supper things. It was the seventeenth of June, in the last hour of a long hot day, and half the fifth year had gone out to lie on the grass with their heads in their History notes. There had been Astronomy and Divination that week, and Muggle Studies for those who cared to; there was only the one paper left. The owl was her father’s, and he had sealed it with the small seal.

She took it to the window at the end of the Slytherin corridor, where the lake lay against the glass, and the light came through it green.

There was no gap in it. She looked. He had not troubled with one for months. It was a single side, and the hand went down across the page like something falling slowly down a flight of steps.

*This will be the last thing I ask of you.*

*The boy must be out of the castle tomorrow, by four o’clock. I do not ask how you will do it, and you need not know why. You have always been able to make him go where you wished. I don’t think you can have any idea how much I have relied on that.*

*He has told me that if it is done, he will have no occasion to trouble Draco.*

*I have been asked to tell you that, my treasure, and I have told you. I believe that I am now supposed to say something more. I find I am very tired. You were the cleverest child that I ever saw. You stood on a stool to read my ledgers. I should have liked to have had you for a little longer before all this.*

*Burn it.*

She stood for a long time.

The green light moved on the page, and something large went by outside the glass, far down, without hurry. She could hear the Slytherin fifth-years coming back along the passage, and Parkinson saying something about Goblin Rebellions. She did the sum. She was quick at it. She had been doing it all her life, on a stool. On one side there was a clear small voice, and a slice of bread and butter cut into strips, and a boy who no longer ran. He could have that back. Or he could have something, some part of it; he might be let off whatever a truthful boy was going to be needed for. It was all perfectly plain. She need only say a word in the chair, tonight. She had always been able to make him go where she wished.

*Them or me.*

She’d been asked that once before, and she had been told in the same breath what it would cost. She had thought, when she answered, that she’d have to do it only the once. It hadn’t struck her that a person might be sent the same bill twice, with a kinder note.

Was he lying? She held on to that for a moment. He was very probably lying. It was what he did. And it couldn’t matter, she told herself, because there was nothing there tomorrow but a trap with nothing in it, and the boy would not go, not for her or anybody; he knew that road. That was all quite true. She noticed that it made no difference.

She had a small flat silver box for her hairpins. She put the letter in it, and touched it with the end of her wand. It went up with a little sigh. She stirred what was left with a pin, and shook it out through the crack at the bottom of the window, where it hung for a moment in the green water, and sank.

She stayed until her hands had stopped. Then she went up to him.

He was in the chair. He had his feet on the fender and the window was open, and he was holding *A History of Magic* more or less the right way up, and he wasn’t reading it. He moved over. She sat down beside him, and he put the book down.

Neither of them said anything for rather a long time.

It was the quietest she had ever known him. He had stopped whistling at about the weekend. He wasn’t grey, though, and he was not on the stairs; he had the look of someone who has packed. He took her hand and turned it over, and went on holding it, with his thumb lying across the palm. Now and then he breathed in, as if he were about to say something, and let it go. She could feel him thinking about tomorrow. She could feel him being pleased with it, in a tight, secret, frightened sort of way. He’d got that small grim look, of having seen the move. It was exactly the look that she could feel on her own face. They must make a very pretty picture, the pair of them; sitting there in the dusk, each with a cousin in their pocket.

*It’ll be over this time tomorrow.* She nearly said it. She could have told him. She could have turned her head and said, *He’s going to be in Dorset, you idiot, I’ve seen to it, you can put away your atlas.* It would have been the greatest pleasure of her life. She could have told him about the letter as well. She would have had to tell him about the egg. A day, she thought. Let it be done first. I’ll tell him when it’s done. She would tell him all of it, on the rug, with her feet up, and she’d watch his face.

“Malfoy,” said Harry.

“Mm.”

“Will you do something? Tomorrow. Take that off the ribbon, and wear it on your finger.” He said it quite lightly, to the fire. “Just for the day. I don’t care who sees. I’d like to know it’s there.”

She looked at him. She thought of several remarks. It was not at all the sort of thing that she did; there were two hundred people who took their whole view of the universe from where Lyra Malfoy did and did not wear jewellery. Then she put her free hand up to her collar, and drew out the green ribbon, and she picked the knot undone with one fingernail. It took a little while. The ring slid down into her palm, and it weighed nothing. She put it on the fourth finger of her left hand, where she had put it when she was thirteen, and it went on as if it had never been away.

“There,” said Lyra. “Now you can’t say I never gave you anything.”

He laughed, under his breath. He lifted the hand and looked at it, and he laid it against the side of his face for a moment, and he shut his eyes.

The light went. She had to be down by ten. They went out by the door in the wall and along the empty seventh floor, and at the head of the marble staircase, where you could see the great hourglasses standing in their niches a long way below, each with its small dull gleam, they stopped, as they always stopped, to look over the banister for Filch.

She heard his breath go in.

It was a small sound. She looked round. He had stopped with one foot on the top step, and his hand had gone up to his forehead. He wasn’t rubbing it. He just had his fingers there, quite lightly, as you’d touch something to see if it was hot. He stood frowning down into the dark of the stairwell. He looked like somebody who has heard his name said, in a house where he had thought he was alone.

“What is it?”

He didn’t answer at once. Down in the Entrance Hall, in the silence, you could just hear the emeralds falling, one at a time.

“Nothing,” said Harry slowly. “It’s nothing. It’s only— It doesn’t usually—”

“Potter.”

He took his hand down, and looked at it. He turned to her, and he smiled, and it was very nearly all right.

“He’s happy,” said Harry.
