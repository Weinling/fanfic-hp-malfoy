# Chapter 48: Lines

“A little supervision,” said Professor Umbridge. “That is all I ask, dear.”

She had stopped Lyra at the end of Defence, on the last Thursday of September, with one small plump hand on her sleeve, and drawn her aside to the window while the others went out, as one draws aside a favourite niece at a wedding to say something about the bride.

“Mr Potter,” she said, “has a further week with me, I’m afraid. He has not yet learned his lesson. I have tried, Miss Malfoy, I really have, but some children seem positively to enjoy being corrected.” She sighed. “And tomorrow I am called to the Ministry. Most inconvenient. The Minister likes to be kept informed. I wondered whether you would sit with him for me. Merely to see that he does his lines, and does not leave before he is finished. One wants someone *reliable*.”

“Of course, Professor.”

She said it at once. She had been waiting since the second week of term to be asked something like it; that was what an informant was for, and she had been one now for three weeks, and had told Umbridge exactly four things, all of them true and none of them worth a Knut. It would look very odd to say no to a little supervision. It would look odder to want to.

At five o’clock on the Friday she knocked on the door of Professor Umbridge’s office, and went in, and he was already there.

⁂

It was a horrible room.

She had expected it to be, after the kittens in the classroom, and it was worse: everything in it was pink, or frilled, or both, and every surface was covered in little lace cloths with vases of dried flowers on them, and the wall behind the desk was covered from floor to ceiling in plates. There were dozens of them. Each one had a kitten on it, and the kittens were all moving, frisking and pouncing and washing their paws, and there was something about so many small things moving at once in the corner of one’s eye that made one want to put one’s hand over the side of one’s face.

Potter was sitting at a little table under the window, with a long piece of parchment in front of him and a quill in his hand. He looked up when she came in.

He did not look surprised. He looked, for one second, as though somebody had walked up behind him and put a cold hand on the back of his neck; and then that went, and he looked at her badge, and at her face, and something came into his own that she disliked a great deal more.

“Malfoy,” he said. “Of course.”

“Professor Umbridge has been called away.” Lyra shut the door behind her and sat down in Umbridge’s chair, behind the desk, which was too low, and arranged her robes. “I am to see that you do your lines. And that you don’t leave before you have finished. She was most particular.”

“I bet she was.”

He bent over the parchment again. He was holding the quill oddly, she noticed, in his right hand, with the back of the hand turned away from her, towards the window, as though there was something on it he did not want looked at. He wrote. There was no inkpot on the table. She noticed that too, a moment after, and thought that Umbridge must have given him one of those self-inking quills that the Ministry used, very expensive, and that it was a great deal of quill to waste on a detention.

She took out her Arithmancy, and opened it, and read nothing.

The kittens frisked.

“How many?”

“She didn’t say. ‘Until it sinks in.’”

“And has it?”

He looked up. He was very pale, and there was sweat on his upper lip, though the room was not warm; and his eyes behind his glasses were perfectly steady, and amused, and tired to the bone.

“What do you think?”

“I think that if you had kept your mouth shut in the first lesson, you would be in the common room now, eating toast, and I would be on the sixth floor confiscating Dungbombs from the Weasley twins, and we should both be a great deal happier.”

“Probably. Toast would be nice.”

“So why didn’t you?”

He went on writing. The quill scratched. It was a curious sound, she thought: thinner than a quill ought to make, and wetter.

“Because she’s lying,” he said, to the parchment. “And they’re all going to believe her, because it’s easier. And somebody’s got to say it, out loud, in a room, every time, or in a year nobody will.” He wrote another line. “It doesn’t much matter who.”

“It matters to you, I should think.” She turned a page of her Arithmancy. “Since it’s your hand.”

The quill stopped.

He kept his head bent over the parchment. When he spoke again his voice was flatter, as if he were reading it off the page.

“We’re not going to do this, are we. You’re her prefect. You’re sitting in her chair. You went to sleep on my shoulder on Saturday, and you came here to watch.”

“I came here because I was asked.”

“Same thing.”

“It is not the same thing in the least. Whatever you imagine that is, Potter, on the seventh floor—” She heard her own voice, and kept it level by main force. “Whatever that is. It isn’t this. It stops at the door of this room. I told you so in June. I am on one side of this and you are on the other, and the side I am on has kittens on its wall, and I sit where I am put. That is all.”

He looked at her then.

“Yeah. I know where you’re sitting.”

And he bent his head, and turned the quill in his fingers, and wrote; and it was not the same as before. She could see it from the desk, even upside down, even across the room: the shape of his hand changed. The long even lines he had been writing for an hour broke off, and underneath the last of them he was writing something shorter, slowly, pressing hard, as though he had to get it right the first time because there would not be another.

“That is not your line,” said Lyra.

He went on writing.

“Potter. You have been told what to write. If you think I am going to sit here while you scribble your opinions of Professor Umbridge on Ministry parchment—”

She got up. She came round the desk, and across the pink carpet, and put out her hand for the parchment, to take it off him, to read whatever cheap insult he had put there, so that she could tell him exactly how cheap it was.

She read it.

There were the lines. *I must not tell lies*, over and over, in his small untidy hand, a column of them going down the parchment, and they were not written in ink. They were red. They were a dark, wet, shining red, and the last of them was still wet; and underneath the last of them, by itself, in the same red, a little uneven at the end where his hand had shaken, three words.

*I love you.*

She looked at it.

Then she looked at his hand, because she could not help it, because he had stopped trying to keep it turned away. On the back of his right hand, across the bones of it, there were letters. They had been cut there, or scratched, or something worse, and healed, and been cut again, so that the skin was raised and red and raw round the edges of them, and they said *I must not tell lies*, the whole line, from his knuckles to his wrist; and under them, quite fresh, opening as she watched as if an invisible nib were going over them a second time, three more, very small.

There was blood on the parchment, and blood on the little pink tablecloth, and a dark spot of it on the carpet by his chair that she had walked straight past.

She had not known. She had sat in Umbridge’s chair and opened her Arithmancy, and he had been writing in his own blood under her nose for an hour, and she had listened to the quill and thought it sounded wet.

He had kept that hand in his pocket on the seventh floor, and she had thought it was cheek. He had polished fifty-four cups with the wrong one, and she had told him it was the most affected thing she had ever seen. *I got polish in a cut*, he had said; and she had won by four.

⁂

She stood there with the parchment in her hand.

There was a thing one did. She knew the thing. She had had it done for her, on a staircase, in the dark, by somebody she could not see; a clean square of cloth put into her hand, washed so many times it was soft as silk. She felt for it. Her hand went to the pocket of her robes, where a handkerchief would be, where it ought to be, before she had told it to; and the pocket was empty.

His handkerchief was in the drawer of her bedside table in Wiltshire, folded small, pressed. She had taken it out of a tin in June and put it there, and kept it, and left it behind, and here she was in a pink room with blood on the carpet and nothing at all in her pocket to give him.

She did not take his hand. She wanted to so much that it frightened her, and she did not.

He was looking up at her. His hand lay on the table between them, bleeding. He did not move it away.

Lyra folded the parchment in half, along its length, so that the lines and the three words were on the inside, and laid it down on the table in front of him.

“Five points from Gryffindor,” she said.

Her voice sounded perfectly steady.

“For writing what you were not told to write.”

She went back round the desk. She picked up her Arithmancy, and her bag, and her wand, and went to the door, and opened it, and stopped there with her back to him and her hand on the frame.

“Your hour is up,” she said. “Go to the hospital wing. Tell Pomfrey you cut yourself on something. She’ll put murtlap on it, if she has any sense.”

“Malfoy—”

She shut the door on whatever it was.

⁂

There was a stretch of corridor outside Umbridge’s office, between two windows, where the torches had not been lit yet. She got as far as that, and no further.

She put her back against the wall and held her bag to her chest, with her eyes shut and her teeth together. Every so often her breath went in sharply, and she let it, because there was nobody to see. She counted the hours he had been in that room, last week and this, with that woman across the desk smiling at him. She got to the letters on the back of his hand, and had to begin again.

*I love you*, in his own blood, on a Ministry form, under her nose, and she had taken five points for it.

He had never said it. Not even in the Owlery, when she had told him it was over and he had said *this year*. She had not said it either. They had debts and rings and a sugar quill bent in the middle; she had thought the words themselves were for other people.

He had found a way to say it that she could not answer, and could not give back, and could not put on any account in the world. She could not decide whether he had meant to give her something or to make her watch him bleed.

At the end of the corridor a door opened, and footsteps went away in the other direction, slow, and a little uneven: towards the hospital wing, she hoped. She did not open her eyes until they had gone.

Professor Umbridge stopped her after breakfast on the Saturday, in the Entrance Hall, with her little black bow bobbing and her handbag on her arm.

“Well, dear?” she said. “How did we get on?”

Lyra looked at her. She looked at the pink, and the smile, and the small plump hand on the clasp of the bag, that had held out a quill to a fifteen-year-old boy every evening for three weeks and told him to write until it sank in.

“Very instructive, Professor,” said Lyra. “Wouldn’t ordinary ink do as well? He made rather a mess of the tablecloth.”

Umbridge’s smile thinned. “The quill is part of his correction, dear. If he cannot complete it under supervision, I shall have to begin the week again myself.” She adjusted the clasp of her bag. “You need only supervise. Unless you would rather not?”

“I can supervise.”

“I am so glad.” Umbridge patted her arm. “I knew you would be a help.”

“May I take Monday’s as well?”

Umbridge looked delighted.

Lyra walked away with her back straight. She could refuse, and he would have Umbridge across the desk on Monday. She could stay and find something for his hand. That was what she had chosen.

⁂

On the Sunday she went down to the Potions storeroom, which a prefect might enter on a prefect’s errand, and took a small stoppered jar from the third shelf on the left, and wrote it in the book in her own hand, *Murtlap essence, one ounce, Slytherin common room, minor cuts*, which was very nearly true.

Crabbe found her in the dormitory afterwards, sitting on the end of her bed with the jar in her lap, turning it round and round in her fingers so that the yellowish liquid inside caught the green light from the lake.

Crabbe looked at the jar. Then at Lyra’s face. Then, for a long moment, at the jar again.

“Somebody cut themselves,” said Crabbe.

“On something.”

Crabbe sat down beside her, carefully, as she had sat down on Christmas night in a yellow dress, and did not ask on what. After a while she said, to the floor, in her low reasonable voice, “My cousin’s in Hufflepuff. Fourth year. She says Umbridge has had three of them in that office since term started, and none of them will say what they did in there, and one of them keeps his hand in his pocket.” She paused. “I just thought you’d want to know. Being a prefect.”

Lyra put the jar in the pocket of her robes.

“Thank you, Vanessa,” she said.

⁂

On the Monday, at five o’clock, she was in Umbridge’s chair again before he came, with her Arithmancy open on the desk in front of her and the jar beside it.

He came in, and saw her, and stopped in the doorway. His right hand was bandaged across the back. Somebody had done it badly, and he had undone it, by the look of it, and done it again worse.

“Sit down, Potter,” said Lyra. “You have lines.”

He sat. The quill was on the table, waiting, where Umbridge always left it. He picked it up, and looked at it, and then at her.

Lyra kept her eyes on her Arithmancy. She reached out with one hand, without looking, and pushed the little stoppered jar across the desk towards the edge nearest him, an inch, and then another inch, until it stood exactly at the edge, and left it there, and took her hand back, and turned a page.

There was a silence.

Then she heard the chair scrape, and his feet on the pink carpet, and the jar lifted from the edge of the desk; and the chair again. She kept her eyes on the page. She heard the stopper come out, and a small breath, quickly let go, that was not a word; and then, after a long time, the quill, writing, the thin wet sound of it going on and on in the pink room under the kittens, line after line, until the hour was up.

He wrote nothing else under the line. She would have known. She was listening for it the whole time.

At six o’clock he put down the quill and stood up. He put the jar back, on the very edge of the desk, where she had left it, a little emptier. He did not say thank you. She would not have known what to do with it if he had.

“Same time tomorrow?” said Potter.

“Yes,” said Lyra, to her Arithmancy.

He went to the door.

“Potter.”

He stopped.

“Keep the jar.”
