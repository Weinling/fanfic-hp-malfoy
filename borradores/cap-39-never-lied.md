# Chapter 39: Never Lied

On the Sunday nobody knew anything.

The Durmstrang boys ate breakfast at the Slytherin table, very quietly, and went down to the shore afterwards in a body and stood there looking at the water. Professor Dumbledore made a short speech at lunch about courage, and the Cup, and Cedric Diggory, who had been brave and was in the hospital wing with a lump on his head the size of an egg and had asked, apparently, that nobody throw anything at Harry Potter on his account. Potter was not at lunch. Moody was, and Snape was, and so was everybody else; and nobody said a word about a ship, or a coat on a chair, or a man in a neat dark suit carrying a crate across a lawn, because nobody but Lyra seemed to have seen any of it.

On the Monday her father’s owl came down the Great Hall at breakfast.

It landed by her plate as it always did, and held out its leg as it always did, and the letter was in her father’s hand, the beautiful sloping hand that had written to her every week of her life; and she knew before she had broken the seal. She knew from the way he had written her name on the outside. He had pressed so hard on the *L* that the nib had caught.

She read it at the table, with the Slytherins round her eating their kippers. It was a page about Draco and a broom in a catalogue, and whether a boy of ten could be trusted with a Nimbus, and what her mother thought of it; and in the middle of the second paragraph, between *your mother* and *says*, there was a gap in the line, the width of two fingers.

She put her wand under the table, and touched its tip to the blank, and the parchment split along its thickness into two thin layers, and between them, in her father’s hand but very much smaller, was the other letter.

*My treasure,*

*Our master has returned.*

*I would have you hear it from me before you hear it from anybody else. He is himself again. He is gracious. And our family stands higher in his favour than I had dared to hope, thanks in no small measure to you, my darling. Your errand in November was a piece of a great work, and he knows whose hand carried it.*

*He has asked after a certain book. I have told him what we know: that it was taken in the Ministry’s raids, the summer that Weasley’s people went through our cellars, and never seen again. You will say the same, if you are asked.*

*You were never anything but ours. I have not doubted it for a moment.*

*Your father.*

Lyra touched the blank again, and the layers closed, and she folded it along its creases.

Her hands did it quite neatly. She put the letter into her sleeve, against the inside of her wrist, where her wand usually lay, and she buttered a piece of toast, and ate half of it.

*Your errand in November.* A sealed letter in green wax, in a hand she did not know, carried up a gangplank by a girl who thought she was using her father to open a door. A piece of a great work.

*The raids.* She had thought of that herself. She had sat across the breakfast table from him at Christmas, eighteen months ago, and handed it to him like a gift, and watched his face light up; and he had used it, and it had held, and now it was what he had told the Dark Lord, and it was what she would say if she were asked. She would say it, too. She knew that already. She would say it beautifully.

And on the end of her bed, that same Christmas, she had sworn to be at his feet before the others had found their masks: at the front, on her knees, making herself so useful for so long that one lost diary would be a thing he could afford to overlook. Even if it meant turning on Potter.

It had been an idea, then. A thing a girl promised herself in a bedroom, in the dark, against a day that might never come.

It had come.

Crabbe, across the table, had stopped eating and was watching her. Lyra put down the rest of the toast, and stood up, and went to Charms.

She carried the letter in her sleeve all day: through Charms and Transfiguration and lunch and a double free period in the library where she did not open a book, against the inside of her wrist, where she could feel the edge of it every time she moved. At six o’clock, on the stairs up from the dungeons, she turned the ring once round her finger with her thumb and thought, very clearly and very coldly, of a boy; and felt it begin to warm.

He was in the Owlery when she got there.

She had known he would be. The ring had pulled her up the west staircase and out along the cold stone passage and up the last winding flight to the top of the tower, warm and then warmer, and it had stopped pulling at the door; and when she pushed it open he was standing at the far window, in the straw, with his back to the light and his hands in his pockets, and every owl in the place awake and watching him.

He had not waited for her at the foot of the dungeon steps for three weeks, and he did not come towards her now. He looked at her face, and whatever he saw in it, he stayed where he was.

“You called,” said Potter.

“Yes.”

She shut the door behind her. It was very quiet up there, at that hour: the wind, and the shuffle of feathers, and somewhere above them in the rafters an owl turning a mouse over and over in its beak. The last time she had stood in this room with him she had been thirteen, and the light had been the same, and his owl had been eating her scarf; and she had told him what Malfoys did instead of favours, and he had agreed to her terms.

She did not take out the letter. Not yet.

“I want to tell you something,” said Lyra, “and you are going to stand there and let me get to the end of it.”

He did not move.

“You knew about the diary,” she said. “The night the Chamber was closed. You knew what it was before anybody had told you anything, and you knew there were seven, and you knew about a house in the north that is in no register in this country. My father made sure of that. You said *somewhere up north* as if it were the weather.”

The owls shifted on their perches.

“You knew a hippogriff was going to go for somebody before it had so much as lifted a claw. You were on your knees beside me before anybody else had moved. You told me to feed a dog, on a train, before you had ever met it; and when I met it, it was starving, exactly as you said. You knew what Flint was going to do with that match before Flint had thought of it, and he only thought of it at lunch. You went out on Hallowe’en and came back and said *just missed him*, and I thought you meant you were unlucky. You weren’t unlucky. You were early. You knew what night he would come.”

“You stood on a pitch in November and looked at the gates and said *rotten weather, I’ll be quick*. And there were Dementors. You came and found me at the World Cup in the middle of the night, under a cloak, and told me to go back to my mother and stay with her, and an hour later there were Muggles in the air over the tents.” She took a breath. “You were *sure* about Moody. You told me so. You were so sure that you broke into his office with a wanted man, and you were wrong; and you still couldn’t tell me why you’d been sure. You knew about the beetle, when I’d told nobody, not one living soul. You knew Krum would ask Granger a month before Krum did. And you said a dead man was dead twice, and you have never in your life needed to say anything twice.”

He had gone very pale. He did not look away from her.

“And the Cup,” said Lyra.

She heard her own voice change on it, and could not stop it.

“In the tent, before the dragon. Bagman shouted it through the canvas, *one step nearer the Triwizard Cup*, and you turned your head. You had sat through every roar of that dragon without moving, and you turned your head at that, as if somebody had said your name. And on Saturday you came up out of that maze on to the podium crouched like a cat, with your wand up, looking round you for something that wasn’t there. And then you looked at the Cup in your hand as if it had lied to you.” She stopped. “You thought it was going to take you somewhere else.”

Potter said nothing.

“None of it surprised you,” said Lyra. “Not one thing, in four years. Not Black, not the Dementors, not dragons, not the Goblet. The only thing that has ever surprised you is me.”

The wind came in at the window and lifted the straw round his feet.

“You’ve done this before,” said Lyra.

It was not a question. She had meant it to be one. It was flat, and final, and stayed in the air between them, and nobody in that room said anything for a long time.

He did not deny it.

“Yes,” said Potter.

He said it to the floor first, and then he lifted his head and said it to her.

“Yes. I have.”

Lyra stood by the door. She had known. She had known since Saturday night on a trampled lawn, and before that, if she was honest, since a beetle and a word; and it made no difference to hear it. It went into her like cold water all the same.

“Not here,” said Potter. “Not this—it wasn’t this. Somewhere very like it.” He took his hands out of his pockets, and looked at them, as though he were not sure what to do with them, and put them back. “I don’t know how to say it so it doesn’t sound mad.”

“Try.”

He tried.

He told it badly, as he had told her about the trunk at Christmas. There had been another life. He had lived it. It had gone on a long way past where they were now, years past it; he did not say how far. And then he had been eleven, in a corridor, in the winter of their first year, with his scar splitting open and Quirrell walking past with a turban on, and he had remembered it. Not all at once. In pieces, like a dream you go on remembering all morning. That January he had been a boy who did not know what a Horcrux was; by Easter he had known nearly all of it.

“Everything,” said Lyra.

“Nearly everything. Not you.”

She waited.

“You weren’t there,” said Potter. “In the other one. There wasn’t any Lyra Malfoy. There was a Malfoy in our year, but it wasn’t you.” He looked at her steadily. “It was Draco.”

The owls had gone quiet.

“He was in Madam Malkin’s,” said Potter, “the day I got my robes. On a stool, being pinned. He talked at me the whole time about Quidditch and Houses and people who oughtn’t to be let in. He offered me his hand on the train and I said no. He was in Slytherin, and he hated me, and I hated him, for seven years. Half of what I knew happened to Draco.” He stopped. “I’d been looking at you since Madam Malkin’s. After the corridor I couldn’t stop. When it all came back, in that corridor, and I went down to breakfast the next morning knowing exactly who everybody was, and what they were going to do, and how most of them were going to die—and there was a girl sitting at the Slytherin table where Draco ought to have been, and I didn’t know one single thing about her.”

“You said once,” she managed at last, “that Draco hadn’t done a thing to anybody.”

“He hasn’t.” Potter’s voice was very quiet. “Not here. He’s ten, and he plays cards, and he writes you letters about Krum.”

“And in yours?”

“In mine,” said Potter, “he had.”

He did not say what. She did not ask. She stood by the door with her back against the wood and looked at him, at the boy who had been sitting in the same rooms as her for four years with seven of them already behind his eyes, and tried to make the two fit, and could not.

“How did it end?” she said. “Your other one.”

“Can’t.” His eyes went to the window, where the Forest was.

“*Can’t?*”

“Can’t.” He held her eyes. “That one I can’t. Not won’t. It isn’t— Lyra, I can’t. Not yet.”

It was the word he had used at Hallowe’en, she remembered, in an empty classroom, when she had asked him whether he had known about the Goblet. *Can’t.*

“Dumbledore knows,” she said.

“Since the end of second year. A year before the cave.” He rubbed his face. “He’s the only one. Not Sirius. Not Ron, not Hermione. Him, and now you.”

Him, and now you. He said it as though it were something he was giving her. She supposed it was.

She had meant to stop there. She had meant to take the letter out of her sleeve and say what she had come to say, and go. But there was a thing at the back of all of it that she had not looked at yet, and she was going to look at it whether she liked it or not.

“The diary,” said Lyra.

“If you knew everything. If you’d done it all before.” She heard her voice go very level, as her father’s did across a desk when somebody had made him a bad offer. “Then you knew about the diary before it ever got to Hogwarts. You knew my father had it. You knew he was going to put it in that little Weasley girl’s cauldron, in Flourish and Blotts, in front of everybody.”

“Yes.”

“And you let him.”

“Yes.”

“She was *eleven*.”

“I know.” It was barely a word. “I know she was.”

“Then why?”

He looked at her before he answered, and when he did, he did not try to make it sound better than it was.

“Because if I’d stopped it,” said Potter, “I’d have stopped knowing what came next. That’s all I had. Knowing. If I changed one big thing, everything after it would come out different, and I’d be as blind as everybody else, and I wasn’t— I didn’t dare. Not with him coming back.” He swallowed. “And I needed it. The diary. I needed it to happen the way it happened, so I could get it, and the fang. You can’t— there aren’t many things that will break one of those. That was one.”

Lyra said nothing for a moment.

She thought of a girl she had never once spoken to, with red hair and a second-hand cauldron, writing to a book every night for a year. She thought of an empty classroom on the night the Chamber was closed, and a boy putting her father’s diary down on a desk between them, and a word. *Hostage.* The whole of it. The pact, and the lake, and the Owlery, and the credit, every single thing between the two of them in four years, had grown up out of a thing he had watched happen to an eleven-year-old and decided to let be.

She had liked him for it. She had stood in that classroom with her heart going, and thought him the cleverest person she had ever met, and liked him for it.

“You said to me once,” said Lyra, “in a shack, with the sleet coming down outside, that you had never lied to me.”

He shut his eyes.

“*I’ve never lied to you, Malfoy*,” said Lyra. She said it exactly as he had, and she watched it land. “You hadn’t, either. Had you? Not once. Not one word. You only ever let me find out what you wanted me to find out, in the order you wanted me to find it, and you never had to lie at all.”

He did not answer.

And then, because it was there, and because she could see it now, as she could see everything now, she said the last of it.

“The egg.”

He opened his eyes.

“You knew,” said Lyra. “You knew what was in it before you ever opened it. You knew about the lake, and the merpeople, and the song. You sat in that library and read the page I gave you, and you let me—” Her voice did something, and she stopped it. “You let me solve it.”

“Yes.”

“*Why?*”

He looked at her across the straw, with the light behind him and the owls all round, and for the first time since she had come through the door he looked as though it hurt him to be looked at.

“I wanted you to have it,” said Potter.

She took the letter out of her sleeve.

She did not give it to him. She touched the blank, and the layers came apart, and she held it up so that he could see her father’s hand, very small, across the inside of the page; and she read him the first line of it, in a voice she did not recognise.

“*Our master has returned.*”

She saw it go into him, and saw that it was not news, and saw that it was worse than news, because he had been standing on a podium on Saturday night with his wand up, waiting for it, and it had happened somewhere else.

“He’s back,” said Lyra. “Since Saturday. My father says our family stands very high in his favour. Higher than he’d dared to hope.” She folded the letter again, along the same creases. “Thanks in no small measure to me. My errand in November was a piece of a great work, he says, and he knows whose hand carried it.”

“Lyra—”

“You’re going to let me finish,” said Lyra. “You owe me that, at the very least, and you know it.”

He stopped.

“My family is in it,” she said. “All the way in. They were in it before I ever went up that gangplank, and I carried it up there for them, and now they’re in his favour because of me. That’s the first thing.” She put the letter back into her sleeve. “And I lied to you. In the classroom, with the moon on the lake, when I’d made you swear. You kept your word and I lied to you, and I am not going to tell you what about. Not now. Probably not ever. That’s the second thing.”

He took a step towards her through the straw, and she held up one hand, and he stopped again.

“And the third thing is you.” Her voice was quite steady. She did not know how. “You had me on credit for four years, Potter, and you never once told me the terms. You knew what I’d be before I was it. You moved me about like furniture, from the first night, knowing exactly which room I would end up in, and the only reason I ever got anywhere you didn’t expect is that you didn’t know me. That was all I was to you, at the start. The one thing in the house you couldn’t place.”

“That isn’t—”

“Isn’t it?”

He did not finish.

“I can’t be with you,” said Lyra.

It was very simple, said aloud. She had thought it would be harder. Nothing in the room fell down.

She looked down at the ring on her finger. She did not take it off. Not here; not in front of him.

“They’ll want things from you,” said Potter. He said it fast and low, as though he had known for some time that he would have to say it and had worked out the words in advance. “Your father, and him. They’ll want you to tell them things. About me, about Dumbledore. Let them. I’ll make sure you’ve got enough to give them—enough that they keep you, enough that you’re useful—and nothing that costs you anything. Nothing that can come back on you. I can do that. I can keep you safe inside it, Lyra. You only have to—”

“Don’t insult me, Potter.”

He stopped.

“I have never in my life needed looking after,” said Lyra, “and I am not going to start with you. If I am going to be on the other side of this, then I shall be on the other side of it, all the way, as myself. I am not going to stand in my father’s house being handed scraps by you to feed to my own family, like a bird you’ve trained to come to the window. I’d rather be nothing at all.”

He looked at her.

Then something in his face gave way, very slightly, and he looked, for a moment, exactly fourteen.

“One more, then,” said Potter. “You don’t have to.”

She did not have to. She knew that. She stood by the door of the Owlery with her father’s letter in her sleeve and the ring warm on her finger, and looked at him in the straw, and for a moment did not move; and then she went across the floor to him, through the feathers and the light, and put her hands on his shoulders, and kissed him.

It was slow, and not careful. His hands came up and held her arms, very hard, and she felt him shaking, or herself, and could not tell which; and all round them the owls sat in their rows in the evening light and watched, and did not make a sound.

When it stopped, she did not step back at once.

“That was the last one,” said Lyra, against his collar.

“This year,” said Potter.
