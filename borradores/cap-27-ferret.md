# Chapter 27: Ferret

The answer came on a Thursday, by an owl so ordinary that nobody at the Slytherin table looked at it twice.

It was a school owl, brown and bored, the kind that carried half the castle’s post. It dropped a thin envelope beside Lyra’s plate, helped itself to a corner of her toast without asking, and left. The envelope said *Miss L. Malfoy* in a hand she had seen on the blackboard every week for a year and had never once seen on anything addressed to her.

She put it in her bag, unopened, and finished her breakfast. Goyle was explaining to the table how a dragon might be got into a school, for the first task, if it were very small, or asleep. Crabbe was watching the bag.

She had written to him ten days ago, on the first of November, on plain parchment with no crest. She had given it to a school owl with no address on it at all, because owls did not need one, and had spent the next week rather hoping it would get lost. It had taken her four drafts. The first had been three pages long. The fourth had said what had happened, in the order it had happened, and finished: *I do not expect this to settle anything. I thought you should know who carried it.*

She opened his in History of Magic, under the desk, while Professor Binns told the back row about the goblin rebellion of 1612 in a voice like a door that would never quite finish closing.

*Dear Miss Malfoy,*

*Thank you for your letter. I have read it several times, which I suspect you would rather I had not, and I am going to answer it honestly, since I think that is what you were asking for.*

*I knew by moonrise that something was wrong with the potion. I did not know that you had seen it. I will not pretend that it makes no difference to me that you did.*

*All the same, I have had the better part of a year to think about that night, and I keep arriving in the same place. You were thirteen, and in a hurry, and a man who had spent twelve years being overlooked was counting on somebody being in a hurry. That is not nothing. Neither is it the whole of it, and I think you know that, or you would not have written.*

*You will have heard by now why I left. It is true, and the decision was mine. Nobody was bitten, and that was luck, and I do not care to rely on luck where children are concerned. I did not leave because of you. Please do not add that to anything.*

*I had the chocolate, by the way. I wondered.*

*You need not write again. I should like it if you did.*

*Yours sincerely,*

*R. J. Lupin*

*P.S. I told you once that I was in your debt. I find that I still am. I imagine that will annoy you.*

Lyra read it twice. Binns had reached 1613.

She had sent that letter as a payment. She had known exactly what it was when she gave it to the owl: the last and largest instalment on a debt that had been sitting in her chest since February, and the most expensive thing she had ever had to write down. She had expected, at best, a receipt. At worst, nothing at all, which would have been fair.

Instead he had looked at what she owed him, and agreed with her about every penny of it, and then written underneath, in the same mild handwriting, that the account was still running the other way.

You could not settle with a man who insisted on owing you. It was the most underhand thing anybody had ever done to her, and she sat in the back of History of Magic with the letter flat under her hand, and found that her eyes had gone hot, and that she could not have said, if Binns had asked her, what century it was.

Crabbe, beside her, did not look round. She turned to a clean page and began, without a word, to take notes for both of them.

Professor Moody had pushed the desks back against the walls. That was the first thing anybody saw, coming into Defence that afternoon: a bare stretch of floor in the middle of the room, and Moody standing at the far end of it with his wand out, and the magical eye going round and round like a thing looking for a way out.

“Imperius Curse,” he said, when they were all inside and the door had shut itself. “I told you what it does. Now I’m going to show you what it feels like.”

Parkinson laughed, and then stopped, because nobody else had.

“The Ministry says you’re too young. Dumbledore says you need to know. I agree with Dumbledore.” The normal eye went along them. “Some of you’ll throw it off. Most of you won’t. The ones who can’t had better find out now, here, from somebody who’ll stop, than later from somebody who won’t. Parkinson. You were enjoying yourself. Out you come.”

Parkinson came out, a little less pleased with himself, into the middle of the floor. Moody raised his wand. “*Imperio*.”

A slow, stupid peacefulness spread over Parkinson’s face, as if somebody had wiped it clean. Then he crouched down, and put his hands on the floor between his feet, and began to hop across the room like a frog: four great solemn hops, with his cheeks puffed out, until he hit the wall.

Nobody laughed this time. Moody lifted the curse, and Parkinson came back to himself crouched against the skirting board, and looked up at them, and went the colour of a beetroot.

Crabbe sang. She was put under, and stood in the middle of the floor with her hands clasped in front of her, and sang the whole first verse of a Celestina Warbeck song in a clear, true, astonishingly sweet voice that nobody in Slytherin had ever heard before, and that Lyra suspected nobody would ever hear again. When it was over Crabbe came back to herself, and looked round at them, and her face did not change in the slightest. She walked back to her place beside Lyra as though she had been out of the room.

“Anyone laughs,” said Lyra, quite quietly, to the Slytherins nearest, “and I will remember it.”

Nobody laughed.

Goyle did a handstand and could not get down from it. The elder Warrington cousin tried to climb the bookcase. Millicent Bulstrode recited her own date of birth backwards eleven times. Moody went down the line with his wand out and his face like a chewed boot, and told each of them, as they came out of it, exactly how far gone they had been: “Didn’t even twitch. Next.” “You felt it, didn’t you, for half a second there? Didn’t do anything about it. Next.”

“Malfoy.”

Lyra walked out into the middle of the floor. She did not hurry and she did not drag her feet. She stood where Parkinson had stood, with her hands at her sides, and looked at the magical eye, since that was the one that was looking at her.

“*Imperio*.”

It was the loveliest feeling. That was what nobody had said. Everything she had been carrying went out of her at once, the letter in her bag and the badge in her pocket and the ring beside it, and a cup of fire, and her father’s travelling cloak with the mud on its hem; it all lifted off her and floated away somewhere she need not follow it, and left her light, and clean, and empty.

*Get up on the desk*, said a voice. It was a kind voice. It sounded as though it had her best interests at heart. *Get up on the desk.*

There was a desk against the wall on her left. It would be the easiest thing in the world to get up on it. Her knees were already deciding how.

*Why?* said another voice.

It was much smaller than the first one, and a good deal less kind, and it sounded very like her own, with something underneath it that sounded a little like her father’s. It did not want to know why in the sense of *is this wise*. It wanted to know what she would be getting for it.

*Get up on the desk.*

*And then what? What’s in it for me?*

Nothing, so far as she could see. Somebody wanted something from her, and was offering nothing in return, and was being very charming about it, which was what people always did when they were offering nothing in return. Malfoys did not do favours.

She stayed where she was.

*Get up—*

“No,” said Lyra aloud, and the lovely emptiness tore across like wet paper, and she was standing in the middle of a cold classroom with all of Slytherin looking at her, and her heart going very fast, and the letter and the badge and the ring and the cup of fire all back where they belonged, heavier than before.

Moody lowered his wand.

“Well, well,” he said.

The magical eye had stopped going round. It was looking at her, and so was the other one.

“Strong,” said Moody. “That’s very strong, for fourteen. Most grown wizards can’t do that, first time.” He tilted his head. “Who taught you that, Miss Malfoy? Your father? He’d know how it’s done. From the other end.”

There was a silence in the room that she could feel on the back of her neck.

“Nobody taught me, sir,” said Lyra.

“No,” said Moody. “I don’t suppose they did.” He jerked his head. “Go on. Next.”

She went back to her place. Crabbe moved up to make room for her, and did not look at her, and pressed her shoulder very briefly against Lyra’s, and took it away again.
