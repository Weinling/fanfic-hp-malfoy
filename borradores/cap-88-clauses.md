# Chapter 94: Clauses

She took stock in the usual order, beginning with the arm.

It lay outside the blanket on a pillow of its own, done up from the shoulder to the elbow in something stiff and white that smelt of dittany; and below that, where nobody had thought it worth the bandage, the thin grey line went down in its two slow curves to the wrist. The eye was open. It was the colour of weak tea, and it regarded the ceiling of the hospital wing as it had regarded everything else since the first of August, without interest.

So that was as before.

She had known it would be. She had felt it go; and then, a good while afterwards and from a long way down, she had felt it come back, like a lodger on the stairs. In between there had been about the length of a song. She knew now what it cost to buy that. That was worth knowing, and she laid it by, and went on.

Second, the light: thin and yellow, coming in low from the left, with a noise overhead of trunks being dragged. End of term. That made it Friday, the twentieth, and afternoon. At seven there would be a feast, with the Headmaster in the middle of the High Table in purple, pulling a cracker; and somewhere in this castle there was a pale wooden box with a card in the lid, and she did not have it. The last she had seen of that box, it was on the rim of a basin. For a hundred and forty-six days she had known where it was to the inch, and now she reached for the place in her head and it was like putting a foot down for a stair that has been taken away.

Third. She turned her head, which was a mistake.

It began under the left ear. It felt as though somebody had run a bootlace into her with a sail-needle and drawn the stitches up tight, over the collarbone and the point of the shoulder and out along the top of the arm; and it was the kind of seam that is put in by a very good tailor who has taken a dislike to the customer. She couldn’t see it. She had no need to. She had sat opposite the same hand’s work at her father’s table all summer, on a woman who told the story at dinner and did the voice.

She lay and looked at the ceiling until the room came back.

He had told her to move. She had that quite clear. He had screamed it at her, with the whole of his face, and the other thing had come out of him on the same breath; and she, who had been getting out of the way of things since she could walk, had stood exactly where she was and looked along his arm and thought: *oh. That.* She had been waiting since a landing in the November of last year to see where that arm would go when it went. To have it over had very nearly been a relief.

She was not going to think about that yet. Fourth, then.

Somebody had laid her things out on the locker in a row, as they are laid out for the relatives. Her wand. A hairpin, bent. The ring on its ribbon, gone green and looking pleased with itself. A folded scrap of paper that was nobody’s business, a Droobles wrapper, and a phial about the size of a tooth, with wire round the cork, in which twelve hours of the most tremendous luck had come through a cutting curse and a journey in Professor Snape’s arms without so much as a crack. She looked at that for some time. It had a sense of humour, evidently.

Beside these lay a pair of grey woollen gloves. They had been washed, and dried too near a stove, and had come out the size for a child of ten, with one thumb still a good deal longer than the other.

And there was a tray.

A bowl, with a plate over the top to keep the heat in, and a spoon, and two fingers of bread and butter. She regarded it without appetite. Nobody could have found fault with it. Then she saw what was the matter with it, which was that it was all there was. She looked at the locker again, behind the gloves and along the edge; and, God help her, she put out her right hand and felt on the far side of the water jug.

Nobody had left anything in greaseproof paper.

Nobody was going to. That had been explained to her on Sunday, at some length, on a dormitory floor. She had sat at dinner afterwards and said *Poor Goyle*, and stood for an hour in a corridor in those gloves; she had known it for five days, as one knows a date. She had not, it appeared, known it at all. A hospital tray had had to tell her: a bowl of soup, and nobody on earth who minded whether she ate it. *Somebody’s got to*, a slow thick voice had said, in the dark. *You won’t.*

It came up from under the bandage. She couldn’t turn over and she couldn’t get a hand to her face in time, and she lay flat on her back in a public ward with her mouth square, making the noise of a small child who has been left behind in a shop. Every breath pulled at the stitches. That was something.

“There, now,” said Madam Pomfrey.

She had come round the end of the bed with a cloth. She didn’t pat anything. She sat down on the edge of the next mattress, with her cap crooked and her hands on her knees, and waited, as one waits for a kettle; and when the worst had gone by she leant over and wiped Lyra’s nose, firmly, as if it had been an instrument.

“You’ve lost the better part of three pints,” she said. “I’ve put some of it back, and it wants feeding. You’ll cry at the wallpaper until Sunday; they all do. You’ll eat that soup.”

“I’m not hungry.”

“I didn’t ask.” She glanced at the tray, and then, for a moment, at Lyra, and her sharp tired eyes did something that would have been kindness in a woman with more time. “I had her in here in October with a burnt hand. She’d been trying to get a sugar to go a particular colour. She wanted to know whether I thought you would fancy a custard.” She got up. “I’ve nobody to send from the kitchens to stand over you, Miss Malfoy. I’m afraid you will have to see to it yourself.”

She did not see to it. She got the plate off. Her right hand was her own, and on Thursday it had been steady enough to hold a wand in a boy’s face; it now turned out that it could not carry a spoonful of barley broth eight inches without leaving most of it on the sheet. She tried four times. Then she laid the spoon down, very straight, and lay and listened to the trunks.

Professor McGonagall came at four, and did not sit.

She stood at the foot of the bed in the green tartan, with her hands folded, and looked the bandage over from one end to the other before she looked at anything else; and Lyra, who had been inspected by her own mother at intervals since birth, knew when she was being read for damage and not for fault.

“I shan’t keep you. I have had Potter’s account of yesterday. I’ve had the ghost’s, which was longer. I should like yours.”

It lay on its pillow with its eye on the ceiling. There were perhaps six true things that could be said in this room, and of the six there was one that would do for everybody: for the woman at the foot of the bed, and for a boy, and for a person in Wiltshire who might at this moment be laying down his book.

“I raised my wand first,” said Lyra.

“He tells me that you had lowered it.”

“Then he is being gallant. It doesn’t suit him.” Her voice was working: the pleasant one, rather thin. “He said a thing I didn’t care to hear, Professor. I had my wand in his face before he’d got to the end of it, and I had begun.”

“Begun what?”

“I didn’t finish it.”

“That is not what I asked you.”

“No,” said Lyra. “It’s what I am able to answer.”

Professor McGonagall’s nostrils went thin. She had the look of someone who has been handed the same parcel twice in two days, by different people, and has again been asked not to open it.

“Potter gave me the whole of his in four syllables,” she said.

“Potter has never had to think what a word would cost him.”

“He is finding out. He is off his House team for good, and he’ll be spending his evenings under my eye until one of us is dead. I thought you would wish to know that something had been done.”

“Thank you, Professor.”

“You don’t think it enough.”

“I don’t think it has anything to do with me,” said Lyra; and that, too, was perfectly true.

The spectacles rested on her for some little time. Then Professor McGonagall asked, in an altered tone, whether there was anything that she wanted; and Lyra said that she would like to see him.

“Whatever for?”

“There’s something I want from him.”

“Madam Pomfrey will say no.”

“Then I should be very much obliged, Professor, if you would say yes.”

It hung there. A long way below them somebody dropped a trunk on the marble, and a prefect began to shout.

“Ten minutes,” said Professor McGonagall. “After nine. That door stays open, and Poppy will be on the other side of it with her knitting. And Miss Malfoy.” She had turned to go. She stopped with one hand on the bed-rail, and did not look round. “Whatever it was that you began. I have seen what he finished. I should like you to understand that I do not regard the two as a pair.”

At seven the feast began. She heard it come up through two floors: four hundred benches, the roar that greets a pudding, and somebody’s cracker going off like a wand. She lay and counted the quarters by the clock over the door. He would have had his soup by now. He would be on the turkey. At some point a small pale box ought to have been walking up the length of that Hall in a pair of steady hands, and it wasn’t, and nothing whatever happened in consequence. Her arm did not burn. Nobody called. This was the first Friday since the summer on which she had had nothing to carry, and she had never in her life been so frightened of a quiet evening.

At ten past nine he came to the door and stopped on the sill.

He had been told about the sill, plainly. He stood with his toes on the join in the boards and his hands in his pockets, and he looked as he had looked on a hill in Wiltshire at four in the morning, only longer in the face. He had washed, though not altogether out of the cuffs. He looked at the bandage and then, because he had always been honest in his way, he made himself look at the rest: the white thing on its pillow, and the grey line below it, and her face.

“You’re letting in a draught, Potter,” said Lyra. “Either come in or go.”

He came in. He got as far as the end of the bed.

“And take your hands out of your pockets. That’s over. I ended it myself; you were there.” She could hear how it sounded: clear, carrying, a little tired, a girl speaking to somebody she had on a string. It would do very well, and was even true. “You look like a bad debt.”

He took them out. He did not seem to know where they went instead. His eyes had gone to the locker, to the bowl with its skin on, and the spoon laid straight, and the four brown splashes on the turn of the sheet; and she saw him read that as he read a Quidditch pitch, in one look.

“Can I?” said Harry.

She had not expected to be asked. Nearly a year had gone by since she told him, on a chalk ridge, that nothing moved without it; she had not supposed that the lesson would be learnt on soup.

She said nothing. He seemed to take that for what it was. He came up the side of the bed and sat down on the very edge of the chair, and picked up the bowl, and got hold of the spoon in his fist as one holds a quill for lines.

“Not like that. You’ll have it down my neck. Underneath, with your thumb on top, and you bring it in from the side.” He changed his grip. “From the *side*, Potter. One doesn’t come at a person from the front. They can see it coming, and they shut their mouths.”

“How d’you know?”

He brought it in from the side, cold barley broth with a skin on it, with his tongue between his teeth and his other hand cupped underneath as though he were carrying an egg across a river; and she opened her mouth, because it had got there, and there was nothing else to be done with it.

She had not been fed since she was three. She had not known what it was. One lay there. One could do nothing whatever to hurry it or to stop it, and a face came near, enormous and intent, minding very much about a spoon; and one took what was put in, and swallowed, and waited to see whether there would be more. She had stood at the other end of this. She had done it eleven times running in a long bright ward, to a man with nobody behind his eyes, while his sister said how nicely he took it from the young ladies.

He had no idea what he was doing. That was nearly the worst of it. He got in a second, and a third, and wiped her chin with the edge of his sleeve, which was not clean.

“I’ll tell you how I know,” said Lyra.

She said it to the ceiling. It could hear this. It had heard it already, in its own house, with approval; there was nothing in it that would cost her mother a farthing.

“Broderick Bode. You’ll remember the name. He had the Kiss in his sister’s kitchen in Battersea, in March, and it was on page nine, and you sat on a rug on the seventeenth with the paper on your knee and said that you ought to have remembered him. You’d known something once, and it had gone out of your head, and a man was sitting in a ward with a bib on because of it. You have carried that about for nine months. I’ve watched you.” He had stopped, with the spoon in the air. “I had his name out of Umbridge on the second. I wrote it to my father on the fifth. Goyle wouldn’t take the letter up, so I took it myself. And when you said it was your fault I told you there was nothing you could have done, which was accurate, and I let you keep it.”

Nothing moved in the ward. On the far side of the open door, a pair of needles clicked, and paused, and went on.

“At Easter I went to see him. One does; there’s a Fund. They give one a bowl. I got eleven spoonfuls into him, and his sister told me that he would have liked me.” Her voice had begun to go, at the edges. She let it. “She knitted me those. She thought I had cold hands.”

His eyes went to the gloves and stayed on them. She waited for him to say something that she would have to forgive him for, and what he did, in the end, was to bring the spoon the rest of the way, from the side, with his thumb on top.

“Open,” said Harry.

It was not kind. She would have known what to do with kind. She opened her mouth, and the tears ran back into her ears, and he fed her the whole of the bowl without another word, and scraped it.

When that was done he put his hand inside his robes and brought out a small black notebook and the stub of a pencil. He tore out a leaf. He wrote on his knee, with his arm crooked round it like a second-year in a test, and he held the paper where she could read it, and neither of them said a word.

*Dumbledore has the box. Your brother is alright he’s with Nevilles lot.*

She read it twice, for the pleasure of the first four words. Then she held out her hand for the pencil. *All right* was two words, and wanted another *l*; she saw to both. She put a full stop after it, and a capital, and gave *Neville* his apostrophe. Underneath she wrote one word of her own.

*Count?*

*23*, wrote Harry.

While he wrote it she saw the back of the leaf, which he had tilted towards her: ruled, and covered to the margins in a cramped upright pencil that she would have known in the dark.

*Oct. 5. Ate half a roll. Oct. 6. Sunday. Out at 2.40. Not at the Owlery. Back at 3.50, washed her face.*

She read it down to the bottom. He saw her doing it, too late, and his ears went a dull red, and he did not take it away. A book of that size has forty leaves. She had kept one on him for five years, with columns, and shown it to him on a rug, and he had told her that it was the nicest thing anybody had ever done. She had never once put down what he ate.

She did not know what one called this. She didn’t try. She looked at it, as she had looked at a wall with a rat by the skirting, to be sure of the number of its toes.

“Your sevens are a disgrace,” said Lyra.

He burnt it at the night-light. He held the corner until it had got to his fingers.

“I want to make a Vow,” said Harry.

He had got up. He was standing at the end of the bed again, in the proper place for it, with his hands hanging. He had clearly been saying this to himself on staircases since yesterday.

“An Unbreakable one. Lupin would bind it; I’d only have to ask. I’ve worked it out.” He was speaking rather fast. “I don’t know what that was. Yesterday. I do know, I’m not saying I don’t, it was me. I told McGonagall it was me. But I can’t tell you it won’t— I said that to you in June. You asked me and I said I didn’t know, and I still don’t, and I’m not going to stand here and look at that and tell you I’m sorry, as if—” He stopped, and got his breath. “So I want to swear it. So it’s not up to me.”

“Write it down,” said Lyra.

“What?”

“One writes it down. On one’s own paper, in one’s own hand. I’m not going to be sworn at in a hospital, Potter. Let me see the terms.”

He tore out another leaf. This time he looked at the back of it first. It took him a minute and a half, and he crossed something out; and what he gave her, when it was done, went as follows.

*1. I swear I will never touch a wand again.*

*2. If I do I die.*

*H. Potter.*

She held it in her right hand, at the proper distance, and read it as she had been taught to read anything that had a signature on it: once for the sense, and once for what had been left out.

“It’s a very bad contract,” she said.

“It’s not a—”

“It has a party, and a penalty, and somebody is meant to hold it. That’s a contract. Be quiet. *Never touch a wand.* Whose? Any? You couldn’t get down from Gryffindor Tower; there are two doors on that staircase which don’t open without one. You’d be dead on Monday morning of wanting your breakfast.” She laid her thumb on the first line. “But say you were careful. Say you lived like a Squib, in a bed-sitting-room, and had your shopping sent. One day you’d be in a street and something would happen to somebody. It does, round you. It’s a kind of gift. And you would be the nearest, because you always are, and there’d be a wand lying in the road; and you would pick it up. You wouldn’t so much as stop to think about it. That’s the first thing the matter with this. It kills you the first time anybody needs you.”

He had opened his mouth. She went on.

“Second. A Vow is sworn *to* someone. You’ve left that out, which I take to be delicacy; I’m to suppose it’s me. Then it would be mine. I should be the one holding the other end. Every morning for the rest of my life I’d come down and look along a table to see whether you were still breathing, and if you weren’t, that would be on my book.” It had gone thin again. She took a moment over it. “I have rather a lot on my book just now. I don’t think I’ve room.”

“Lyra—”

“And third.” She turned the leaf round, and held it out to him, so that he could see his own hand. “Show me my side of it. Go on. What do I get? I don’t get my arm back; Madam Pomfrey has been very clear. I don’t get June. I get you, in a room somewhere, never doing anything again, and feeling a good deal better about it. You’d sleep. That’s what this is for. You are frightened of yourself, and you would like to hand yourself to me to mind, and to have my name on the bottom as the reason.” She could not keep it level any longer, and she didn’t try. “I won’t. I won’t have you on those terms. I’ve been put in a safe place by somebody who couldn’t trust himself, Potter. I lay in it for five hours. I am not going to do it to you, and you’re not going to make me.”

She let go of the paper. It went down on to the blanket, between them, face up.

He didn’t pick it up. He stood and looked at it, at the two lines and his name; and she watched it reach him, slowly, that he had come in here with the largest thing he owned and had it handed back as a convenience. He went white, and then he went red. He had never been able to hide anything below the collar.

“Then what?” said Harry. It was hardly a voice. “What d’you want? There’s got to be— I can’t just *walk about*.”

“Yes, you can. I’m going to. I shall wear what I like, whatever she says, and people will look at it, and you’ll be one of them.” She lifted her chin by as much as the stitches would allow. “That’s the arrangement, as regards the past. As regards the future, I want something I can use.”

“What?”

“Teach me the song.”

He stared at her.

“The one you sang. I heard it. I wasn’t in a state to attend, but one does notice when somebody is making a noise like a cat in a gate over one’s arm. It shut for an inch and came open again. Then Snape did it, and it stayed.” She held his eyes. “You can’t tell me that you won’t open anything again. Very well. I’m not asking. I want to be able to close what you open. On me; on anyone. I want it in my own hands, where I can get at it, and I don’t propose to wait thirteen weeks.”

“It took me thirteen weeks.”

“Then I shall want six.”

It was out before she had looked at it: exactly what she would have said to him over a cauldron in the fourth year, with a mark at stake; and for one moment, standing at the end of a hospital bed with a contract lying on the blanket, he looked as he had looked then. The corner of his mouth went. He got it back at once. He had seen it as well as she had, and neither of them was fool enough to say so.

“You have to sing it,” said Harry. “Properly. You can’t stop to breathe.”

“I had gathered that.”

“I’ve heard you sing. First year, at the feast. You were flat.”

“I was eleven, and I was being drowned out by a Hufflepuff.” She shut her eyes. She was more tired than she had been since the summer, and there was soup in her, and the ward was going quietly round. “After Christmas. Bring something that can’t complain.”

The needles had stopped. A chair scraped, on the far side of the door.

He took up the leaf from the blanket. She heard him fold it. She had supposed he would burn that one too, and when she got her eyes open he was putting it away, inside his robes, on the left, where he kept the book.

He wrote one more. He did it standing, against his palm, and held it down by the pillow.

*I can keep D here for Christmas. Neville’s gran would have him. Say yes.*

He had got the apostrophe in. She noticed that before she noticed anything else, and then she read the last two words, and lay looking at them.

*Say yes.* He had asked. She would give him that. He had put it on paper and brought it to her and waited, with his hands where she could see them; and it was a good plan, and it was kindly meant, and a year ago she’d have wept with relief to be offered it. There was a hillside in it somewhere, and sheep. She could hear her brother’s voice at the bottom of a staircase, on the first of September, after the jump. *You take everything.*

She took the pencil. Her hand was a little better for the soup.

*Ask him.*

Her mother came for them at eleven the next morning, by the Floo in Professor Snape’s study, in the grey.

She walked up through the castle as if she had been asked to tea in it. She thanked Madam Pomfrey, in three sentences, of which the third was a cheque to the hospital wing; she looked at the bandage for exactly as long as Professor McGonagall had, and at Lyra’s face for rather less; and she said that she had brought the fur, since the bandage wouldn’t go into a sleeve. Her eyes had gone once to the pillow, and nothing else needed saying.

Lyra got down the marble staircase on her own feet. It took some time. She had her mother’s arm on the right, and on the left the cloak hung empty from the shoulder, with a sling under it, and the collar turned up to the ear. The Entrance Hall was full of trunks and owls and people who stopped talking. She heard it go round. *That’s her. He did. Myrtle said.* She kept her chin where the stitches liked it, and went on going down.

They were at the bottom, by the hourglasses.

Draco was sitting on his trunk, with his feet together and his hands on his knees. He had his travelling cloak on, and his gloves, and his hair had been wetted and combed by somebody who had sisters. Longbottom stood beside him, holding a plant. And a little way off, against the wall, with his hands at his sides and nothing in them, stood Harry.

She knew, then, before anybody had opened his mouth. He had asked. One could see that he had asked; it was all over him, like rain.

“I’m coming home,” said Draco.

He said it to their mother. He had got down off the trunk. He stood very straight, in the middle of the flagstones, at twelve, with his chin up, and his voice came out high and careful and perfectly clear.

“I was asked to stay. I should like it to be known that I was asked, properly, and I said no, thank you. You’d be by yourself. She can’t do her own buttons.” He did not look at Lyra. “I counted. There’s only me.”

Their mother did not say anything at all. She put out her hand, flat, and laid it on the top of his head; and for the first time since Lyra could remember, he stepped out from under it.

He turned round. He went across the Hall, between the trunks, to the place by the wall, and stopped a yard off, and looked up.

“You said you’d fix it,” said Draco.

Harry did not go on one knee. He stood there with his arms hanging, and considered a small boy in a travelling cloak, and then, once, past him, a girl on the bottom stair with an empty sleeve.

“I can’t fix that,” said Harry.

He said it quite quietly. Lyra had known him for five years and four months, in which time he had told her that he could get round a dragon, and find a house that wasn’t on any map, and keep her safe inside a war; she had never before heard him say the other thing, and she saw what it cost him, to the Knut, and that he’d paid it.

Draco nodded. He seemed to have expected about that much. He put his hand in his pocket, and felt for something there, and left it where it was.

Then he found that his bow tie had come undone.

It had slipped in the business with the trunk, and one end was hanging. He looked down his nose at it. Lyra had taken a step, from habit, before she remembered that she had one hand; and their mother’s gloves had come up, and her wrists had turned over, ready.

“I can do it,” said Draco.

Everybody waited. The whole of the Entrance Hall seemed to be waiting. He pulled off his gloves and gave them to Longbottom to hold. He turned up his own collar. He crossed the ends, with his tongue showing, and brought the long one up and under, and lost it, and found it again; he made the bow, and it came out with one wing a good deal larger than the other; and he pulled it tight, and turned his collar down, and gave the whole thing a small firm tweak, to the left.

It finished under his ear.

Nobody touched it. Their mother’s hands went down, slowly, and folded.

“You have my cousin’s way of standing,” she said, to the air beside the wall, as she drew her glove straight. “He never knew when to go, either.”

She took Lyra’s arm. Draco picked up the handle of his trunk, with both hands, and went ahead of them towards the doors.
