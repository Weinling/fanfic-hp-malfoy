# Chapter 14: Hands Off

Lyra’s wand was halfway out of her sleeve.

She knew exactly how much good it would do. Sirius Black had killed twelve Muggles and a wizard in a crowded street with a single curse, and Lyra had a wand, thirteen years and the best marks in her year in Charms, and none of those had ever been asked to stand between her and a man like that. The sensible thing was to run. She would have, very probably, if running had not meant turning her back on him, and if Potter had not been standing a yard from a murderer with his hands in his pockets, smiling as if he had introduced her to somebody’s aunt.

She reached for him. She did not know what she meant to do: pull him back, or get between them, or simply hold on to something. “Potter, get *away* from—”

“Steady, Malfoy.”

He caught her hand before it reached his sleeve, quite gently, and held it between both of his.

“Whatever they’ve told you about him,” he said, “it isn’t true. He’s not a murderer. He never was.”

She had every reason not to believe him. Black’s face had been on the front of the *Prophet* since July. Her father had discussed him over dinner and Professor Snape in his office, and between them they had not found a kind word. Against all that there was Potter, and his word, which she had never in her life taken for anything.

It was not her mind that was the trouble. Potter was only holding her hand. His were warm and dry, with ink on one thumb, and while he held hers she could not make it shake.

She took her hand back, and glared at Black instead.

“Right,” said Potter. “Inside, I think. It’s starting to rain.”

It was. A thin, freezing rain had come in off the hills while nobody was looking, and the snow on the brambles was turning to slush. Potter went to the back door of the Shack, which was boarded over like everything else, said something to the boards, and they swung outward like a gate. Lyra went in sideways, so as never to have Black behind her, and Black came in last and did the same.

Inside, it was worse than the outside had promised. Every stick of furniture in the place had been broken, and not by ghosts: the legs of the chairs were scored with long parallel gouges, as if something with claws had taken against them. The wallpaper hung in strips. In one corner the roof let the rain through in a thin, steady trickle onto the boards. Potter set an upturned chair on its feet for Black, and dragged a crate across for Lyra, and put the whole width of the room between them. It was the first sensible thing he had done all day, and neither of them thanked him.

Black spoke first. His voice was hoarse and rusty, as though it had not been used for a long time.

“Harry. Do you know whose girl that is? Her father would have licked Voldemort’s boots clean if he’d been asked nicely. And you’ve just shown her me.”

Lyra’s shoulders went up at the name. Malfoys did not say it, and were not, as a rule, obliged to hear it said across a room.

“If I’d known it was Sirius Black under all that fur,” she said, “I’d have eaten the chicken myself, in the lane, with my fingers!”

“And if I’d known who was buying,” said Black, “I’d have gone hungry.” His eyes narrowed. “Narcissa’s girl. I should have seen it. You’ve got her nose.”

He was her mother’s cousin. Lyra had always known it, the way one knew the names on a family tree, and had never once thought of it as anything but ink. It sat differently now that the fact was looking at her. He was Regulus’s brother, too. She knew two things about Regulus Black that this man, very probably, did not.

“A real dog would have been sick on that chocolate,” she said coldly. “I looked it up. You didn’t so much as hiccup.”

“Twelve years of Azkaban food,” said Black. “You’ll have to do better than Honeydukes.”

They looked at each other across the room like two cats on one wall. On the edge of the window seat, Potter’s eyebrows went up.

“Hang on. Chocolate? When was this?”

This was precisely the conversation she had gone to some trouble not to have.

“The Monday after the match,” said Lyra. “When you were in the hospital wing. I went down to the lake and the creature was simply there—”

“You don’t eat dark chocolate. You pulled a face at Lupin’s on the train like it was medicine.” Potter was looking at her with growing interest. “So you had a bar of something you won’t eat, the day I was in the hospital wing, and you were out by the lake, which is nowhere near the hospital wing, and then a dog got it.” He snapped his fingers. “Aha. It was for me.”

“Don’t flatter yourself!”

“It wasn’t?”

“It was— the dog’s *now*.” Lyra could feel her ears going hot, and there was nothing whatever to be done about ears. “I had a spare bar. I got as far as the door and heard Wood explaining Quidditch to a man with concussion, and I thought better of it. The dog was more grateful.”

“Pity,” said Potter, and sounded as if he meant it. “Well. As long as it went to family.”

Black was staring at him. His mouth had come slightly open, and he was looking at Potter as if Potter had just done something Black had seen somebody do a very long time ago, and had not expected to see again.

“So.” Potter turned to her as though nothing had happened. “You came all this way to tell me about Sirius.”

Lyra looked at Black, and at the leak in the corner, and back at Potter.

“I’ve changed my mind.”

“Go on, though. What have you got?”

“That he was your parents’…” Saying it in front of the man himself seemed suddenly indelicate, which was ridiculous. “Secret Keeper.”

“Hm.” Potter tilted his head. “Somebody on the staff, then. Nobody else would know. Snape?”

Lyra looked back at him with polite interest. Underneath the polite interest something went cold, because Professor Snape had told her that in a room with a charm on the door, and Professor Snape could write to her father whenever he pleased. Potter only nodded, as if she had answered, and let it go. With an Animagus sitting on a broken chair across the room, where she had got the rest was hardly the point.

“Then you know about the Fidelius,” said Potter. “Good. Saves time. Dad did pick Sirius.”

“And Sirius sold him.”

“Don’t you *dare*—” Black was half out of his chair, and his voice cracked down the middle like a dropped plate. “I never sold *anybody*!”

“Sirius.” Potter did not raise his voice, and did not seem to need to. “Let me tell her. Sit for a minute.” He waited until Black sat, slowly, and then turned back to Lyra. “Dad picked him, and Sirius said no.”

“What?”

“Think about it. Everyone knew they were best friends. If the Potters had a Secret Keeper, it’d be Sirius; you wouldn’t need to be Voldemort to work that out. So Sirius told them to let him be the one everybody went after, and give the real secret to someone nobody would look at twice.”

It was a good plan. Lyra had been raised on her father’s notion of a good plan, and she could find nothing wrong with this one: put the obvious man in the window, and the valuable thing somewhere dull.

“Dumbledore and Sirius picked the real one between them,” Potter went on. “And almost straight away he lost his nerve. He said Voldemort would go after Sirius, and Sirius would hold out for a bit, and then he wouldn’t, and then they’d know who to go for next.”

“Because the charm hides the house,” said Lyra slowly. “Not the person keeping it.”

Potter looked at her. “Yes. Exactly that. So Dumbledore and Sirius tied their own tongues. A binding, so neither of them could ever give him away, not if they were tortured, not if they wanted to.” His voice did not change. “All that, to keep him safe. And he was the one who went and told.”

Lyra was no longer quite listening. She was back in a dungeon office with a charm on the door, and Professor Snape’s voice was going on, dry as paper: a week, Black had kept their secret one week. And afterwards a street, and a wizard who had gone after Black shouting and been blown apart for it, and who was buried alongside twelve Muggles in every version of the story she had ever heard. The only other friend. The one nobody would think of.

“Pettigrew,” she said.

Potter nodded.

“But he’s dead.”

“Is he? Somebody blew up a street, and they found one of his fingers, and Sirius was standing in the middle of it and couldn’t say who’d really done it. If you wanted to die without the bother of dying, I can’t think of a better way.”

“He opened his mouth just now,” said Lyra, with a nod at Black. “Quite loudly.”

“He can say he isn’t the traitor. He’s been saying it for twelve years, and nobody listens. What he can’t do is point. Not a name, not a hint, nothing that would lead you to the one who was.”

Lyra turned on her crate to face Black.

“Who was your Secret Keeper, Mr Black?”

Black opened his mouth. Nothing came out of it. His face emptied as she watched, every line in it going smooth and blank, until he sat there like a man painted on a wall, and only his eyes were left, red-rimmed and fixed on hers, with everything behind them that he was not allowed to say.

Lyra looked away first.

“Then why didn’t Dumbledore *say* something? Before they took him to Azkaban? He’s Dumbledore. He could have stood up in front of the whole Wizengamot and—”

“Same binding. He couldn’t.”

“But he told *you*!”

“He found a way. It took him years.” Potter shrugged. “I was the one thing the charm was hiding, you see. Whatever was inside it, I was inside it too, so I was the only person he was allowed to tell. Even then it was more like a riddle than telling.”

Years, to find a way round, to tell a boy. And in twelve years no way round, long or short, to get an innocent man out of Azkaban. Lyra did not say so. She put it away somewhere safe, with the other things she knew about Albus Dumbledore, for later.

There was something else to put with it. *Dumbledore told me.* There it was at last, the answer she had not asked for in the library, handed over for nothing. It accounted for the dog, and the charm, and Pettigrew. It did not account for a house in Northumberland that was on no map and in no register, that her father had never named to a living soul, and that Potter had brought up in May without so much as blinking. Dumbledore might know a great many things. She did not see how he could know that.

She kept that too.

“Very well,” said Lyra. “You win.”

“I wasn’t playing.”

He meant it kindly, which was worse. She felt the heat start at the back of her neck and climb, and there was no stopping it. Meanwhile the whole term was rearranging itself behind her eyes, piece by piece, into a shape she did not care for: a boy walking out of the castle with no form; *Just missed him*, on Hallowe’en; a dog he had told her about on the train, that would be hungry, which she had taken for a joke.

“Hogsmeade,” she said. “On Hallowe’en. That was him. You went looking for him.”

“Mm. Can’t exactly have him round for tea in Gryffindor Tower.”

“And the dog. On the train.”

“Dumbledore again. He knew what Sirius turned into.”

“And I stood in that corridor and *told* you not to go.” It came out smaller than she had meant it to. “You must have thought it was the funniest thing you’d ever heard.”

Potter shook his head.

“I didn’t laugh. I thought it was nice. That you worried.”

“That is what people say.”

“I’ve never lied to you, Malfoy.”

She could think of one lie at least, to do with the preface of *The Monster Book of Monsters*, and found that she did not want to bring it up.

“So,” she said. “What next? Where do we start?”

Potter smiled at her. It was a kind smile, patient and fond: the one you might give a younger brother who had asked whether he could come too.

“About that,” he said. “Hands off, Malfoy.”

“…What?”

“I only told you about Sirius because you’d have got there anyway, the hard way, and somebody would have noticed you getting there. That’s all it was for. You’ve got your answer. You can go home now.”

He was showing her out.

She had not seen it coming. She had got this close to it, closer than anybody, and he was shutting the door with her foot still in it. Her voice did something she did not give it permission to do.

“You— you bring me all the way out here and tell me *everything*, and now I’m to go back to the castle and pretend I don’t know it?”

“It isn’t everything. It isn’t even much.” Potter spread his hands. “Some bloke broke out of Azkaban and he can turn into a dog. That’s all you’ve got. It’s nothing next to the diary.”

*The diary.* He said it as easily as he had said *Hogsmeade*, and the floor of the Shack seemed to go a little further away beneath her.

“Dumbledore will hide him now we’ve found him. Nobody finds a thing Dumbledore’s hidden. Sirius is safe.”

“And what about me?”

“You stop here. Whatever comes next, it doesn’t need you in it. You’re safer out of it, and so am I.”

“But I *am* in it. I’ve been in it since May. I—”

*Tell me*, she wanted to say. *Don’t send me back with nothing.* She had never in her life asked anybody for anything in that voice, and she heard herself doing it, and did not stop. Her pride was the only thing she had ever really owned, and she had just put it down on the floor between them.

“I was hoping I wouldn’t have to say this,” said Potter.

When it came, it was harder than she would have believed he could be.

“It isn’t just you who gets hurt if you stay. It’s your family. And I know what you think of half of them, but Draco hasn’t done a thing to anybody. Would you be all right if something happened to Draco?”

On the broken chair, Black had turned his head and was staring at his godson. Whatever he thought of it, he kept it to himself; but it was not the way he had looked at Potter before.

Draco. She had never once said her brother’s name to him. Draco, who wrote her letters that ran down both sides of the page about nothing at all, and was learning to play cards from an old man in the east cottage, and had not answered her in seven weeks.

It was a real threat. That was what she could not get past. It was not a bluff and it was not a game. In May he had made her his hostage, and she had gone along with it, because it had been only her. It had not crossed her mind until this moment that a hostage might have a brother. He would never touch Draco himself; she did not think that of him. He would not need to. The diary had sat quietly in his keeping since May, and he had only to stop keeping it, to let it go where it could do the most harm, the Ministry or the *Prophet*, and the house would come down on her parents with Draco standing underneath.

“Do you mean it?” She asked anyway. She wanted him to say he hadn’t.

“Yes,” said Potter. His voice was perfectly steady, and quite cold. “You’re not in this, Malfoy.”

Lyra Malfoy did not cry. Tears were a white flag and a begging bowl at the same time, her father said, and she had been brought up to carry neither. She had cried in front of two Houses in September, but that had been a hippogriff and a great deal of blood, and did not count. So the wet on her face was rain. The roof leaked. It had been leaking all afternoon, in the corner, and it must have found her somehow.

“From now on,” she said.

She wanted to hurt him. She wanted to give him something he would carry for the rest of his life, and he already had one of those, on his forehead, where anyone could see it, and she could not think of anything that would show up next to it. Every cruel thing she knew had been used on somebody else first. So in the end she said the only true thing she had.

“Don’t smile in front of me.”

It worked. She saw it work: the smile went out of him, the kind, patient one, and for a second what was left underneath looked about eleven years old. It was no comfort at all.

She tore the button warmer off her collar and threw it. It struck the boards and bounced, and went rolling off across the room in a long, unsteady curve, and tipped through a gap between two floorboards over by the wall, into the dark underneath. She did not wait to hear it land.

She turned round and walked out of the Shrieking Shack.

Outside, it really was raining.
