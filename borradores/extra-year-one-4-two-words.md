# Year One, Part 4: Two Words

Severus Snape did not sit down.

The Headmaster had pointed him to a chair with the air of a man who had been expecting him for an hour and had kept the tea warm, and Snape walked past the chair and the tea and stopped in front of the desk.

“What,” he said, “did you tell the boy?”

“That you owe James and Lily Potter a debt you can never repay,” said Dumbledore, “and that until you have paid it, you will keep their son alive.” He folded his hands. “I thought it was time somebody told him something true, Severus. He came here, you know. All the way up the stairs, on his own, to complain about you.”

“He is eleven. He complains about everything.”

“He looked me in the eye and told me that you are unfair. Not very elegantly. But I could not find anything in it to correct.”

Snape had nothing to say to that. He knew what he did in his dungeon, and why, and the reasons he gave for it, which were not quite the same thing.

“And the rest?” he said at last. “Did you tell him the rest?”

“No.” Dumbledore regarded him over his spectacles. “If Harry knows the rest, he did not learn it from me.”

There was a silence. One of the silver instruments on the shelf behind the desk let out a thin puff of smoke and was still.

“You may be as disagreeable to the children as you please,” said Dumbledore. “That is your affair, and I have long since stopped hoping otherwise. But it is not to cost us the Stone. Watch it as you have been watching it.”

“I have not been careless.”

“The boy hates you, Severus. He will get in your way at every turn, and he is a wizard, however small. I should not like you to be looking at him when you ought to be looking somewhere else.” He paused. “And I know whom you suspect. Say nothing, to anybody, until you can prove it.”

Snape knew that voice. Very few people did. It belonged to the man who sat on the far side of the desk from the Ministry and the Wizengamot and, once, from somebody a great deal worse, and it did not say anything twice.

He ought to have bowed and gone. He did not.

“The boy has changed,” he said. “His manner. All of it. He used to look at the floor. Now he says the Dark Lord’s name across my classroom as though he were reading it off a list, and he looks at me—” He stopped. “When I look at him, Headmaster, I think of the Dark Lord. I do not know how else to put it.”

He had expected the Headmaster to be troubled. Instead the old man looked interested, and then, unforgivably, amused.

“You need not worry about Harry,” said Dumbledore. “He has something I lack, and that you, once, had a great deal of.”

“Don’t.”

“Love,” said Dumbledore, quite unrepentant. “It sent a little boy up my stairs this week who used to be frightened of his own shadow. I do not think he will go far wrong while he has it.”

Snape looked at him for a moment longer, with the face of a man who had heard this sermon before and would hear it again, and went out without a word.

“Why,” said Lyra Malfoy, “should *we* be frightened of *him*?”

Nobody answered her. Crabbe and Goyle, on the bench beside her in the corridor outside the library, looked at their shoes, which was an answer of a kind.

It had been three weeks since the Potions lesson. In three weeks, Slytherin House had learned to give Harry Potter the width of a corridor.

It had not been one thing. It had been a great many small ones, and the trouble with each of them was that nobody could prove it. Two third-years had cornered him by the greenhouses to teach him some manners, and had both spent the night in the hospital wing, and neither of them would say what had happened; Potter, who had a split lip, said he had walked into a door. A fourth-year who had tripped Weasley on the stairs was found the next morning with his head jammed in a lavatory in the dungeons, and Potter, asked about it in front of the whole Great Hall, looked so honestly puzzled that a Hufflepuff laughed out loud. Whatever you did to Potter, or to anybody near him, came back. It came back worse, and it came back without a single mark on it that a teacher could pick up and look at.

And through all of it he went about the castle with half the school at his elbow, laughing, as though it were all enormous fun.

So the Slytherins had stopped. They went round him. They did not catch his eye in corridors. And since the whole of Slytherin had gone quiet, nobody was much frightened of Lyra any more either. She could take out her wand in a corridor now and watch people go on walking. The second-years had stopped getting out of her way. And the worst of it, the part she could not forgive anybody for, was the game.

“*Potter’s coming!*” somebody sang out from the far end of the corridor, a Ravenclaw by the sound of it, and three or four voices laughed.

Lyra did not turn round. Goyle did, all the way round on the bench, and so, a fraction of a second before her, had Lyra’s eyes.

The corridor behind them was empty, apart from a suit of armour.

“You turned round,” said Goyle, under her breath.

“I did not turn round.”

“You looked.”

“I *looked*. One looks. It is not the same thing.”

“It’s a bit the same.”

“It is *not!*”

Because the name had started doing it to her too. In Slytherin they said *Potter* now very much the way grown-ups at her father’s dinners said the other name, quietly, and only when they had to.

“Malfoy,” said Crabbe, in the careful voice she kept for things she thought might be unwelcome, “couldn’t we just keep out of his way? Like everybody else?” She twisted the end of one plait round her finger. “My mum says you don’t argue with a wasp. You just go into another room.”

“Crabbe! Are you *deserting* us?”

“No! I only meant—”

“He is a *nobody*,” said Lyra. “He is an ordinary boy with a scar who happened to be lucky once, when he was too small to know anything about it. Why on earth should anybody go into another room for *that*?”

She believed it, too. She very nearly believed it.

At the start of the year it had been different. At the flying lesson she had come off her broom twenty feet up, which was not a thing she cared to think about, and Potter had thrown himself after her, and she had not minded nearly as much as she ought to have. She had been brought up to be looked at, and she knew what being looked at felt like, and she knew what he had looked like that day, reaching up for her with his hand out and his face wide open. He had been looking *up*. Even upside down and falling, she had been the one in the middle of the story.

Now, on the rare occasions he looked at her at all, his eyes went through her like something thin and cold, and then he let out a short breath through his nose, as if she had disappointed him again, and turned away.

It was not a way she was used to being looked at. It was not, she was quite sure, a way anybody had the *right* to look at her.

“I am not frightened of him,” said Lyra.

Goyle said nothing. Crabbe said nothing, more loudly.

“I am *not*—”

And then, as if somebody had arranged it, Neville Longbottom came out of the library with an armful of books and went past them towards the stairs, walking in the anxious, hurried way he always walked, as if he expected the floor to give way.

Lyra watched him go. It had been her plan since before Christmas, after all, and she had simply not got round to it: leave Potter alone, the one person stupid enough to say the name, and go for the people round him. Potter might be awkward. Longbottom was what Longbottom had always been.

She took out her wand.

“I’ve had an idea,” she said. “Let’s see if we can do the Leg-Locker Curse.”

Neville came into the Gryffindor common room through the portrait hole with his legs stuck together, and fell flat on his face on the hearthrug.

He had hopped. He had evidently hopped all the way from the library, up seven floors, with his books clutched to his chest and his knees locked, and half the common room was on its feet laughing before anybody had worked out it was not a joke. Hermione was the first to stop. She went across the room at a run, knelt beside him, and said the counter-curse, and his legs sprang apart, and he got up slowly, shaking.

“Neville?” said Harry. “What happened? Taking up the hopping?”

“Malfoy,” said Neville miserably. He sat down on the arm of the nearest chair as if his knees did not trust themselves yet. “Outside the library. She said she wanted to practise it on somebody.”

“Go to Professor McGonagall!” said Hermione. “Report her!”

Neville shook his head. “I don’t want any more trouble.”

“You’ve got to stand up to her, Neville!” said Ron. “She walks all over people, and you just lie down in front of her and make it easier—”

Harry got up.

He did not say anything at first. He took out his wand, and turned it over once in his fingers, and something in the way he did it made the Gryffindors nearest to him go quiet, and look at each other, and then look back at him with a sort of nervous, hopeful interest, as at a firework somebody has just lit.

“Outside the library,” said Harry. “That’s what you said, Neville?”

“Yes… why?”

“Why?” Harry closed his hand on the wand and put his head on one side. “Well, she can’t only practise on you. That’s hardly fair. Somebody ought to give her a go.”

“But there’s three of them,” said Neville. “There’s always three of them.”

“So?”

“Back in a bit,” said Harry to the room, and went out through the portrait hole.

He had got as far as the first staircase when he heard somebody scrambling after him.

“Harry, *don’t*.” Hermione had caught up with him, out of breath, her hair coming down. “You can’t do magic in the corridors. It’s against the rules.”

Harry tipped his head back towards the portrait hole, behind which Neville was presumably still sitting on the arm of a chair, trying to get his legs to stop shaking. Hermione followed the look, and pressed her lips together, and changed her ground.

“She’ll go straight to a teacher. She’s clever, Harry, she’ll make it look like it was all you. You’ll get into trouble.”

“So I’ll get into trouble.”

“She’ll go to *Snape!* You don’t know what he’ll do!”

“Snape.” Harry’s face went cold, as though somebody had opened a window behind it. “I’d quite like to know that myself. Whether he’d get rid of me while he was pretending to give me a detention.”

“*Harry!*”

Hermione’s mouth fell open. She looked up and down the empty staircase as if somebody might have heard, and then took hold of his shoulder.

“You’re being *strange*. You’ve been strange for weeks.”

“Strange?” Harry spread his arms, wide, as if he were showing her the whole castle. “Isn’t it stranger *here*? Everybody’s so scared of Voldemort’s name they won’t even say it, and all the people who followed him are walking about the place as if they owned it. And they call me the Boy Who Lived, and then they go on about being pure-blood at me. Does that sound normal to you?”

“But everything you *do* is dangerous! You know somebody wants to—Harry, somebody might really try to kill you!”

“I know,” said Harry. “I’m rather hoping they will. Then I’ll know.” He smiled at her as he said it, and did not seem to know what his face was doing. “Who’s who.”

Hermione let go of his shoulder.

She looked at him the way she had looked at the troll in the girls’ lavatory at Hallowe’en, which was not a way she had ever looked at Harry before. He pulled away from her and went on down the stairs.

“Oi! Harry! *Wait!*”

Ron came clattering down after them both, red in the face and grinning all over it, having climbed out of the portrait hole behind Hermione and come down two steps at a time.

“You’re not going to watch them hop about on your own, are you? That’s not fair either.”

He fell in beside Harry, still grinning, and started telling him in great detail what Crabbe would look like with her legs stuck together, and Harry laughed. It was his ordinary laugh. The face he had worn a minute ago on the stairs might never have been there at all.

Behind them, after a moment, Harry heard Hermione make a small, furious noise on the landing, and come after them.

They were still outside the library, the three of them, on the bench under the window, and they had seen him coming from the end of the corridor.

“You wanted me, Malfoy?”

Harry smiled as he said it. It was a good smile, and he knew it was; he had been practising since the summer, on the instructions of somebody who had told him in a robe shop to smile like he meant it. He meant this one. He meant it the way you mean a punch.

“You didn’t put the Leg-Locker on Neville for nothing,” he said. “Obviously. It was a message for me. You’d hardly waste a whole evening on something that childish otherwise.”

He watched it land. In a second and a half he had turned her into a girl who jinxed people’s legs in corridors for want of anything better to do, and she knew it, and so did the people beginning to drift over from the library doors to see what was happening.

Malfoy’s mouth went thin.

“Harry Potter,” she said.

“That’s right. The famous one.” He said it loudly, for the corridor. “Is that it? You went to all that trouble to say my name? Were you going to confess something?”

“Don’t be absurd. I wouldn’t. Not now, and not ever.”

She put up a hand and swept her hair back over her shoulder.

It was a very small thing to do. Harry, who had spent five months watching her across classrooms when he was supposed to be doing something else, knew exactly what it was for. The hair went back, pale and bright, and for a moment every eye in the corridor went with it, and he saw her feel them go, and stand up straighter because of it. Chin up. Shoulders back. She had done the same thing to him once, with her own two hands.

“I’m warning you, Potter,” said Malfoy. “That’s quite enough of your cheek.”

“Cheek? What cheek?”

“This, to begin with. But not only this.” There were twenty people round them now, and more coming, and she raised her voice to reach the back of them. “You pick fights with older students. You talk in Potions. You show the professors no respect at all. If you don’t learn your place, you’ll find things get very awkward. For you, and for everybody round you.” Her hand had gone to her wand. “Longbottom was a warning.”

Harry took a step towards her.

All three of them flinched, as neatly as if they had rehearsed it. Malfoy caught herself at once and turned it into something else, a shake of the head, but he had seen it, and so had the people at the front.

“Sorry, Malfoy, I’m confused,” he said, and he did look confused, honestly and openly, as though he truly wanted her to help him with it. “*I* pick fights? You mean those two third-years who waited for a first-year behind the greenhouses and lost? I’d keep quiet about that, if I were them. I’d be too embarrassed to come down to breakfast.”

Somebody at the back laughed. Then several people did.

“And respect?” Harry turned so that he was talking to all of them. “When did I tell a lie? Is it a lie that Snape was one of *his*? Everybody knows. They just don’t say it. Or is it a lie that he picks on eleven-year-olds because he can? Which bit was the lie?”

This time the whole corridor laughed: the Gryffindors, and the Hufflepuffs who had been on their way to the library, and a knot of Ravenclaws by the window with their books held against their chests. Everybody but the Slytherins.

It had taken him less than a minute. Crabbe and Goyle had both gone quiet, and Malfoy was biting her lip.

“Stop it! Both of you!” Hermione had pushed her way to the front. She looked at the crowd and then at Harry, and pulled at his sleeve. “Harry, that isn’t what we came for. We came to get an apology for Neville!”

“Did we?” muttered Ron, and stopped when Hermione looked at him.

“You’re going too far as well, Harry, you know you are. But *you*—” she rounded on Malfoy, “—you can’t just go round jinxing people because you feel like it! You owe Neville an apology!”

It was the worst thing she could have done. Harry knew it a second before it happened. Malfoy had just been made a fool of in front of half the school; she was not going to apologise to anybody, and certainly not because some girl with frizzy hair had stepped out of the crowd and told her to.

“Nobody asked *you* to stick your nose in!” Malfoy’s voice went high and shrill. “Get out of it, you filthy little *Mudblood*!”

The corridor changed.

Harry felt it before he understood it. He did not know the word; nobody at Privet Drive had ever needed one like it. But he could feel what it was from every face round him. The laughter went out of the place as if somebody had shut a door on it. Hermione went white. Ron, who five minutes earlier had been grinning about Crabbe’s legs, stepped in front of her with his fists clenched and his ears scarlet, and even some of the Ravenclaws by the window had gone pale with anger.

So it was that sort of word.

Harry moved first.

“*Locomotor Mortis!*”

Crabbe’s legs snapped together and she went down sideways, and her wand skittered away across the flagstones. Goyle had hers out a moment later. A jet of something hit Harry’s hand like a slap, and his wand went spinning out of it and away under the bench.

Ron went for Goyle. There was a great deal of flailing; Goyle shoved him so hard he went rolling across the floor, but he had got hold of her wand on the way, and by the time the two of them were on their feet again both wands had been flung down the corridor in opposite directions, and Ron and Goyle were glaring at each other with nothing in their hands at all.

Which left Malfoy.

Harry walked towards her. She had her wand up, and her hand was shaking.

“*Flipendo!*”

It hit him on the side of the head. The corridor tipped. He took one step to the side to stop himself falling and kept going, and was on her before she could say anything else. He got her wand out of her fingers and threw it as far as he could, and took hold of her wrist, and put her back against the wall.

She hit the stone with a thump. “*Ow!*”

“Watch your mouth, Malfoy.”

“Let—go of me!”

“You’re frightened of his *name*,” said Harry, “and you’ll say *that*? If you’re going to be frightened of words, be frightened of that one!”

“I’ll say it as often as I *like!*” She was frightened; he could see it all over her. She said it anyway, straight into his face. “*Mudblood!*”

“*Voldemort!*”

People flinched all round them, as if he had thrown something. Something was running down into Harry’s left eye, warm and wet. The jinx had split his eyebrow open. Half of the corridor had gone red, and he did not care; he could still see her.

“He tried to kill me,” said Harry, “and he couldn’t do it. He couldn’t even manage a baby, and then he disappeared. That’s what you’re frightened of? So frightened you can’t even say his name?” He leaned closer. “That isn’t frightened, Malfoy. That’s pretend. Do you want to know what being frightened is *really*—”

Her face crumpled.

He watched it go. She had been holding it all together the whole time, the hair and the voice and the chin, and now it went, and she burst into tears: not neatly, not like a girl in a book, but like a child, with her mouth open and her eyes screwed shut and great hiccupping sobs that echoed off the stone.

It went straight through him.

The anger went out of him as if somebody had pulled a plug. The pounding in his head went with it. And something cold and small and horrible came up in its place, and sat in his stomach.

He looked down at his own hand. It was still round her wrist, and there were red marks coming up on her skin where his fingers had been. That would hurt. He had not thought of that. He let go of her as fast as if she had burnt him, and took a step back.

“Wh—why are you *crying*?”

“I hate it,” sobbed Malfoy. She did not seem to know she was talking. “I *hate* it—Vol-Voldemort, and the—that word—all of it—I hate all of it—”

Harry stared at her. She had said the name. He did not think she knew.

“—and I hate *you*—”

It was the most childish thing he had ever heard anybody say. It hit him harder than the jinx had. He actually stepped back from it, as though she had pushed him.

Behind him, Crabbe got her legs apart. It had only been a first-year’s jinx, and it had given out. She came across the flagstones like a small round cannonball and hit Harry square in the chest with both hands.

“*Get off her!*”

He went over backwards. He was not expecting it, and he had nothing left to stop himself with; he sat down hard on the floor with his head ringing. By the time he had sorted out which way was up, Crabbe had Malfoy by one arm and Goyle had her by the other, and the three of them were going away down the corridor as fast as they could go, with Malfoy still sobbing between them.

After a storm, it turned out, everything went very quiet.

Harry lay on the flagstones and looked at the ceiling. By rights the corridor ought to have been cheering. The Slytherins had run; that was how these things were scored. Nobody cheered. Tears did not seem to belong to any House, and somehow, without anybody saying a word, the whole corridor had arrived at the conclusion that the person in the wrong was the one lying on the floor. People looked at him with uncomfortable faces, and then looked away, and drifted off in twos and threes towards their own common rooms.

Ron came and crouched beside him. “You all right? Sorry, mate. I should’ve had Crabbe.”

“Doesn’t matter.” Harry waved a hand at him. It was better this way, really. If she had stood there crying any longer he did not know what he would have done.

“*What* is going on here?”

Professor McGonagall came round the corner at a speed that did not seem possible in a woman her age, and stopped dead at the sight of Harry on the floor with blood all down one side of his face.

“Potter! Your head is bleeding!”

“I think I had a bit too much blood in it, Professor,” said Harry. “I was letting some out. Is there much left?”

McGonagall’s nostrils went thin and white.

“Fighting. *Again*. I told you the last time, Potter, I told you in so many words. I have never in all my years seen a Gryffindor start so many fights in so short a time. Fifty points, and a detention, and you may thank your stars it is not worse—”

“Why only me—”

“Fifty points from every student involved in this, Mr Weasley and Miss Granger *included*, and detentions all round! Hospital wing, Potter, this minute.” She turned on a tall Hufflepuff who had been edging away towards the stairs. “Mr Diggory. Who was Potter fighting?”

So Malfoy would get a detention too. Harry found that he was glad, and could not have said why.

He got himself up off the floor. His head had gone cold and clear, now that all the heat had gone out of it, and things were coming back to him in order. He could not quite remember, now, how he had got from Neville on the hearthrug to Malfoy against the wall. He remembered the word, and the corridor going quiet, and the name in his own mouth. Mostly, though, he remembered her face.

He had decided, in October, that he was going to change her. He had put his chin up on a broom and told himself so.

“I was going to change her,” Harry said, to nobody. “I made her cry.”

Madam Pomfrey mended his eyebrow in about a minute, with a great deal of tutting, and in the morning there was nothing to show where it had been. That was the last anybody saw of it. The Gryffindors did not tell the story, because it was not the sort of story that made Harry look good. The Slytherins did not tell it, because it was not the sort that made Malfoy look good either. By Easter it had gone the way of the Remembrall, and nobody spoke of it at all.

Crabbe came up to the dormitory after lights out, when the others were asleep, and sat down on the end of Lyra’s bed in the dark.

Lyra had the hangings drawn. She did not open them.

“I didn’t cry,” she said.

There was a pause on the other side of the curtain. Crabbe, who was the worst liar in Slytherin, and possibly in the castle, took a long time over it.

“No,” she said at last. “You didn’t.”

She went on sitting there. After a while Lyra put her hand out between the hangings, without meaning anything by it, and Crabbe took hold of it and held on, and neither of them said another word until Lyra was asleep.

She did not say the word again that year. The next time was the following autumn, on a Quidditch pitch, about some brooms, and that time it was Weasley who went for his wand. She went on flinching at the other one, the name, as everybody she had grown up with flinched at it. But there was a particular face that she flinched from more: a boy’s face, white and set, with blood running into one eye and the Dark Lord’s name in its mouth.

She was very good, by the summer, at not thinking about it. She was so good at it that a year and a half later, when a mild man in patched robes sat in a shabby armchair and asked her whether Harry Potter had ever given her a really bad fright, it did not so much as occur to her that there might be an answer.
