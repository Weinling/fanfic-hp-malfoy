## Chapter 1: Hostage

Lyra Malfoy had many virtues, and she saw no reason to be modest about any of them.

She was beautiful, for a start.

“Honestly, if she weren’t so pretty, somebody would have hexed her by now.”

She came from one of the oldest pure-blood families in Britain, and one of the busiest.

“Board of Governors, half the Ministry, the parents’ committee, and oh, look, there’s one in Slytherin as well. There can’t be more than four of them. How are they *everywhere*?”

And she was clever and quick and very, very good on a broom, which was the part that seemed to upset people most.

“Can somebody please do something about Snape? He marks her up in Potions, he hands Slytherin the pitch whenever they ask, and we’re meant to train where, exactly? The corridors?”

Lyra had been overhearing remarks like these since her first week at Hogwarts, and she counted every one of them as a compliment. The school had a natural order, and she sat very comfortably near the top of it.

Her friends gathered round her like ivy on a good wall. The hopefuls and hangers-on ran her errands, and anything properly unpleasant went to Crabbe and Goyle, who didn’t mind, or at any rate had never worked out how to say so. Her real smiles she saved for people who had earned them: her mother, her father, her little brother when he was behaving, and, on particularly good mornings, her own reflection.

There was, however, one flaw in the arrangement, and it wore glasses.

Harry Potter had been getting in her way since before she knew his name. He had been the silent boy on the next stool at Madam Malkin’s, the summer before first year. A month later, on the Hogwarts Express, she had found out who he was and offered him her hand in front of the Weasley boy, and he had looked at it and told her he could work out the wrong sort for himself, thanks.

Nobody had ever refused Lyra Malfoy’s hand before. She had not enjoyed the novelty.

In fairness, and Lyra was always fair when it cost her nothing, he wasn’t bad-looking. Without those dreadful round glasses he might even have been called handsome. This was a purely objective assessment, of the kind anyone with a trained eye would make, and it said absolutely nothing about her. His hair was a lost cause.

What she couldn’t forgive was everything else. Most families needed generations of careful marriages and well-placed donations to earn the kind of attention Potter had got by surviving something as a baby. He had been raised by Muggles, his mother had been Muggle-born herself, and the result wandered round the castle with his tie at half-mast, eating as though he expected somebody to take the plate away. Girls in the other Houses called this unpretentious. Lyra called it poor breeding, and she had been told more than once that she had a gift for accuracy.

What nobody called him was harmless. By the end of his first year Potter had become, by any fair reckoning, the worst-behaved boy in the school, and he paid back every slight with interest, whoever it came from. He had answered Snape back in front of the whole class and spent the rest of that term making sure Snape regretted it, and even the fifth-years had learned to give him a wide berth. When half the school decided over the winter that he must be the Heir of Slytherin, he announced at dinner that they were welcome to call him that, provided the Slytherins started kneeling. Lyra had found herself in the absurd position of defending him, since she had no intention of kneeling to anybody.

Then there was Quidditch. He had been made Gryffindor Seeker in his first year, the youngest House player in a century. This year Lyra had joined the Slytherin team, also as Seeker, and her father had very kindly equipped the entire side with Nimbus Two Thousand and Ones, which was a gesture of family generosity and had nothing whatsoever to do with anything. Granger had suggested otherwise, back in October, before she got herself petrified. That was why Lyra had called her a Mudblood, and she had no intention of apologising. Weasley had tried to curse her for it and spent the rest of the afternoon belching slugs, which remained one of the highlights of her year.

She did not discuss the November match. If anyone brought up the Snitch, or her left ear, or the few seconds in which the Snitch had apparently hovered beside her left ear while she was busy telling Potter exactly what she thought of him, Lyra changed the subject. Potter had caught it with a broken arm. People talked about that as if it were heroic rather than simply careless.

And somehow he was always at the centre of everything. First there had been the Philosopher’s Stone, and Dumbledore handing out points at the leaving feast like sweets until Gryffindor had snatched the House Cup out from under Slytherin’s nose. Lyra had bitten one of her nails almost to the quick that night, and Crabbe had pulled her hand away from her mouth before the nail was ruined entirely, which was probably the most useful thing she had done all year. Now there was the Chamber of Secrets, because of course there was. Potter apparently couldn’t get through a school year without nearly dying in some spectacular way, and the whole school had to stop and watch him do it.

Hogwarts without him, Lyra sometimes thought, would be very nearly perfect. Quieter. Better run. Considerably less likely to involve giant snakes.

The news reached the dungeons a little before midnight, and it arrived the way news always arrived in the Slytherin common room: sideways, in pieces, and from somebody’s cousin.

Ginny Weasley was alive. Dumbledore was back. Something enormous had been killed somewhere underneath the school, and Potter and Weasley had been involved, because naturally they had. A fifth-year came down from the entrance hall saying *basilisk*, and everybody laughed at him, and then Professor Snape appeared in the doorway with the expression of a man who had been ordered to be festive and was taking it personally. There would be a feast in the Great Hall, he informed them, now, in whatever they happened to be wearing, and nobody was to ask him a single question about it. Nobody laughed about the basilisk after that.

Lyra refused to go up with her hair the way it was. Millicent Bulstrode said nobody would notice. Lyra said that was hardly the point, sat down in front of the mirror in the second-year girls’ dormitory, and took ten minutes she didn’t really have. When she finally came down, in her dark green dressing gown with the silver embroidery at the cuffs, she looked, if not her best, then at least deliberate. Her hands had not been entirely steady with the brush. She blamed the cold.

Her father had been in an excellent mood all year. At Christmas he had written to tell her, in the careful way he wrote anything that might be read by the wrong eyes, that she should keep her head down, stay well out of whatever happened at school, and let events take their course. She had expected a scandal, perhaps. Arthur Weasley sacked from whatever dreary little office he worked in, and a few resignations among the governors who had always been too fond of Dumbledore. She had not expected a basilisk.

Still, nobody had died. That was the important thing. Her father was clever and well connected and had spent Lyra’s entire life making sure that consequences happened to other people. Whatever had gone wrong could be tidied up. It always was.

Crabbe and Goyle were waiting at the foot of the dungeon stairs in flannel nightdresses that had clearly been bought with growth in mind, and they fell in behind her without being asked. The three of them had just reached the entrance hall, which was full of noise and candlelight and Hufflepuffs in nightcaps, when someone stepped out of the shadow beside the marble staircase and said, “Malfoy.”

It was Potter.

He hadn’t changed. Everyone else in the castle was in their nightclothes, and Potter was still in his school robes, and they were filthy: streaked with something grey and slimy down one side, torn at the hem, and stiff along the right sleeve with what Lyra realised, after a moment, was dried blood. His hair was worse than usual, which she would not have thought possible. There was a fresh scratch along his jaw.

He looked, in short, exactly like somebody who had spent the evening under the school killing something, and Lyra decided on the spot that she was not going to be impressed by it.

“A word,” he said.

“I’m busy.”

“It’s about the diary.”

Behind her, Goyle made a confused noise. Lyra didn’t turn round. Something inside her had gone very still.

“Go on ahead,” she told them. “Save me a seat. Somewhere away from the Gryffindors.”

Crabbe looked at Potter, then at her. “You sure?”

“Did I ask for a second opinion?”

They went.

Potter led her down a side corridor off the entrance hall and into a classroom nobody seemed to have used in years. The desks had been shoved against the walls, the blackboard still held half a sentence about the uses of moonstone, and the only light came from the window, where the moon sat fat and pale above the grounds. It smelled of chalk dust and damp. Potter shut the door.

“If you’ve dragged me out of a feast,” Lyra began, “in my dressing gown, to—”

“Shut up for a minute and listen.”

She was so surprised that she did. People did not tell her to shut up. Weasley did, but Weasley didn’t count.

Potter reached into his robes and dropped something on the nearest desk. It was a small black book, or what was left of one. A hole had been punched straight through the middle of it, ragged at the edges, as if something long and sharp had been driven in through the cover and out the back.

The pages around the wound were soaked with ink that seemed to have bled out of the paper rather than spilled onto it. It had dried in thick, glossy streaks. Lyra had the unpleasant impression that until very recently the book had been alive.

Lyra knew it at once.

Last summer, in Borgin and Burkes, her father had laid out a row of objects on the counter for Mr Borgin to make an offer on. Lyra had been bored, and had been told twice to stop poking at the withered hand in the glass case, and she had watched him take a small black book out of his inside pocket, look at it for a long moment, and put it back. *Not that one,* he had said, mostly to himself.

“That’s Father’s,” she said, before she could stop herself. “He had it at Borgin’s—”

“I know,” said Potter.

She barely heard him. She was looking at the hole.

“You and your dad,” Potter said, “are in a lot of trouble.”

That brought her back. Lyra lifted her chin. “Do you have any idea who you’re talking to?”

“Yeah. That’s why I’m talking to you and not him.”

“Where did you even get that? If you’ve been going through my father’s things—”

“He chucked it on the floor in Dumbledore’s office.” Potter’s mouth did something complicated. “He was a bit upset. I’d just freed his house-elf.”

“You did *what*?”

“Dobby. He’s free. He’s got a sock.” He said this as though it explained anything at all. “Your dad worked out what had happened and nearly cursed me, and then he stormed off. I don’t think he noticed he’d left this behind. He had a lot on his mind.”

Lyra opened her mouth, found she had about eleven things to say and no idea which mattered most, and shut it again. Dobby was *theirs*. Dobby had been theirs since before she was born. You couldn’t simply go around freeing other people’s house-elves; it was like walking into someone’s house and giving away the furniture.

Her father must be livid. He might even be frightened, though she had never once seen him frightened, and she found she did not want to picture it.

“Why,” she said carefully, “are you showing me a ruined book?”

Potter looked at her for a moment. Then he pulled a chair away from the wall, sat on it backwards, and folded his arms along the top.

“It’s not a book,” he said. “It’s a Horcrux.”

Lyra waited for the rest of the sentence. It didn’t come.

“I don’t know what that is,” she said at last, and resented every word.

“It’s a bit of Voldemort’s soul.”

She flinched. She couldn’t help it; she had been flinching at that name since before she could read. Potter noticed. He didn’t say anything about it, which was somehow more insulting than if he had.

“He tore his soul up and put a piece of it in there,” Potter went on, “so that if anyone killed him, he wouldn’t die properly. Your dad’s had it for years. Then he slipped it into Ginny’s things so she’d be the one opening the Chamber, and Mr Weasley would get sacked, and Dumbledore would get chucked out. And it nearly worked. Ginny nearly died.”

“No,” said Lyra.

“Yes.”

Even as she said it, she could see Flourish and Blotts last August: her father picking the youngest Weasley’s battered Transfiguration book out of her cauldron, sneering at it, and dropping it back in. She had been standing right beside him. A few weeks later the first message had appeared on the wall, and she had not asked herself a single question about it. Questions were for people whose fathers weren’t Lucius Malfoy.

“You’re making it up. You’re making all of it up, you can’t possibly—” She stopped. “Prove it.”

Potter gave her the look you might give someone who had just tried to post a letter in a fireplace.

“Think, you idiot. How am I meant to prove it? I’d have to take it to the Ministry and get somebody to look at it, and then the Ministry knows it’s a Horcrux. So does everyone at the Ministry who used to be a Death Eater, and there are loads of them, your dad’s mates for a start—”

“My father’s friends are *respectable people*!”

“—and they all find out Voldemort made one, and that it’s been destroyed, and who had it last.” He flicked the diary with one finger, and it slid an inch across the desk. “You want me to do that?”

“Then hand it in and don’t tell them what it is.”

“And when someone works it out?”

“They won’t.”

“Your dad worked out what it could do,” said Potter. “It’s not that hard.”

There was no good reply to that. Lyra folded her arms instead, mostly so he wouldn’t see what her hands were doing.

“Honestly,” Potter said, “your dad’d be better off just saying he opened the Chamber himself.”

“He did *not* open the Chamber!”

“I know he didn’t. I’m saying he’d be better off. If he confessed, what’s the worst the Ministry could do? Stick him in Azkaban?”

“My father,” said Lyra, and her voice climbed in a way she couldn’t stop, “is not going to Azkaban. He knows everyone at the Ministry. He has friends on the Wizengamot. He’s on the Board of Governors—”

“Right. Exactly. So that’s the *good* version.” Potter leaned forward over the back of the chair. “The Ministry has trials. Your dad knows people, so maybe he gets a fine, or he loses his seat on the board, or at the very worst he goes to Azkaban for a bit. But if Voldemort comes back and finds out a piece of his soul got destroyed because your dad wanted to get at Mr Weasley, he’s not going to bother with a trial. He’ll just kill him.”

For most of Lyra’s life, the Dark Lord had lived somewhere between history and a ghost story. Grown-ups lowered their voices when they spoke of him, if they spoke of him at all. Some families still went pale at any mention of the war, and some, the older and grander ones, remembered it with an expression Lyra had never been able to read and had learned not to ask about.

Nobody at the Manor said out loud that her father had served him. Nobody needed to. Lyra had grown up with the sense of an enormous locked room at the heart of the house, and she had been raised, like any sensible Slytherin, to respect whatever was kept in locked rooms. It was why she had always hated the careless way Potter said the name. He tossed it about like a Chocolate Frog card, as if it were just a word, and every time he did it she wanted to tell him, very sharply, to stop.

She didn’t want to tell him anything now. She was looking at the diary and the ink that had bled out of it, and trying to take in that a piece of the Dark Lord had been inside. Had been in her house, in her father’s pocket, on the counter at Borgin’s while she poked at a withered hand in a glass case. And now it was dead, and the thing that had killed it was a fang, and the person who had driven the fang in was sitting backwards on a chair in front of her with blood on his sleeve, looking tired.

“Technically,” she heard herself say, “*you* destroyed it. Not my father.”

“I don’t think Voldemort’s going to care about technically.”

She couldn’t argue with that, which annoyed her almost as much as everything else.

“Your dad’s just cost Voldemort one of his seven lives,” Potter said. “Your family needs to keep its head down. Properly down. No more favours for his old friends, no more—”

“Seven?”

Potter stopped.

“You said seven,” Lyra said. “Seven lives. He made *seven* of these things?”

“That’s what he was going for.” There was a pause before he added, “Seven’s a powerful number. Everyone knows that.”

It was a short pause. Lyra had grown up at a dinner table where half of what mattered went unsaid, and she knew what a pause like that was for.

“Everyone does not know that he tore his soul into seven pieces. I didn’t know he’d torn it into *one*. You just said nobody can know about any of this, and you’re twelve.” She stared at him. “How do *you* know?”

“Does it matter?”

“Yes!”

“Not tonight, it doesn’t.”

“Did Dumbledore tell you?”

Potter looked at her very steadily and said neither yes nor no, and Lyra understood that he had no intention of saying anything at all. It was the look her father wore when a dinner guest asked too directly about his investments. She had never seen it on a boy who couldn’t knot his own tie, and it sat on him oddly, like something handed down from someone much older. She made a note of it for later, when she was not standing in a freezing classroom in her dressing gown.

“The houses,” Potter said.

“What houses?”

“You lot have got houses all over the place. Cottages, and places on the estate, and somewhere up north. And people stay in them sometimes, don’t they? People who got let off after the war because they said they’d been Imperiused.”

Lyra’s face went hot. “That is a disgusting thing to say.”

“I didn’t say anything. I asked.”

“You didn’t ask. You never ask anything, you just *say* it, as if you—” She made herself breathe. “We do not harbour criminals.”

Potter shrugged.

It was a perfectly ordinary shrug. But there were guest cottages on the Wiltshire estate that Lyra had been told since she was small not to go near, and there had been men who came up the drive after dark and were gone by breakfast, and there had been one winter when a soft-spoken man lived in the east cottage for a month and her mother had asked her, very gently, never to mention him at school.

There were names that stopped conversations. There were friends of her father’s who had spent the entire war under the Imperius Curse, poor things, and turned out to be such excellent company afterwards. Lyra had never needed to ask about any of it. That had always been the comfortable thing about being a Malfoy: some doors stayed shut, and behind them the grown-ups dealt with whatever needed dealing with, and you were allowed not to know. Potter had just walked up and kicked one of those doors in, and he’d done it with a shrug.

“If Voldemort comes back,” he said, “those people aren’t going to look after you. They’ll be first in the queue to prove they were loyal all along. And the easiest way to do that is to hand him whoever lost his Horcrux.”

She could see it far too clearly: the drawing room at the Manor full of her father’s old friends, every one of them smiling, every one of them so terribly sorry.

“So your family needs to stay out of it,” Potter said. “All of you. And if they can’t…” He hesitated, and for the first time that night something in his face looked twelve. “Then you, at least.”

Lyra stared at him.

“Me?”

“Yeah.”

“Why on earth would you care what happens to me?”

He seemed to hear the question and decide it was in a language he didn’t speak. “This is blackmail, Malfoy,” he said instead. “Just so you know.”

“Oh, *is* it?”

“If anyone finds out what this diary was, and who had it, and how it got destroyed, your family’s finished. The Ministry’ll want to know why Lucius Malfoy was carrying round a piece of Voldemort. The Death Eaters’ll want to know why he lost it. You’ll have both lots turning up at your door with knives and forks, and they’ll sit down to dinner on everything you’ve got.” He picked the diary up again, gingerly, by one corner. “So if you don’t want that, you’re going to be our hostage.”

For a second Lyra genuinely didn’t understand the words.

“Your *what*?”

“Hostage.”

“*Our*,” she said. “You said *our*. Whose? Yours and Weasley’s? Is Granger in on it, is that what she’s been doing in the hospital wing, lying there plotting—”

“Not your problem.”

“It is *entirely* my problem, I’m the hostage!”

“Look,” said Potter, and rubbed his forehead just beneath the scar, as if it ached, “I’m not going to lock you in a cupboard or anything.”

He said it in an odd, flat voice, like a joke that wasn’t one. Lyra had no idea what to make of that, so she ignored it.

“How extremely comforting. As a rule, that *is* what happens to hostages.”

“It’s not like that. You’re…” He hunted for the word. “Insurance.”

“Against *what*?”

“Your dad. And anyone else who reckons the Malfoys ought to go back to Voldemort when he turns up.” He held her gaze. “I don’t need you to spy on anyone. I’m not asking you to nick letters out of his desk or listen at doors. You probably don’t know anything useful anyway. Your dad’s not going to tell his twelve-year-old his plans.”

“Excuse me?”

“Well, he isn’t.”

“*You’re* twelve!”

“Yeah, and nobody tells me anything either,” said Potter, with feeling. “That’s not the point. The point is, you’re with us now, and your family’s going to know it. Your dad’ll think a lot harder about helping Voldemort if it means helping him against you. And you’re going to make sure he thinks about it.”

“And if I say no?”

Potter turned the diary over in his hands.

“Then I stop keeping this quiet.”

Lyra looked at him. Not five minutes ago he had spent a great deal of breath explaining why he could never tell anyone what the diary was, and now he was threatening to tell everyone. It was probably a bluff. She was fairly sure it was a bluff. She was not, however, prepared to bet her father’s life on it.

The classroom was very cold. Somewhere down the corridor the feast had started. She could hear it faintly through the walls, a roar of voices and the scrape of hundreds of benches, and somebody cheering. Gryffindors, probably. Probably for him.

That morning the world had been simple: good families and embarrassing ones, Slytherins and everybody else, a father who could fix anything. She had never much cared who won the war, if there was ever another one, because whichever side won, she had always assumed she would still be standing in the middle of it. Now the floor of that world felt as thin as glass, and she could hear it beginning to crack.

It had not occurred to her until tonight that a world might arrive in which the Malfoys were simply in the way.

“Fine,” she said.

It came out very small. Potter raised his eyebrows, and she could have hit him.

“*Fine*,” she said again, louder. “I’ll do it. I’ll be on your side, whatever that means. Are you happy now?”

“Not really,” said Potter. “But thanks.”

She had not been expecting that, and it wrong-footed her.

“You’re smarter than I thought, Malfoy,” he added.

“Than you *thought*?”

“I’d leave it there, if I were you. That’s the nicest thing I’ve ever said to you.”

He got up off the chair and held out his hand.

Lyra looked at it. It was a thin, grubby hand with dried blood in the creases of the knuckles and a raw scrape across the palm, and she knew it. Or rather, she knew the shape of the moment, because she had been on the other side of it. Two years ago on the Hogwarts Express she had stood in a compartment doorway and held her own hand out to this exact boy, and he had looked at it and turned it down, and she had carried that around ever since like a stone in her shoe.

Now the hand was his, and she was the one expected to take it. In the moonlight the scrape across his palm had the ugly look of a bite, and Lyra thought of a snake striking: how fast it would be, and how you would see the fangs before you ever felt them.

Everything she had been brought up to believe told her to turn round and walk out. Potter was a Gryffindor. Potter was a jumped-up, Muggle-raised nobody with a scar and a legend and no manners, who had never in two years done anything but get in her way.

But she was a Malfoy, and Malfoys survived. That was rather the point of them.

She took his hand.

His grip was warm and dry and a good deal firmer than she had expected. He shook once, as though sealing something, and let go.

“Go and have your feast,” he said. “Act normal.”

“I always act normal.”

“Act like you, then. Same thing.” He tucked the diary back inside his robes and went to the door, and stopped there with his hand on it. “And Malfoy? Don’t write to your dad about any of this. Not by owl. Tell him when you get home.”

Then he was gone, and Lyra was alone with the moonstone sentence and the smell of chalk and the distant roar of the Great Hall.

She stayed there longer than she meant to, going back over all of it: the Horcrux and the number seven and the cupboard, and the way he had said *then you, at least*, as if it had cost him something to say it. She went over it line by line, looking for the mistake. It was only when she reached the door herself that she found it.

*That’s Father’s,* she had said. *He had it at Borgin’s.*

And Potter had said, *I know.*

Lyra stood with her hand on the cold brass handle and thought back to Borgin and Burkes last summer: the dusty counter, the withered hand in its case, Mr Borgin bowing and scraping, her father taking the little black book out of his pocket and putting it away again. There had been nobody else in the shop, nothing but junk and that hideous black cabinet by the door. Potter could have heard about it somehow, she supposed. Knockturn Alley was full of people who would sell anything, gossip included.

But he had also said *somewhere up north*, as carelessly as if he were remarking on the weather.

The house in Northumberland was not in any register. Her father had made very sure of that. Lyra had been there exactly once, when she was seven, and in the five years since she had not been allowed to mention it even to Crabbe and Goyle.

Potter knew it existed.

## Notas de la versión 2

Potter entra ahora hacia la palabra 1.300, frente a la 1.950 de la versión publicada en Wattpad. El capítulo pasa de unas 5.100 palabras a unas 4.650 y no pierde ninguna semilla que se pague más adelante.

**Qué se recorta.** La primera escena baja de unas 1.400 palabras a unas 950. Salen los Gryffindor que se erizan como gatos, los profesores que aplazan entregas, la descripción larga de Madam Malkin’s (el capítulo 3 ya la cuenta), las notas de Potter gracias a Granger, el párrafo de “froth” y parte de lo que Lyra esperaba del plan de su padre.

**Qué se mueve.** La frase del heredero de Slytherin pasa al párrafo “What nobody called him was harmless”. El recuerdo de Flourish and Blotts pasa al aula, justo después de que Potter diga que Lucius metió el diario entre las cosas de Ginny: ahora Lyra dice “No” sabiendo que estaba delante cuando ocurrió, lo que convierte su negación en autoengaño.

**Qué no se toca.** La escena del aula y el final quedan como estaban, salvo los párrafos de más de unas 150 palabras, que se parten en dos para leer mejor en el móvil.

**Base.** La pestaña original no coincidía con lo publicado en Wattpad, que es posterior. La v2 incorpora los cambios hechos a mano en Wattpad: Millicent Bulstrode en lugar de Pansy, Crabbe y Goyle en camisón al pie de la escalera de las mazmorras, Northumberland sin Pansy y el Potter que replica a Snape delante de toda la clase. Conserva, en cambio, al hermano pequeño en la lista de sonrisas, que falta en Wattpad y que el capítulo 2 da por sabido.
