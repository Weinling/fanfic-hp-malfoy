# Chapter 27: Ferret

The answer came on a Thursday, by an owl so ordinary that nobody at the Slytherin table looked at it twice.

It was a school owl, brown and bored, the kind that carried half the castle’s post. It dropped a thin envelope beside Lyra’s plate, helped itself to a corner of her toast without asking, and left. The envelope said *Miss L. Malfoy* in a hand she had seen on the blackboard every week for a year and had never once seen on anything addressed to her.

She put it in her bag, unopened, and finished her breakfast. Goyle was explaining to the table how a dragon might be got into a school, for the first task, if it were very small, or asleep. Crabbe was watching the bag.

She had written to him ten days ago, on the first of November, on plain parchment with no crest. She had given it to a school owl with no address on it at all, because owls did not need one, and had spent the next week rather hoping it would get lost. It had taken her four drafts. The first had been three pages long. The fourth had said what had happened, in the order it had happened, and finished: *I do not expect this to settle anything. I thought you should know who carried it.*

She opened his in History of Magic, under the desk, while Professor Binns told the back row about the goblin rebellion of 1612 in a voice like a door that would never quite finish closing.

*Dear Miss Malfoy,*

*Thank you for your letter. I have read it several times, which I suspect you would rather I had not, and I am going to answer it honestly, since I think that is what you were asking for.*

*I knew by moonrise that something was wrong with the potion. I did not know that you had seen it. I will not pretend that it makes no difference to me that you did.*

*All the same, I have had the better part of a year to think about that night, and I keep arriving in the same place. You were thirteen, and in a hurry, and a man who had spent twelve years being overlooked was counting on somebody being in a hurry. That is not nothing. Neither is it the whole of it, and I think you know that, or you would not have written.*

*You will have heard by now why I left. It is true, and the decision was mine. Nobody was bitten, and that was luck, and I do not care to rely on luck where children are concerned. I did not leave because of you. Please do not add that to anything.*

*I had the chocolate, by the way, in case you wondered.*

*You need not write again. I should like it if you did.*

*Yours sincerely,*

*R. J. Lupin*

*P.S. I told you once that I was in your debt. I find that I still am. I imagine that will annoy you.*

Lyra read it twice. Binns had reached 1613.

He had looked at what she owed him, and agreed with her about every penny of it, and then written underneath, in the same mild handwriting, that the account was still running the other way.

You could not settle with a man who insisted on owing you. It was the most underhand thing anybody had ever done to her, and she sat in the back of History of Magic with the letter flat under her hand, and found that her eyes had gone hot, and that she could not have said, if Binns had asked her, what century it was.

Crabbe, beside her, did not look round. She turned to a clean page and began, without a word, to take notes for both of them.

Professor Moody had pushed the desks back against the walls. That was the first thing anybody saw, coming into Defence that morning, the last lesson before lunch: a bare stretch of floor in the middle of the room, and Moody standing at the far end of it with his wand out, and the magical eye going round and round like a thing looking for a way out.

“Imperius Curse,” he said, when they were all inside and the door had shut itself. “I told you what it does. Now I’m going to show you what it feels like.”

Parkinson laughed, and then stopped, because nobody else had.

“The Ministry says you’re too young. Dumbledore says you need to know. I agree with Dumbledore.” The normal eye went along them. “Some of you’ll throw it off. Most of you won’t. The ones who can’t had better find out now, here, from somebody who’ll stop, than later from somebody who won’t. Parkinson. You were enjoying yourself. Out you come.”

Parkinson came out, a little less pleased with himself, into the middle of the floor. Moody raised his wand. “*Imperio*.”

A slow, stupid peacefulness spread over Parkinson’s face. Then he crouched down, and put his hands on the floor between his feet, and began to hop across the room like a frog: four great solemn hops, with his cheeks puffed out, until he hit the wall.

Nobody laughed this time. Moody lifted the curse, and Parkinson came back to himself crouched against the skirting board, and looked up at them, and went the colour of a beetroot.

Crabbe sang. She was put under, and stood in the middle of the floor with her hands clasped in front of her, and sang the whole first verse of a Celestina Warbeck song in a clear, true, astonishingly sweet voice that nobody in Slytherin had ever heard before, and that Lyra suspected nobody would ever hear again. When it was over Crabbe came back to herself, and looked round at them, and her face did not change in the slightest. She walked back to her place beside Lyra as though she had been out of the room.

“Anyone laughs,” said Lyra, quite quietly, to the Slytherins nearest, “and I will remember it.”

Nobody laughed.

Goyle did a handstand and could not get down from it.

“Malfoy.”

Lyra walked out into the middle of the floor. She did not hurry and she did not drag her feet. She stood where Parkinson had stood, with her hands at her sides, and looked at the magical eye, since that was the one that was looking at her.

“*Imperio*.”

It was the loveliest feeling. That was what nobody had said. Everything she had been carrying went out of her at once, the letter in her bag and the badge in her pocket and the ring beside it, and a cup of fire, and her father’s travelling cloak with the mud on its hem; it all lifted off her and floated away somewhere she need not follow it, and left her light, and clean, and empty.

*Get up on the desk*, said a voice. It was a kind voice. It sounded as though it had her best interests at heart. *Get up on the desk.*

There was a desk against the wall on her left. It would be the easiest thing in the world to get up on it. Her knees were already deciding how.

*Why?* said another voice.

It was much smaller than the first one, and a good deal less kind, and it sounded very like her own, with something underneath it that sounded a little like her father’s. It did not want to know why in the sense of *is this wise*. It wanted to know what she would be getting for it.

*Get up on the desk.*

*And then what? What’s in it for me?*

Nothing, so far as she could see. Somebody wanted something from her, and was offering nothing in return, and was being very charming about it, which was what people always did when they were offering nothing in return. Malfoys did not do favours.

She stayed where she was.

*Get up—*

“No,” said Lyra aloud, and the lovely emptiness tore across like wet paper, and she was standing in the middle of a cold classroom with all of Slytherin looking at her, and her heart going very fast, and everything she had been carrying back where it belonged, heavier than before.

Moody lowered his wand.

“Well, well,” he said.

The magical eye had stopped going round. It was looking at her, and so was the other one.

“Strong,” said Moody. “That’s very strong, for fourteen. Most grown wizards can’t do that, first time.” He tilted his head. “Who taught you that, Miss Malfoy? Your father? He’d know how it’s done. From the other end.”

The room had gone very quiet.

“Nobody taught me, sir,” said Lyra.

“No,” said Moody. “I don’t suppose they did.” He jerked his head. “Go on. Next.”

She went back to her place. Crabbe moved up to make room for her, and did not look at her, and pressed her shoulder very briefly against Lyra’s, and took it away again.

When the bell went for lunch, she sent Crabbe and Goyle on to the Great Hall without her. Crabbe looked at the classroom door, and then at Lyra, and went without arguing, towing Goyle by the sleeve.

Moody was putting the desks back. He did it with his wand, without turning round, so that they slid in from the walls one after another and settled into their rows with small wooden knocks, like an orchestra sitting down.

“Come to tell me something about your father?” he said to the blackboard.

Lyra shut the door behind her.

“I’ve come to ask you to stop using my family as teaching material, Professor. Twice now. In front of the whole class.”

“Thought you lot liked that. Snape’s always on about who your families are, I hear.” The desks went on knocking into place. “No? All right. What else?”

She had a perfect right, she said, as a Slytherin, to ask what was being done about a fourth-year who had cheated his way into the Tournament, when they all knew what he was like—

“Oh, give over,” said Moody.

He turned round. The magical eye went straight to the pocket of her robes, the way it had in September, and rested there.

“Worried about your boyfriend?”

For a moment Lyra did not think anything at all.

“Hallowe’en night,” said Moody. “First-floor classroom, the one with the bad window. Locking Charm on the door. Potter’s got a good cloak. I’ve got a good eye.” He tapped the side of it with a fingernail. “It doesn’t stop at doors, girl. It doesn’t stop at cloaks, either. I’ve been watching the pair of you since the first night of term.”

There were a number of things she might have said. She went through them very fast, and threw them all away, because every one of them was a lie and he would see straight through it, the way he saw through doors.

“And?” said Lyra.

“And nothing. Keep it quiet. You’re doing all right so far.” He limped to his desk and lowered himself into the chair behind it, with the wooden leg stuck out in front of him. “A sweetheart’s the first thing a Dark wizard goes for. Easiest lever there is. You keep it so nobody knows it’s there, and nobody can pull it.”

He sounded almost approving. She had not expected that, and did not trust it.

“Then you know whose side I’m on,” she said.

“Do I?”

“I could help.” She had not meant to say it like that, but it was said now, and she went on. “Somebody put his name in that cup. Whoever it was, the people who’d know about it aren’t going to tell you. They’ll never tell an Auror anything. They might tell me. I’m in Slytherin, and I’m a Malfoy, and nobody in my House would think twice about saying things in front of me. I could find out.”

Moody looked at her for some time with both eyes.

“No,” he said.

“Why not?”

“Because you’re Lucius Malfoy’s daughter.” He said it quite calmly, as though it were her date of birth. “I don’t tell you what I’m doing. I don’t tell you what Potter’s doing. And I don’t take anything you bring me, because I don’t know who gave it to you to bring.”

Lyra had stood outside a wall like this once before, in a shack, in the dark, with her pride on the floor and Potter refusing to tell her anything for her own good. She knew the shape of it. It was not the same wall, and the man on the other side of this one was watching her through a hole.

“My father has nothing to do with it.”

“Your father,” said Moody, “had people on strings once, and some of them are walking about to this day and don’t know it.” The magical eye rolled down and up again. “And here’s his girl, come out of nowhere into Harry Potter’s pocket, the year somebody wants Harry Potter dead. You see how it looks.”

“That has nothing to do with—”

“Maybe he asked you to,” said Moody. “Nicely. Daddy’s little helper. Maybe he didn’t ask. Maybe he put it on you, and you don’t know.” He leaned forward. “You threw mine off very prettily just now. Very prettily indeed, for his daughter. I keep asking myself where a girl learns that.”

Her hands had gone cold. She could feel them, the way you can feel a draught.

“Take that back,” said Lyra.

“Can’t, I’m afraid.” He got up, and came round the desk, and stumped past her towards the cupboard in the corner, close enough that she could smell the hip flask on him. “It’s my job to think it, and yours to put up with it. The people we’re up against do worse than suspect a child. If you’re what you say you are, it’ll do you no harm. Constant vigilance, Miss Malfoy.”

He had his back to her. He was reaching up to the top of the cupboard, as though she were already gone.

Her wand was in her hand. She had no curse in mind. She had nothing in mind at all except that she would not be talked about like that, by anybody, to the back of his head, and that he was going to turn round and *look* at her while she—

Moody spun on his wooden leg faster than anything that size had any right to move.

“*Never*—”

There was a crack, and a white light, and the floor came up at her with a great rush, and went on coming.

“—point a wand at a man’s *back*! That’s a coward’s trick, girl! You want a duel, you bow, and you face him!”

The room had become enormous. That was the first thing. The desks went up and up into a dimness overhead, and the legs of them were like the trunks of trees, and the floor was very close, and smelt overwhelmingly of chalk and old floor polish and Moody’s boot. She tried to get up, and could not find where up was. Something was wrong with her hands. She looked down at them, and they were small and white and furred, and had claws on the ends.

She made a sound. It was not any sound she had ever made before.

Somewhere far above her, Mad-Eye Moody said, “Hah,” with deep satisfaction.

The door opened.

“Professor Moody? You said after lunch, about the—”

It was Potter. From down here he was mostly shoes: a pair of scuffed black school shoes, one lace undone, and a great deal of trouser going up into the gloom. The shoes had stopped just inside the door.

Lyra knew what she looked like. She had seen her own paws. She was small, and white, and furred, and she had a tail, and the last small furred animal Potter had had anything to do with had spent twelve years asleep on Weasley’s bed and then sold his parents to the Dark Lord. If she went near him he might very well kick her across the room, and nobody could blame him.

She ran at his feet anyway. There was nowhere else in the whole enormous room she wanted to be.

He did not kick her. He bent down and picked her up, quite without hesitating, with one hand round her middle, so that her back legs dangled and her front ones stuck out in front of her like a pair of tongs. It was the most undignified moment of her existence, and she told him so at the top of her voice. It came out as a long, furious squeak.

“Sorry,” said Potter. “Sorry—hang on.” He shifted his grip, and tucked her into the crook of his arm against his chest, with her back legs supported and her head up under his chin, and held her there firmly, the way you might carry something that mattered. Then he looked past her at the floor.

Her wand was lying there, where it had fallen. He had seen that wand pointed at him a great many times. He looked at it, and at Moody, and at her, and she felt him work it out through his chest.

“Is this—”

“Your girl pointed her wand at my back,” said Moody. He had gone back to the cupboard. “So now she’s a ferret. Wears off inside the hour. Do her good.”

“That’s a bit much, sir.” Potter bent, carefully, without letting go of her, and picked the wand up and put it in his own pocket. “She’s fourteen.”

“And her father’s Lucius Malfoy. I told her, and I’ll tell you.” The magical eye swivelled round to fix on Potter. “Careful, Potter. Don’t trust anybody with a great deal to lose. That sort’ll put a knife in your back the minute it’s turned.”

Potter considered this. Lyra could feel him considering it, a slow rise and fall under her paws.

“If Lyra wants to make holes in me,” he said, “I can spare a couple.”

Moody looked at him for a moment. Then he snorted.

“And sir,” said Potter. “I know Dumbledore told you to leave Professor Snape alone. I still think you’d do better watching him than her.”

“The one who looks harmless,” said Moody, “is the one you watch. Never mind the ones who look like trouble. I don’t change my methods for Dumbledore, or Snape, or you.” He jerked his head at the door. “Tomorrow, Potter, same time. Go on. Take your ferret.”

Potter took his ferret.

The day of the hippogriff he had run with her under a cloak, blind, and stumbled on the stairs, and caught himself on the wall with his shoulder rather than let go. This time he walked. Down the corridor, and down the marble staircase, one hand cupped round her back and the other under her, as though he had all afternoon. Lyra lay along his forearm and could hear his heart going, much too fast for a boy who was only walking.

In the Entrance Hall a tall black shape came up the dungeon steps and stopped.

“Potter.”

“Professor.”

“The list of animals permitted at this school,” said Snape, “is an owl, a cat or a toad. I do not recall a ferret.”

“It’s on loan, sir. From Professor Moody.”

At Moody’s name Snape’s mouth went thin. He looked down at the ferret. The ferret looked back at him. It went on a little longer than Lyra liked. She had been looked at by Professor Snape for three years, and she knew this look; it was the one he gave a potion that had come out the right colour for what he suspected were the wrong reasons.

“Then I suggest you return it to him,” said Snape, “before it bites you. They do.”

He went on up the marble staircase without looking back. Potter waited until the black robes had gone round the corner. Then he let out a breath, and carried her out of the great oak doors into the cold bright afternoon.

He took her down to the lake, to the big beech on the bank above the water, where the ground was dry and nobody much came in November, and sat down with his back against the trunk and set her on his knee.

“So,” said Potter. “You’re a ferret.”

Lyra sat up on her hind legs and looked at him with all the contempt a ferret could manage, which turned out to be a surprising amount.

“A very nice one,” said Potter. “I mean that. Your ears are good.” He held out one finger, and she considered biting it, and decided that she would rather save it for something that deserved it. “You went to see him on your own. Because of what he said in your lesson.”

She had not told him what Moody said in her lesson. Moody must have. Or Potter had simply guessed, from the look of her, which was worse.

“And then you offered to help, didn’t you,” said Potter. “Spy on Slytherin for him. Find out who put my name in.”

She squeaked. It was meant to convey that he had no business knowing that either, and that she had been perfectly entitled to offer, and that she would do it anyway, with or without Mad-Eye Moody’s blessing. Potter seemed to understand at least the last part. He looked at her for a while without saying anything, and rubbed his thumb once along the top of her head, between the good ears.

“Thanks,” he said. Then, after a moment: “He knows about us. You’ll have worked that out. He’s known since the first week of term. He told me in October.” He rubbed the back of his neck. “I’d have told you. You weren’t answering.”

That was perfectly fair, and she disliked it extremely.

There were voices coming along the bank.

Three of them, Hufflepuffs, fourth-years, with Zacharias Smith out in front. Every one of them had a large badge pinned to the front of their robes, glowing a soft red. *Support CEDRIC DIGGORY*, said the badges, *the REAL Hogwarts Champion!* As Lyra watched they flickered, and the letters rearranged themselves into something greener and much shorter. POTTER STINKS.

One of the three was the boy she had once made cry over a Chocolate Frog card. She had told him, last year, in a corridor, that he would make a natural ferret. She remembered it now with great clarity.

“Oh, look,” said Smith. “All on his own. That’s a shame, Potter. Mind you, you needn’t worry. Somebody’ll come running to save you. They always do, don’t they?”

Lyra had a great deal to say to that. She said all of it. Nobody heard a word.

“And what’s that?” Smith leaned closer and peered at her. “Your pet? You should take it into the first task with you. It can die for you.”

Potter stood up. He did it without hurrying, with Lyra tucked into his arm, and she felt him go very still and very pleasant, which was how she knew it was bad.

“Does Diggory know you’re wearing those?” he said. “I bet he’s asked you not to. I bet he hates them. He’s a decent bloke, Cedric. Shame about his fans.”

Smith went red. It was plainly true. One of the other two looked down at his own badge, as though he would have liked to take it off.

“Does it bite?” said Smith, to cover it, and poked a finger at her.

It did.

Smith yelped and snatched his hand back and put the finger in his mouth, and the boy with the Chocolate Frog card made a noise that might have been a laugh, very quickly swallowed. Lyra settled back into the crook of Potter’s arm. She felt a good deal better.

“Harry!”

Granger was coming down the slope from the castle, fast, with her bag bumping against her hip, and Weasley behind her, not fast at all. The Hufflepuffs looked at the two of them, and at Potter, and decided they had somewhere else to be. Smith went last, nursing his finger and saying loudly that it was probably diseased.

“What did they want? Were those the badges? I *told* them those were—oh!” Granger had seen her. Her whole face changed. “Oh, Harry, what a *sweet* ferret! Where on earth did you get it?”

She reached out a hand to stroke her. Lyra flattened herself along Potter’s arm and slid round the back of his elbow, out of reach.

“Oh. It doesn’t like me.”

“It’s shy,” said Potter.

Lyra had never been shy for a single minute, and she looked up at him to make sure he knew it.

“You shouldn’t be on your own, Harry,” Granger was saying. “Not with those badges about. You ought to stay with your friends.”

Potter looked past her. Weasley had not stopped. He had gone on along the bank without them, with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders up round his ears, and was standing by himself at the edge of the water, a long way off, throwing stones in.

“Tell him that,” said Potter.

Granger sighed. “He thinks you put your name in and didn’t tell him. That’s all it is. He thinks you could have told *him*, at least.”

“I didn’t put my name in.”

“I know you didn’t. I *know*. He’ll come round.” She looked at Weasley’s back. “He always does.”

Lyra could have told them a good deal about the Weasleys, and what came of growing up seven to a house with nothing in it that somebody else had not had first. She could not say any of it, and found, somewhat to her surprise, that she did not much want to.

“Go after him,” said Potter. “I’m all right. Honestly.” He lifted the arm with the ferret in it, a little. “I’m not on my own. I’ve got her.”

Granger looked at the ferret. Then at Potter. Then, much more slowly, back at the ferret.

“Harry,” she said. “Is that—”

“Go after Ron.”

She went. She looked back twice on her way down the bank, and the second time she was very nearly laughing.

Potter sat down again under the beech. He lifted Lyra up in both hands until they were face to face, and then, very solemnly, touched the end of his nose to the end of hers.

“Sorry,” he said. “First time I take you out anywhere, and it’s this.”

She looked at him. He had grass on his knees and leaves in his hair, and a finger that she had decided not to bite, and his face was very close and still unhappy about Moody. She put her front paws on his chest and pushed, quite hard, which was as near as she could come to telling him not to be ridiculous.

It seemed to help. He smiled.

“Can I kiss you?”

Lyra squeaked. It was a clear and unambiguous no.

“No?”

She showed him her teeth.

“Fair enough,” said Potter, and laughed, and did not let go of her.

She sat on his chest and thought about it.

She was a ferret. There was nobody on the bank, and nobody on the slope, and Granger was a small figure a long way off with her hand on Weasley’s arm. A ferret could not be entered in anybody’s accounts. Nothing a ferret did could possibly count, in any ledger she had ever heard of, which meant that it could not cost anything either, and that nobody, afterwards, need ever owe anybody for it.

She shut her eyes and stretched out her nose.

He kissed the very end of it. She felt it right down to the tip of her tail.

Then she felt something else. It began at the end of her nose, where he had kissed her, and ran backwards all over her like pins and needles, as if every hair on her were standing up at once; and the bank went small around her, and the beech came down out of the sky to its proper size, and Potter said, “Oh—hang on—” and dragged at his bag, and there was a rush of something silvery and cool, and the world went dim and soft at the edges.

She was sitting on the grass beside him, under the beech, under his cloak, in the middle of the grounds in broad daylight. She had leaves in her hair. Her robes were twisted round under her. Her knees were drawn up nearly to her chin, and her face was so hot she was surprised the cloak did not steam, and Potter, six inches away, was being very careful indeed not to laugh.

“That,” said Lyra, “did not count.”

“No?”

“I was a *ferret*.”

“You were a very nice ferret.”

She hit him. Under an Invisibility Cloak it was difficult to aim, and she did not try especially hard.
