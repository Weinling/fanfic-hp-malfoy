# Year Four, Part 3: Out of Your Depth

It had been brought to Lyra’s attention, in February, on a rock, that the Hogwarts champion could not swim.

She had given the matter some thought. There was a third task to come, and nobody knew what it was. There might be water in it. There was a lake at the bottom of the lawn, at any rate, and it was the first hot Saturday in May, and she considered that a person who had been underwater for an hour in front of three schools had no business being unable to do the thing on purpose. It was slovenly. She mentioned this to him over breakfast. She mentioned that she’d looked into it, and that it was a matter of attitude.

“Have you ever taught anyone to swim?” said Potter.

“Certainly not.”

“Can you swim?”

“I have had lessons,” said Lyra, “from a Frenchwoman, at Biarritz, in a costume with sleeves. I fail to see it’s relevant. One doesn’t have to be able to lay an egg in order to know when it has been done badly.”

They went down at two. She took up her position on the big flat rock at the edge of the shallows, in a clean blouse and her good shoes, with a parasol, and a silver whistle that she had got from Madam Hooch by means she did not propose to go into. Crabbe sat on the shingle behind her with a stopwatch, because somebody had to. Weasley had come for the sake of the spectacle. He’d brought a sandwich.

Potter stood in the lake up to his shins. He had on an old pair of trousers, rolled, and a shirt that had been made for somebody a good deal wider, and he had taken off his glasses and handed them to Crabbe. He looked out over the water, peering, with his arms held slightly away from his sides. He had faced a dragon with rather more assurance.

“Further out,” said Lyra.

“It’s cold.”

“It is water, Potter. I’m told that’s one of its properties. *Further out*.”

He waded. It came up to his knees, and to his waist. The shirt filled up with air and rose gently round his ears. She blew the whistle, once, for tone, and it went off across the lake and put up a duck.

“Now,” she said. “Attend. It’s perfectly simple. The human body floats; it has been established. People drown because they have taken up a defeatist position. You will lie forward upon the water, with your chin up and your shoulders back, and you’ll conduct yourself as though you had a perfect right to be there. Do you understand? It’s a matter of attitude.”

“Right,” said Potter.

He seemed to give that some consideration. Then he took a deep breath, and shut his eyes, and with a look of absolute confidence on his face he lay down on the lake, and sank.

It was the most extraordinary thing. There was no struggle. He went down at a slight angle, all in one piece, like a dropped spoon, with his chin up. A number of bubbles came to the surface, in a thoughtful way.

“Four seconds,” said Crabbe.

Nobody moved. Lyra stood on her rock. She had been about to say something about the shoulders. She looked at the place where he had been; the ripples went out from it, and it was a very calm day.

“Six.”

“Weasley,” said Lyra.

“He’s doing really well,” said Weasley, with his mouth full.

“Eight. Nine.”

“*Weasley!*”

He sighed. He laid the sandwich on a stone, and took off his shoes, with no great haste, and walked in. He was fifteen, and he had got most of the way to being six feet tall, and it hadn’t reached his armpits. He went along the bottom, wading, with his long arms hanging, feeling about with one foot. He stopped. He reached down into the lake and rummaged, and came up with a fistful of wet shirt collar; and after it, streaming, with weed on one ear, there rose a thin dark boy. He stood there hanging from the end of Weasley’s arm, about a foot off the bottom, and he coughed up a surprising amount of water. His expression had not altered.

“How was that?” said Potter.

She blew the whistle.

“That,” said Lyra, “was the most disgraceful thing I have ever seen. You went down like a *deed box*. Where were your legs? I’ve seen better form from a dropped sandwich. Crabbe, what was that?”

“Twelve and a half,” said Crabbe. She wrote it down.

“Again.”

They did it again. They did it, by the end of that long bright afternoon, nineteen times. She tried him with his chin up and with his chin down. She had him lie on his back. She had him think of something buoyant; he said he’d thought of Slughorn, whoever that was, and he went down faster than ever. She directed from the rock, with the parasol, in a clear carrying voice, and she got a certain amount of sun on her nose, and at some point in the third hour she discovered that she’d taken off her shoes. Weasley had stopped going back for his sandwich. He just stood there, up to his chest in the lake with his red hair drying in spikes, finishing an apple with one hand and holding Harry Potter up by the neck of his shirt with the other. It seemed to have become a thing that he did. Every so often he would let go, for purposes of research, and look down at the bubbles, and fish about, and bring him up again.

“You’re not *trying*,” Weasley told him, shaking him a little, to get the water out. “You’re just going down there. I’ve seen you. You like it.”

“It’s nice,” said Potter, dripping. “It’s quiet.”

“You’re a menace, mate. What’s she paying you?”

“Fourpence,” said Lyra. “And he owes me for the whistle.”

It got to be five o’clock. All the long shining lake had turned the colour of weak tea. Crabbe closed her notebook, and she held it out at arm’s length, and looked at it. He had gone from twelve and a half seconds to fourteen. It was, she said, in her low reasonable voice, an improvement in the sense that it had taken him longer to be got out.

He sat on the edge of the rock, in the sun, with his feet in the water. He had his glasses back. There was a bit of green stuff in his hair, and he looked as happy as she had ever seen him. Weasley lay on his back on the stones with his eyes shut.

“I can’t swim,” Potter remarked.

“No.”

“I don’t think I’m going to be able to.”

She looked down at him. She reached out, with the end of the parasol, and removed the weed. She considered the boy, the lake, and the whole of the situation.

“You needn’t look so pleased with yourself,” said Lyra. “It’s a matter of attitude. We shall simply have to arrange for you to be held up.”
