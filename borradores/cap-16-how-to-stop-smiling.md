# Chapter 16: How to Stop Smiling

A week before the holidays, in the Shrieking Shack, the door banged once against its frame and stayed open, and the rain came in.

Harry did not go after her. He stood where she had left him, in the middle of the room with his hands in his pockets, and listened to her going away through the brambles until he could no longer tell her from the rain.

“There wasn’t another way,” he said. It came out flatter than he had meant. “One more step in and she’s hurt. Properly hurt.”

On the broken chair, Sirius shifted. He had the look of a man who could see that something needed saying and had picked, out of everything on offer, the worst thing.

“Bit of a risk, though, isn’t it? I’m not saying anything against her, Harry. But that’s Lucius Malfoy’s girl, and the Malfoys have always— She’ll be home for Christmas in a week, and if she sits down to dinner and mentions what she saw today—”

“Sirius.” Harry did not turn round. “I didn’t tell her any of that thinking she’d keep it to herself. I told her what she could shout off the Astronomy Tower and not hurt anybody.”

There was a silence behind him. When Sirius spoke again his voice had gone very quiet.

“That’s fair. I picked the wrong man once, didn’t I. Picked him because it was easy.”

Harry had not meant it like that. Or perhaps he had, a little. “That was twelve years ago. We’ve got enough to do with this year.”

He turned round. He had got his face in order by then; he was good at that, these days.

“Quirrell showed us he wasn’t dead. That was first year, with his face on the back of somebody’s head. The diary showed us how. That was Lucius, last year, slipping it into Ginny’s cauldron to make trouble for her dad.” He counted it off on his fingers, as though it were a list of homework. “Horcruxes. More than one. Some of them handed out to his followers to mind. He could have come back by now if he’d wanted to, and he hasn’t, so he’s waiting for something.” He shrugged. “Lucius Malfoy sold his own master out to win an argument with Arthur Weasley. Without that diary we’d still be guessing.”

“Then let them sink,” said Sirius. “If the Malfoys bring the roof down on their own heads, I don’t see why we should—”

He stopped. Harry did not know what was in his own face just then. Whatever it was, it made Sirius look at him the way he had looked at him earlier, when Malfoy was still in the room: as if at somebody else.

“Not on her,” said Harry. “Not on the boy.”

Sirius was quiet for a long moment. “All right.”

Harry let out a breath. “Right. We’re busy. Malfoys weren’t the only ones he trusted with his spares. Lestranges, Blacks, Flints, for my money. Every one he made cost him a bit of himself, so he won’t have handed them out like sweets.”

“The Blacks.” Sirius rubbed a hand down his face. “Then you’ve picked the one family I can’t get you into. I walked out at sixteen and they burnt me off the tapestry. Regulus would’ve known, if anybody did. Regulus has been gone for years.”

Harry thought of Malfoy writing to her mother, and wondered whether that had really been all her mother had written back, and said nothing about it.

“Then we’ll start somewhere else. And there’s a job that comes before any of it—”

“—there’s—” Sirius began, and stopped dead, and his face went smooth and empty as if a hand had been passed over it.

“Pettigrew,” said Harry, for him. “I know. Dumbledore’s already sent for help with that.”

“Of course he has.”

“He’s been planning this since before I could walk.”

There was more to it than that, and Harry did not say it, to Sirius or to anybody. So that was the plan, and it was a good one, and every piece of it was where it was meant to be. Harry looked at the door, which was still open, and at the rain coming in, and could not think why the room should feel so much bigger than it had an hour ago.

“Sirius,” he said. “Can I ask you something?”

“Anything.”

“How do you stop smiling?”

Sirius looked at him. Harry knew what he must look like; he could feel it from the inside. There was nothing on his face for Sirius to answer.

“You don’t,” said Sirius gently, after a while. “James never could. You’ve got his grin. It comes with the face.”

“No.” Harry looked at the gap between the floorboards, over by the wall. “I didn’t have it. Not when I was eleven.” He was quiet for a second. “Somebody gave it to me.”

The summer he was eleven, Harry did not know what a broomstick was for.

He had found out he was a wizard the night before, from a giant, and Diagon Alley had not given him a moment since to get used to it. A witch went by with a cauldron full of shopping that was trying to climb out. A man in a lilac cloak was arguing with a mirror outside the apothecary, and losing. Somebody’s parcel hissed at Harry as it passed. Everyone else stepped round these things the way people in Little Whinging stepped round puddles, and Harry, who had no idea which of them were puddles and which were not, stepped round all of them, kept his head down, and tried not to be noticed doing it.

Hagrid had left him at Madam Malkin’s and gone off to the Leaky Cauldron, and Harry was standing on a footstool at the back of the shop while a witch pinned black robes up round his ankles. On the stool beside his was a girl.

She was about his height, standing on her stool, with very fair hair that went most of the way down her back and shone like something that had been polished. She had a small, pointed, perfect face and grey eyes, and she was standing absolutely straight, with her chin up, as though somebody might be about to paint her. She looked like the dolls in the window of the toyshop in Little Whinging, the expensive ones in boxes, the ones the other girls’ mothers bought them and Harry had never once been allowed to walk too close to. Yesterday Harry would have said there was no such thing as magic. Today he was buying a wand. He wondered, quite seriously, whether somebody might have made her. He very nearly put out a finger to see if she was warm.

She caught him staring.

“What? Have I got something on my face?”

“No! Sorry. I just—” He floundered. “I thought you might be a doll. One somebody made. With magic.”

Her eyes went very wide. For a moment she seemed unable to decide whether to be furious; then something happened at the corners of her mouth, and she coughed, and lifted her nose an inch higher.

“Well. I’ll let that go. This once.”

After that she talked, and Harry listened. She talked about Quidditch, and which House she would be in, and he had to admit that he did not know what the Houses were, which made her stare. There were four, she explained, with the patience of somebody explaining something to a very small child, and Slytherin was by far the best, because only the purest and most beautiful wizards were allowed in.

“Then it suits you,” said Harry, because it seemed true.

She beamed. Nobody had ever looked at Harry like that in his life, as though he had handed her something. “I like you,” she announced. “You’ll do. Even if you don’t know anything.”

She told him about families, and how there were good ones, and old ones, and then there was the wrong sort, and one learned to tell them apart. Harry, who had been the wrong sort every day of his life at number four Privet Drive, did not say anything to that. She asked about his parents, and he told her they were dead, and that he lived with his aunt and uncle, who were Muggles, and that he had not known about any of this until yesterday.

“Then your uncle’s wasting his time,” she said. “You can’t keep it in. It leaks. When I was six, Father said I couldn’t have a pony, and every peacock in the garden turned pink. What did you do?”

“I made some glass disappear. At the zoo.”

She looked at him sharply. “Without a wand? That’s practically a Vanishing Charm.” Her eyes narrowed. “Well. They don’t even teach that till fifth year. I expect I’ll be early.”

Harry could not see why that should make her cross. As far as he was concerned, the glass and the peacocks were about as impossible as each other. She did not stay cross, though. She told him about her family’s owls, of which there were so many that they had a room just for them, and Harry, who had never had a letter in his life until this summer, said it must be like Christmas every morning, and she looked very pleased with him again. Then she pointed across the street, through the shop window, to the window opposite, where a broom stood on a stand with a crowd of boys round it.

“Nimbus Two Thousand. It’s ugly, but it’s the fastest there is.”

“Fastest?” Harry looked at it. “Does it sweep better?”

She turned round on her stool and stared at him.

“You *ride* it,” she said, as though he had asked whether a wand was for stirring tea.

“Sorry,” said Harry. “I didn’t— We haven’t got them where I live. Sorry.”

She went on looking at him, and something in her face set. “Don’t.”

Harry knew what came next; it was what always came next. Uncle Vernon had told him he was hopeless often enough that he believed it, a bit; perhaps he had done it again without noticing, whatever it was he always did. He heard his own voice go small, as it always did at number four.

“Sorry. I don’t know much yet, I—”

“*That.* That’s what I mean!” She jabbed a finger at him. “Sorry, sorry, sorry. Stop apologising for nothing! You’re a *wizard*. You were one before anybody told you, so you can stop standing like a Muggle—”

“Miss, your hem—” said the witch with the pins, but the girl had already hopped down off her stool.

She put both hands on Harry’s shoulders and pushed them back, briskly, the way you might straighten a doll. “Shoulders back.” Her hand came up towards his chin, and Harry jerked his head away from it; hands that came at your face at number four did not usually mean anything good. He found he had shuffled halfway along his stool. She did not seem to notice. She simply followed him, and tipped his chin up with one finger. “Chin up.” And then, while his face went hot all the way to his ears, she put two fingers at the corners of his mouth and pushed them up. “And smile. Like you mean it. You don’t lose your dignity, ever, anywhere. Mumbling is for Muggles.”

“Sor—” said Harry, and shut his mouth on it just in time.

A tall, fair woman had appeared in the shop doorway, as cool and lovely as the girl, and was looking at the two of them without much expression. “Darling. We’re waiting.”

“Coming, Mother!” The girl let go of his face and looked him over, as if checking her work. “There. You’ll want to practise that. Practise it all month, and by September you might be fit to be seen with.”

Seen with. In September. At Hogwarts, then, where he was going too; where he would be going for seven years. Harry had never in his life had anything to look forward to that lasted longer than a birthday.

“If I get better,” he said. “At being a wizard. Will that— would you think I was all right?”

“That’s the idea.”

Then she took the sides of her robes in her fingers and sank into a small, neat curtsy, the sort he had only ever seen on Dudley’s television, and Harry nearly fell off his stool. She was gone a second later with a toss of her hair, plainly very pleased to have had somebody to show it to. He was fairly sure she would have forgotten all about him before she reached the corner, and a month later, on the train, he found out that he had been right. Harry did not know any way of saying goodbye except waving, so he waved, through the shop window, until he could not see her any more.

He realised afterwards that he had never asked her name, and she had never asked his. She had never once looked under his fringe.

Hagrid was at the door a minute later with an ice cream in each enormous hand. “There yeh are! All done? I’ve bin stood out here like a lemon—”

“Sor—” said Harry.

He stopped. He put his shoulders back. He lifted his chin, and he looked Hagrid in the eye, which was a long way up.

“I made a friend,” he said. “I think. A girl. She’s going to Hogwarts too.”

Hagrid did not shout at him for not saying sorry. He did not tell him to stop staring. He beamed all over his enormous face and said that Harry must take after his dad, then, making friends before he’d even got his robes. And it occurred to Harry, standing there on the step, that looking at the floor had never once kept anything from landing on him at number four. It had only meant he never saw it coming. He might as well point his chin up and look.

Hagrid handed him an ice cream. “Go on, then. What’s she like?”

Harry looked down the street, the way she had gone.

“Magic,” he said.

The rain was still coming in at the door. Sirius had not moved from the broken chair; he was waiting, with the patience of somebody who had had twelve years’ practice at it.

“She told me to smile,” said Harry. “Like I meant it. She told me not to lose my dignity, ever, anywhere.” He nearly laughed. “So I didn’t. I’ve been doing it ever since. The first time I ever stood up straight to anybody was on the train, a month later.” He looked at the doorway. “It was to her.”

“Ah,” said Sirius.

“And now she’s told me to stop.”

He went over to the wall, to the place where two of the floorboards had pulled apart, and got down on his knees, and then flat on his front in the dust. The gap was just wide enough for a hand. He put his arm into it up to the elbow, and then up to the shoulder, and felt about in the dark among the cobwebs and the cold and things he did not want to think about, until his fingers closed on something small and round.

It came up grey with dust. He wiped it on his sleeve, and the brass showed through.

It was still warm.

Harry sat back on his heels and held it for a while in his closed hand. Then he put it in his pocket, next to his wand, and got up, and did not smile at all.

At the door he stopped.

“Dumbledore’s somebody,” said Sirius behind him. “For Pettigrew. Who is it?”

“Dad’s other friend. The one person left who can stand up and say who Pettigrew is.” Harry looked back once, at the man on the broken chair. “He’s been teaching me Defence since September. Remus Lupin.”
