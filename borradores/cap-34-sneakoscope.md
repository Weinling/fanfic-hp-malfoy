# Chapter 34: Sneakoscope

By the second week of January, eleven separate people had told Lyra that they had always known.

Montague had known since the train in third year, though he could not say what he had seen on it. One of the Warrington cousins had known since the match in second year, when Potter fell off his broom, which proved nothing except that Warrington had not been watching the Snitch. A fifth-year girl whom Lyra had never spoken to in her life stopped her outside the Charms corridor to say that she had seen it in their faces at the Quidditch final, and that she thought it was terribly romantic, and that she had a cousin in Ravenclaw who wanted to know whether it was true about the Listening Charm.

“It was not,” said Lyra, and walked on.

Nobody, it turned out, had ever thought it was a secret. They had simply never said, because nobody had asked them; and now that the whole school had watched her stand up in the Great Hall and take Harry Potter’s hand, they could not stop saying it, in corridors and on staircases and behind library shelves, in the pleased, important voices of people who had been right about something.

Panther Parkinson had not known, and would not have it.

“It’s *political*,” he told the third-years in the common room, on the first Sunday of term, with his back to the fire. “Obviously. You don’t think she actually— Look at it. The Boy Who Lived, Dumbledore’s pet, a champion, and he’s eating out of her hand. You couldn’t buy that. Her father’s had people trying to get near Dumbledore for years.” He lowered his voice, for effect. “She’s playing him. She’s been playing him since the Ball.”

The third-years looked at Lyra, in her chair by the window, to see whether this was so.

Lyra turned a page of her Arithmancy, and let them look.

It was useful, Parkinson’s theory. It was the most useful thing he had ever said. It was also, almost to the word, what her father had written to her on the twenty-ninth of December, and she sat with her book open on her knee and the ring on her finger and did not read anything for some time.

“I knew,” said Crabbe, with dignity, to a second-year at breakfast. “I’ve known since we were eleven.”

Lyra got up from the table and left.

Potter, for his part, appeared to have decided that if the whole school knew, the whole school might as well watch. He was at the foot of the dungeon steps on the Monday morning when she came up to breakfast, leaning against the wall with his hands in his pockets, in full view of the Entrance Hall.

“You’re in the way, Potter.”

“I’m walking you to Arithmancy.”

“Arithmancy is on the seventh floor. You have Divination. In the other tower.”

“I know,” said Potter, and fell in beside her.

He did it the next day, and the day after. He carried nothing for her and opened no doors and said nothing to her that anybody could repeat; he simply walked beside her up seven flights of stairs and down again, with his shoulder an inch from hers, while the portraits leaned out of their frames to watch and the Grey Lady went through a wall to avoid them. Lyra told him every morning that it was ridiculous. She did not tell him to stop.

On the Wednesday, Professor Karkaroff stopped her in the Entrance Hall on her way in to dinner. He had his furs on, and he did not take her hand.

“Miss Malfoy. I wished to say. Viktor— the business before Christmas— I was very much at fault. A misunderstanding. I hope you will not think too badly of Durmstrang.” He said it all very fast, as though he had been carrying it about for a fortnight and wanted it off his hands. Then, lower: “Your father has written to you? Since?”

“Yes, sir.”

He waited for more. She did not give him any, and after a moment he smiled with all his teeth and went away up the marble staircase, rubbing his arm.

On the Monday after that, her father’s owl came down the Great Hall at breakfast with a red envelope in its beak.

The whole Slytherin table flinched. Goyle put her hands over her ears. Somebody at the Hufflepuff table said, “*Howler*,” in a voice of pure joy, and half the Hall turned round. The eagle owl dropped it neatly beside Lyra’s plate and settled on the edge of the table to wait, and the envelope began at once to smoke at the corners.

Lyra did not run. She had never in her life run from a piece of paper. She sat quite straight, with her hands in her lap, and looked at it.

The envelope opened its mouth.

“*Lyra*,” said her father’s voice.

He did not shout. That was what nobody at the table had been ready for. It was his ordinary voice, the one he used across the breakfast table at the Manor to ask for the marmalade, magnified until it filled the whole Hall, perfectly calm and perfectly clear.

“*When your father writes to you, you will answer him. Your mother is worried. The owl will wait.*”

The envelope burst into flame, and curled, and was gone.

There was a short silence along the Slytherin table.

“That,” said Goyle, taking her hands off her ears, “was the quietest Howler I’ve ever heard.”

“It was practically a lullaby,” said Parkinson, who sounded disappointed.

Lyra took out a quill.

She did not take long over it. She wrote it at the breakfast table, on a page torn from the back of her Arithmancy notes, with the eagle owl watching her and half of Slytherin reading upside down, and she blotted it on her napkin when she had done.

*Dear Father,*

*Thank you for your letter. He confides very little. I find I don’t mind.*

*Your loving daughter,*

*Lyra.*

She tied it to the owl’s leg herself. The owl looked at her with great orange eyes, as if it had read it, and went.

Her mother had not answered her at all.

“Miss Malfoy,” said Moody, at the end of Defence on Friday. “Stay behind.”

The Slytherins went out past her with their eyes down and their ears open.

Moody did not keep her in the classroom. He jerked his head at the door behind his desk, the one that led up into his office, and stumped up the three stone steps ahead of her without looking round to see whether she followed; and she followed, because there was no version of not following that did not end in a ferret.

She had never been inside it. It was smaller than she had expected, and much fuller: a desk heaped with papers and odd brass instruments that ticked or hummed or, in one case, very faintly wept; a cracked mirror on the wall that showed nothing in it but grey shapes moving; a window with the curtain half drawn. And in the corner, under the window, a trunk.

It was a large, old, scarred trunk with seven locks down the front of it, seven keyholes, the brass gone green. Lyra looked at it once, and then at the seventh lock, and then at Moody, and knew that he had watched her do all three.

“Sit,” said Moody.

There was a chair in front of the desk. She sat on it.

He did not sit. He put two things down on the blotter between them, one after the other, the way a man lays down cards. The first was a glass top, something like a spinning top, with a little brass point at the bottom; it stood upright on the blotter by itself and did not fall over. The second was a crystal flask about the length of her finger, stoppered, with a clear liquid in it that might have been water.

“Know what that is?”

She knew what the second one was. She had read about it in the restricted section in her second year, when she was looking for something else.

“Veritaserum,” said Lyra.

“Three drops,” said Moody, “and you’d tell me what you had for breakfast on your sixth birthday, and who you’d like to kill, and in what order.” He tapped the flask with a scarred finger. “I won’t. Not without Albus’s say-so, and Albus won’t give it, not for a student. But I’ve got it. I thought you should see it.”

She saw it. She thought, quite clearly and quite fast, of a diary in a girl’s cauldron, and her father’s hand putting it there, and seven of something, and a cave; and of what three drops of that would do to her family; and she made her face do nothing at all.

“And the other one?”

“Sneakoscope.” The blue eye rolled down to the glass top and back up to her. “Cheap thing. Goes off if there’s somebody untrustworthy about. Gets it wrong half the time, round me.” He showed his broken teeth. “Let’s see if it gets you right.”

Then he leaned his fists on the desk and began.

“You’ve been on Igor Karkaroff’s ship four times since November.”

“Professor Karkaroff is an old friend of my father’s,” said Lyra. “It would be rude not to call.”

The Sneakoscope did not move.

“Who sent you the first time?”

“My father wrote and asked me to take him a letter.”

“What was in it?”

“I don’t know. I didn’t open it.”

That was true. She had held it up to the window, once, in the dormitory, and seen nothing, and put it away; but she had not opened it. The glass top stood on the blotter as still as a candlestick.

“The last time. Before Christmas.”

“Krum,” said Lyra. “For the Ball.”

“Which you turned down.” The dark eye was on her face; the blue one was on the Sneakoscope. “In front of the whole Great Hall.”

“That is what I said in the Great Hall,” said Lyra.

There was a pause. Moody made a sound in his throat that might, in another man, have been appreciation.

“Clever,” he said. “Your father’s good at that too.”

She did not answer that, and he did not seem to expect her to. He straightened up off his fists, and looked at her for a long moment with both eyes at once, which very few people could do and which she did not enjoy.

“Why Potter?”

It was not the question she had prepared for. She had prepared for Karkaroff, and the letter, and her father, and Durmstrang, and she had an answer for every one of them that was true as far as it went; and he had walked round all of them and come in at the side.

She gave him the answer that had been ready since she was eleven, for anybody who asked her about anything that mattered. She said it lightly, with a little lift of one shoulder, the way her mother said things at dinner that were not meant to be taken up.

“To pass the time.”

The Sneakoscope went off.

It spun on its brass point so fast it blurred, and lit up from inside, and gave a high, thin, furious shriek like a kettle left on a fire, and went on shrieking, and spun itself across the blotter until it fetched up against a stack of papers and stood there screaming at her.

Moody did not look at it. He watched her.

“Again,” he said, when the noise had died to a whine. “Change the part that isn’t true. Leave the rest.”

Lyra sat in the chair with her hands in her lap.

There was nothing she could do. That was the extraordinary thing; she went through it, very quickly, and there was nothing. She could not lie, with that on the desk, and she could not refuse, with the flask beside it; she could only say it again, with the wrong part taken out, to Mad-Eye Moody, in his office, with the trunk in the corner where Potter had spent Christmas.

“He asked first,” said Lyra slowly. The Sneakoscope was silent. “He always asks first. And I—”

She stopped.

“And I accepted,” said Lyra, “because I *wanted* to!”

It came out louder than anything she had said all term, and there was no taking it back, because there it was in the room: said, to the last person in the world she would have said it to, before she had ever once said it to anybody else. Not to Crabbe. Not to him.

The Sneakoscope stood quite still on the blotter.

She could feel her face. It had gone hot from the collar up and there was nothing at all she could do about that either; so she did the only thing that was left, which was to lift it, and look Mad-Eye Moody in both his eyes, and hold them, as though she had said something perfectly ordinary and was waiting for him to have the manners to go on.

Moody did not laugh.

She had been ready for that, too, and it did not come. He picked up the little flask of Veritaserum and put it away inside his coat, and righted the Sneakoscope, which had fallen over on its side and was humming to itself, and set it back in the middle of the blotter. Then he lowered himself into the chair behind the desk, with the wooden leg stuck out stiffly to one side, and sat looking at her for a while.

“Your boy,” he said, “came into this office looking for a dead man.”

Lyra waited.

“Barty Crouch’s son. Bartemius.” Moody said the name as if it had a bad taste he was used to. “I put him in front of the Wizengamot myself, with three others, and his father sent him down for life. He died in Azkaban a year after. I saw him buried, girl. I stood at the grave.” The blue eye swivelled, very slowly, round to the trunk in the corner, and back. “And your boy comes in here in the middle of the night with Sirius Black and goes straight to the seventh lock, as if he’d been told where to look, to find a lad who’s been in the ground twelve years.”

*A man who sent his own son to Azkaban. His own son, to die there.*

Lyra kept her face exactly where it was. She had been doing it all her life; it was the one thing she had always been able to do better than anybody, better than her father, and she did it now with the whole of her attention, so that Mad-Eye Moody, who was looking for something in it, should not find it. Inside, behind the face, Karkaroff’s yellow teeth were saying *to die there*, and stopping, and looking at the door.

“He wouldn’t tell me how he knew the name,” said Moody. “I asked him twice a day for three days. He wouldn’t tell me why he thought a dead boy was sitting in my trunk, and he wouldn’t tell me who’d put it in his head. He just looked at me. Like he was sorry for me.” His mouth went sideways. “Albus says the boy has his reasons. Albus has been getting a good many reasons from that boy, this past year, and I’ll tell you something for nothing, Miss Malfoy. He doesn’t like it any more than I do.”

He leaned forward over the desk.

“Do you know? What’s in his head?”

“No,” said Lyra.

The Sneakoscope did not stir.

Moody looked at it, and then at her, and grunted.

“No,” he said. “Didn’t think you did.”

She did not know what to make of the way he said it. It was not triumphant. It sounded, if anything, as though he had wanted her to.

He sat back.

“I’ll tell you one thing more, and then you can go and do whatever it is you do in that dungeon. Igor’s arm has been itching since the summer. You’ll have seen him rub it. He’s not the only one with something under his sleeve that itches, and you know as well as I do who else has.” He let that sit. “He’s coming back. I don’t know how and I don’t know when. But I’ve been at this thirty years, and I know the smell of it.”

Lyra said nothing. There was nothing in her mouth to say.

“And when he does,” said Moody, “one of you dies. You or Potter. I’ve seen it go that way more times than I can count, girl, and I’ve never once seen it go any other.” The magical eye had stopped moving. Both eyes were on her, and there was something in them she had not seen there before and did not want: not suspicion. Something worse than suspicion. “Can you keep your heart in one piece? Between him and Dumbledore’s lot? With your family on one side and that boy on the other?”

He did not wait for an answer.

“I couldn’t,” said Moody. “And I’d nobody on the other side at all.”

Then he jerked his head at the door, and she got up, and went down the three stone steps into the empty classroom on legs that did not seem to belong to her, and out into the corridor, where Crabbe was sitting on the floor against the wall with her bag in her lap, waiting.

Crabbe looked up at her face, and got up, and did not ask.

The ring began a little after nine that night, warm and then cool and then warm, and she went.

He was in the classroom on the third floor, behind the tapestry of the knight asleep on his horse, sitting on a desk with his feet on a chair, exactly as he had sat there in November. He got up when she came in. That was new.

“Moody kept you behind,” said Potter. “Ron saw.”

“Ron sees a great deal, for somebody who never looks at anything.”

“What did he want?”

“To know about Karkaroff,” said Lyra. “And my father. And the ship.”

All of it was true. The Sneakoscope would have stood still for every word. She did not tell him about the glass top, or the flask, or what she had said in that office with her face on fire; she found that she could not imagine telling him, not in any version, not even the one where she was very cool about it and he laughed. He would know, one day, perhaps. He was not going to know it from her, and certainly not tonight.

He was watching her. She let him.

“Potter,” she said. “Who is Barty Crouch’s son?”

He did not move. Then he did, all at once: he came round the desk, and stood in front of her, much too close, and his face had gone a way she had seen only once, on a staircase in the dark a long time ago, when he had been eleven and she had been frightened of him.

“Who told you that name?”

“Moody.”

“Stay away from Moody.”

“I shall stay,” said Lyra, “wherever I like. Who is he?”

He looked at her for a long moment. She watched him decide, as she had watched him decide on the floor of the Great Hall not to say he was sorry; and this time she watched him decide the other way, and close the door, and stand in front of it.

“He’s dead,” said Potter. “Moody told you. He died in Azkaban. He’s dead.”

He said it quite quietly, the way he had once said *it’s a dragon, Lyra*, on a bench in a tent; and she understood that he did not believe a word of it.
