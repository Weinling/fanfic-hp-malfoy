# Chapter 82: Where the House Was

The plant arrived at breakfast on the morning that Harry turned sixteen, and it bit Uncle Vernon.

It came by owl, a very tired one, in a pot with a sock over the top, with a card tied to the stem. *Happy Birthday Harry. It likes the dark. It bites. Sorry. N.* Uncle Vernon had picked it up to see what it was. He stood in the kitchen with his thumb in his mouth and his neck going slowly purple, saying a number of things about the post, while Aunt Petunia took the pot between two oven gloves and put it out on the back step. Dudley watched this over a grapefruit. He had got very large across the shoulders and a good deal quieter, and he had a boxer’s nose now. He looked at Harry, and at the card on the floor, and went on eating.

That was all the post there was.

He hadn’t expected anything. He went upstairs and told himself that for some time. Ron was at the Burrow, and hadn’t written. Lupin had sent a line. Hermione’s last owl had been a booklist. From a house in Wiltshire there had come, on the ninth of July, a sheet of notepaper with six words on it, *I trust you have found socks*, and since then nothing at all. He had his ring on. It had been a thin dull silver for a month. Every night at about ten he took it off, and put it on the other hand, and put it back.

He had the cauldron on a camping stove that he had found in the garage, on the floor of the second bedroom, between two towers of Dudley’s old video cassettes. It had gone a good deep gold. You had to turn it four times a day, and there was a bad morning in the fifth week when it had tried to climb out. The room smelt of something like hot pennies and treacle. It made Aunt Petunia’s face go narrow on the landing. He knelt down and looked into it, and it looked back at him, shining. Four and a half months.

He had a bit of a cake that he had bought himself, in the shop on Magnolia Road. He ate that. He did not do anything else. It was very hot, and they had the Test Match on, downstairs. He lay on the bed with his hands behind his head and his boots on, in the slow white heat of the afternoon, and looked at a crack in the ceiling, in the place where he had lain at ten and not been anybody. Sixteen. He’d had that one before. It had been in a good deal of a hurry.

*Good one, Prongs*.

He turned his face to the wall.

It got dark at about half past nine. Somewhere down the road a dog was barking; somebody from the Order was sitting in the hedge at number seven with a thermos, and being bitten. He had got up to turn the cauldron. He had the ladle in his left hand, and he reached across with the right to steady the pot.

It felt as if somebody had shut his finger in a door.

He dropped the ladle. He had his hand up in front of his face before he’d had time to think. He could see it, by the blue flame of a Muggle camping stove. It had gone the colour of a fresh cut.

He didn’t stop to think about anything. That was a thing he noticed, later on. He had thought he would think. He had his Cloak in one hand and the Firebolt out from behind the wardrobe in the other, and he’d got as far as the top of the stairs when he remembered the atlas. It was on the floor, somewhere under the cassettes. He went back and threw things, and it wasn’t there, and the ring had started to burn down into the bone. He could hear himself breathing. *If mine goes red, I’ll know.* He’d said that when he was fifteen.

“Oi,” said someone behind him.

Dudley was on the landing, in a vast pair of shorts, with a glass of milk. He filled most of it. He looked at the broomstick, while he swallowed. He looked at the door of the second bedroom, and the smoke, and at Harry’s hand, which Harry had put behind his back. It hadn’t been a thing they discussed, the two of them, since an alley in Little Whinging. Dudley didn’t discuss. He bent down, with a grunt, and reached behind the banisters, and he came up with something large and floppy that had a torn cover and a coffee ring on Hampshire.

“You left it in the bog,” said Dudley.

He held it out. Harry took it. Dudley stayed there for a moment, breathing through his boxer’s nose, and then he turned sideways, with some effort, and flattened himself against the wallpaper so that you could get past.

“Mum’s got the telly on,” he said. “Use the back.”

There wasn’t a breath of wind.

Seventy-four miles. He had done ninety-one in a blizzard at fifteen, with his eyelids freezing together, and it had taken him four hours and two falls. This was a warm thick still summer night, with half the stars of England out, and the Firebolt went through it like a hot wire. He crossed the Hog’s Back with the lights of Guildford going by under his left boot, and picked up the railway, and the long black line of the Downs. The atlas stayed inside his shirt. He had no need of it: the ring was pulling, hot and straight, a little south of west, like a hand round his finger, and all he had to do was lie along the broom and let it. *I’m coming*, he said, out loud, with the air ripping it away. *I’m coming. Hold on. I’m coming, I’ve got it, I’m nearly there.*

He looked at his watch at Andover. Forty minutes. He’d never been so fast. There wasn’t a person in England who could have got there faster. Nobody had held him up; he hadn’t had to go round anything, or wait for anyone, or stand in a corridor while somebody’s bloody Hedwig got her breath. He was early. Whatever it was, it had just started; his whole body knew it, it was singing in him. He had been late for everyone he’d ever cared about, in two lives. He had been late for his mother by ten years. He had been five seconds and four steps too late for a man in a waistcoat.

Not this time, thought Harry. His lip had split again, and he found he was grinning. Not *this* time.

The ring turned over.

He felt it go. It had been leading him on and on across a great dark pale-streaked country of chalk and barley; and all at once, over a long low hill with a clump of beeches on it, it stopped pulling forward and began to pull down. He stood the Firebolt on its nose. He dropped through two hundred feet of warm air with his wand out, and his heart going, and he pulled up over the shadows and came down, hard, running, and stopped.

It was a field.

He stood in it. Long grass, wet already and lately grazed, sloped away to a ragged hedge. He could smell sheep, and hear them; they had got up, a little way off, in a grey uneasy bunch, and were looking at him. There was a gate, tied up with blue string. Somebody had left a feed trough on its side. Over the hedge there was another field, just the same. A long way off, a Muggle car was going along a road with its lights on, and it went behind something, and came out.

He turned round, slowly, with his wand held out.

The ring had nearly got his finger off. It was pulling straight down at the ground between his boots. He looked. There were some thistles.

“Lyra,” said Harry.

It didn’t sound like much. A sheep coughed.

He went up the field. He went at a run, along the hedge, and across the top, and he tried what he had. He tried a Revealing Charm, and the one that Moody used on the cupboards. He tried the one from the other life, from the woods. He got out the Cloak, and put it away. He walked back and forward over that slope with his hand held out in front of him and the ring tugging at it like a dog on a lead, this way and that way. It got hotter, and it got hotter, and it took him to the same bit of ground. *Homenum revelio.* Nothing. A wand-light on the thistles. He went down on his knees in the wet grass. He put his left hand flat on the earth; he didn’t know why. It was just the earth. It was warm. Somewhere under the palm of his hand, or a hundred yards away, or in some other kind of direction that you couldn’t have shown on any page of the AA Road Atlas of Great Britain, somebody was—

He didn’t know what somebody was doing. It was going through his finger. There was something very wrong with his breathing.

*Hidden*, he thought. He knew what that was. He had grown up with what that was: he had the whole of his life to look at. It had been done to a house in Godric’s Hollow, and somebody had given it away. You could walk up and down in front of the door with an army, and you could put your face against the wall and you would never find it. One person could tell you. Just the one. You could be the fastest thing in England. You could come for her at a hundred miles an hour, with your wand out and your heart in your mouth, four and a half years’ worth of being too late all finished with for ever; and you could stand in a field.

“I’m here!” Harry shouted. “I’m *here!* I came! Lyra! I *came!*”

It went out over the hedge, and away. Every sheep in the field turned round, and ran.

He stayed on his knees, because he had nothing else to try. He put his forehead against the back of his own hand, in the dark, with the ring beating against his skin. He didn’t pray; he had never known how. He counted, which he was good at, and he got up to a very large number. At some time after one o’clock, when the Plough had gone a long way round, it stopped, not fading, but as a clock stops. He held up his hand, and it was a plain thin band of silver, with the starlight on it, and quite cold.

He couldn’t have told you why he went to the horse.

It wasn’t anywhere near; it was fourteen miles north-east of the field, by the atlas, and he went there, because you had to go somewhere and that was where she had been, on the only other night that this had ever happened. He came down on the ridge a little before three. There it lay on the dark side of the hill, long and thin and white, with its one round eye; it had been running for a good while, and it hadn’t got anywhere. He sat down in the short dry grass beside the Firebolt. It was the same place. There wasn’t any snow, and the wind had dropped. He put his hands in his pockets. He looked at it, and he left them there.

He didn’t think she would come. He sat and didn’t think it, for an hour.

The sky went a dull grey-green along the bottom, and birds had started up, down in the valley, one at a time. He had got rather cold. He heard it before he saw it: a faint high rushing, and a whisper in the grass. He stood up. He stood up so fast that he went dizzy, and there was a dark thing coming along the top of the ridge, low and rather slowly. It came down on the far side of the horse’s ear, and stayed where it was for a moment. Then someone got off it, with care, and stood the broom against nothing, and it stayed. She came along the turf towards him, with her cloak round her.

She walked very straight. He’d seen her walk like that down the middle of the Great Hall with a broken arm. She had a long grey dress on, with long sleeves, buttoned at the wrist, and it was the thirty-first of July; or it had been. She stopped about four yards away.

He couldn’t see her properly. It was too dark for that, and then the light came up a little. Her face had no blood in it at all. He’d seen wax that had more. It was white as a wall, as plaster, right to the edges of her lips, and all he could see of her eyes was how very large they were. She had done her hair. Every pin of it was in place.

He had opened his mouth. He had thought of something to say, on the ridge. He’d had all night to think of it.

“You’re early,” said Lyra.

It stopped him as if he had walked into a pane of glass.

He knew that voice. He had heard it across a table with a lace cloth, over a plate with a kitten on it. It was perfectly pleasant, and rather high, and it had no one in it. It didn’t seem to be addressed to him so much as to the air slightly above his left shoulder. She’d used that voice, once, to ask if he took sugar.

“It went red,” said Harry. His own voice came out as a croak. “It went— At half past nine. For *four hours*. I came, I was there in an hour, I was there. There’s nothing there! I was in a field—”

“Yes,” said Lyra. “I imagine that you were. There’s been a certain amount of building work.”

“There’s no *house*—”

“No.”

“Who did that? Is it them? Is it— What happened? What did they do to you? *Lyra*—”

“Many happy returns,” said Lyra. “I’ve brought you something. It’s very small. I wasn’t able to get to the shops.”

She put her right hand into the fold of her cloak and held something towards him, at the full length of her arm; and she stayed where she was, so that he had to come forward. He went across the grass with his eyes on her face. It was a little flat parcel in tissue paper, tied up with a bit of green thread, and he took it from the very ends of her fingers. They were shaking. He could see that now. All of her was, not much, you’d have had to know, in a fine fast tremor that ran through the hand and the arm and the long grey sleeve, like something with the current on. He had seen that in a chair, with strips of linen. He undid the thread, because it was what you did, with hands that didn’t feel like anything.

It was a sock, a very good one, in fine dark grey wool. There was just the one.

“You seemed to be short,” said Lyra.

He looked at it, and at her. A long way behind her, the sky had started to come up, a thin cold yellow, and the first of the light had got on to her cheek, where a small bright bead of sweat was running down from her hair, and she didn’t lift a hand to it. She stood on the chalk with her chin up, smiling very slightly, and she was going to fall down. He had known her for five years, and he had never seen anything so clear in his life. She was shaking all over, and it had been on this hill, in the snow, with her teeth going, that she had got him by the wrist and pulled his hand out of his pocket and put it on her; and that had been the end of the worst thing that had ever happened to either of them. It was there in his arm. It knew the way.

His hand had come out. He saw it go. It went past the sock, and across the little space between them, towards her left wrist.

“Hold it,” said Harry.

“No.”

She hadn’t raised her voice. She had moved: it was a very slight movement, and very quick. She had put her left arm behind her back. She stood with it there and looked at his hand, out in the air by itself, with the line and the three words across the back of it, as she might have looked at something on a plate. He had never been looked at like that. Not by her. Not on the worst day there had ever been.

It took him a moment. He saw it arrive. *You don’t take hold of me. Not my arm. Not my wrist. Until I tell you.*

He had forgotten. That was the thing. He had given his word, near enough. He’d had his hands in his pockets for six weeks, on a platform, on a rug, and he had thought he was getting rather good at it; and the very first time, the very first time that it had counted for anything, the thing had gone straight out on the end of his arm, without so much as a by-your-leave, and made for her.

He took it back. It seemed to weigh a good deal. He put it in his pocket, and the sock with it.

“Sorry,” he said.

She didn’t answer. Something had happened to the skin round her eyes, and for about a second, and no longer, somebody looked out of them, from a very long way down. She looked away. She turned her head, carefully, as if it had been filled up to the brim, and she looked at the horse.

“Is it them?” said Harry. He could hardly get it out. “Lyra. Is it them, have they got you— Is that where you are? Under that field? Are they *in* there? I’ll come. I’ll get Dumbledore. Tell me who’s got the— Tell me how to get in.”

She went on looking at the horse. It had come up out of the dark by now; you could see the long bones of it, cut into the hill. There was a muscle going in her cheek. He’d seen that, on this ridge. He knew what it meant, and to the Knut. He stood and waited, and she breathed in, through her nose, and her lips moved a little, as if she was reading something over to see it would do.

“You mustn’t come,” she said, in the pleasant voice. “You won’t find it.”

“You said *me*. You stood up in June and you said it. Them or me, and you said—”

“Yes,” said Lyra. “It’s in writing.”

He had no idea how long they stood there. The birds had got very loud. All down the valley a mist was lying along the river, with the tops of the willows coming out of it, and it was going to be another beautiful day.

She walked up to him. It was only about a yard. She came quite close, closer than she had been since a rug in June, and he stopped breathing. He kept his hands exactly where they were. He pushed them down, as far as they’d go, so that his shoulders hurt. She smelt of soap, and something else, under it, faint and sharp, like a burnt match. She stood there with her left arm behind her. She looked at his mouth, and the split in his lip. Then she put up her right hand and took hold of his collar, and she stood on her toes, and it cost her something; he heard her breath catch. He bent towards her before she had finished pulling.

Her lips were very cold. He felt them shake against his, felt the uneven pressure as she tried to stay high enough, and went lower with her. The split in his lip stung. He shut his eyes and kissed her back, holding the sock inside his pocket so tightly that his nails went into his palm.

It wasn’t long. Her fingers loosened at his collar, and for a few seconds he could feel her mouth answer his. Then her heels came down. Their mouths parted, and he opened his eyes, still bent towards her, with his hands in his pockets. He’d have stood there till he died.

She let go of the collar. She smoothed it down, once.

“That was the last one,” said Lyra.

He couldn’t help it. He had no breath at all. It was just what you said. You had been saying it for thirteen months, in a tower, and at a window, and on a hill, in the blackest dark there was, and it had never once failed.

“This year,” said Harry.

He waited.

She looked at him. She had got the light full on her now. She looked for what seemed a very long time, with those enormous eyes, and her lips were a little apart. He saw them start to move. He would have given the rest of his life. It was only two words. It was *It’s August*, it was anything, it was any of the things that she had ever said.

She didn’t say anything.

She turned round, and she walked back along the top of the hill, in the new sun, with her shadow going out long and thin on her right hand. She didn’t hurry. She walked as far as the broom, and stopped there, for a moment, with her back to him. He saw her shoulders go up and come down. She put one leg across, and took the handle in her right hand. The left she kept in against her body. She rose off the ridge very slowly, and turned south; and when the broom had steadied she let go of it, as she had let go of a good many brooms, and her right hand came across and closed round her own left arm, just above the wrist, and pressed. She flew like that, up into the light, until she was too small to see.

He stood on the hill with a sock in his pocket.

He had seen that before. He had seen it across a good many rooms, and in the other life he had seen it in a whole line of them, in black, as they got up from a long table. He’d watched a thin man in a dungeon do that, in the middle of a sentence. It didn’t mean anything. He told himself so. A person could have hurt her arm. He stood there for quite a long while, in the sun, with the larks going up, telling himself that.
