# Chapter 24: Replay

The ring went gold at a quarter past two on a Tuesday morning, and Lyra, who had certainly not been lying awake, sat up to look at it.

She had drawn the hangings round her bed at midnight, as she did every night that summer, and put it on in the dark, where nobody could see. It was not a thing she did for any particular reason. The slip of instructions from Zonko’s, which she had long ago learned by heart without meaning to, listed more colours than any reasonable person could want, and in July she had found out that most of them were real. Purple, *on guard*. Gold, *success; something accomplished*. Green, *safe*. Red she had not seen since February, and did not intend to see again.

It had been gold for about a minute. Then it went green, and stayed green, and Lyra lay back down on her pillow with the satisfied feeling of somebody who has read the last page of a book first.

She had no idea what Potter did with his holidays. She was not, in the least, curious. It was simply that a boy whose ring went purple at four in the morning and gold at a quarter past two was plainly not spending the summer weeding his aunt’s garden, and it was very rude of him not to say what he *was* doing, considering that she had not asked.

He wrote, sometimes. Not often; an owl going too often to the Manor would be noticed, and anything that came to her by the family owl went by way of her father’s study window first. Potter’s letters came by birds she had never seen before, a different one every time, and they were signed *D. Dursley*, which was so ugly a name that it could only belong to a real person. They said very little. They told her it was hot. They told her to look after herself. Once, at the bottom, there was a line she read twice and then put inside the cover of a book she did not intend to lend to anybody.

“He’s telling *me* to be careful,” said Lyra, to the canopy of her bed. The ring, at that moment, was purple.

There was one more thing on the slip, and it was the thing she minded most. The rings worked both ways. Whatever hers told her about Potter, his was telling Potter about her, at that very moment, in colours, wherever he was. So when she lay down at night she was careful to think about nothing in particular: her Arithmancy, or the new broom polish, or the Quidditch World Cup, which was in a fortnight and which her father had got them into the top box for. She was certainly not going to lie there thinking about anything that might come out blue.

She turned the ring round on her finger, and turned it back.

He would be at the World Cup. Everybody would be at the World Cup.

Lyra fell asleep looking at it, which was nobody’s business.

“Lyra! *Lyra*, look! It’s Viktor Krum!”

Draco came into the morning room at a run, without knocking, which he had been told about, and unrolled a poster across her Arithmancy before she could stop him. On it, a young man with heavy black eyebrows and a nose like a hawk’s was hurtling straight down at the ground on a broom, faster and faster, until it seemed impossible that he would not go into it head first; then at the last instant he pulled up, the player behind him ploughed into the turf, and the picture swung round to show Krum rising, unhurried, with a Snitch in his fist. Underneath, the words *WRONSKI FEINT* blazed up in red and faded, and the whole thing began again.

“I thought you supported Ireland,” said Lyra.

“Ireland’s Seeker is *boring*. Krum’s *eighteen*, Lyra. He’s still at school and he’s Seeker for the whole of Bulgaria.” Draco was ten that summer, and he said *the whole of Bulgaria* as though he had personally been consulted about it. “Look, he does it again in a minute—”

Lyra leaned in to look. Draco snatched the poster back against his chest.

“No. You can’t see. It’s mine.”

There was a time, not very long ago, when Lyra would simply have taken it off him. But he was ten now, and had found out about stubbornness, and on the subject of stubbornness Lyra knew she was up against the only person in the Manor with more of it than she had. So instead she reached under the table, into the bag she had been meaning to show him anyway, and brought out a pair of golden binoculars covered in little brass knobs and dials.

Draco’s eyes went perfectly round.

“What are *those*?”

“Omnioculars,” said Lyra. “Made specially for the Irish team, for their training. Slow motion. Replay. They’ll follow any player you like for the whole match, and keep it, so you can watch it again afterwards. There are about twelve pairs in the world.”

“Whose are they?”

“Mine. The way the poster’s yours.”

“Give me a go!”

“I don’t think I shall, you know,” said Lyra, and turned them gently from side to side so that the light ran up and down the brass. Draco pretended not to watch them and failed. “Unless you’d like to swap.”

“*No*.”

“Never mind. You can look at Krum doing the same dive over and over again. I’ll watch him doing it live.”

“That’s *cheating*.”

It took him nearly a minute, which was a long time for Draco, to fling the poster at her. He took the omnioculars with both hands, as if they might fly away, and went to the window at once to look at a peacock through them.

Lyra unrolled the poster and watched Krum come out of the dive again. He really was extraordinary. She had flown against Potter for two seasons and she had never seen anybody do that with a broom, or with that little visible effort, as though the ground had been a suggestion. There was something not quite right about the picture, all the same. It was the face, she decided: heavy and sullen, and rather a waste of a dive like that. If it had been a different face, somebody’s with untidy hair and glasses, say, it would have been a good deal more—

“You *fancy* him!” Draco had the omnioculars trained on her from the window, and was delighted. “You do! Girls are all the same. They see somebody good at Quidditch and they go all stupid.”

“I do not fancy Viktor Krum,” said Lyra, with great clarity. “Not even slightly.”

“Then you don’t want the poster.”

She considered. “No. I’ve seen it. You can have it back.”

“*Yes!*” Draco snatched it off the table before she could change her mind, and clutched both his treasures to his chest, and bolted. She heard him shouting from the hall that he was going to show the gentleman.

Lyra sat for a moment after he had gone. The gentleman in the east cottage still lived in the east cottage, then. She had not asked, all summer, and nobody had said.

Then she reached into the bag again and took out the other pair of omnioculars: the real ones, Irish team issue, with the gold leaf on the dials. The pair she had given Draco had cost ten Galleons in Diagon Alley, and he would never know the difference, and he was perfectly happy, and so was she.

“When,” she said to the empty room, “is that child going to grow up?”

The Malfoys did not queue.

The Ministry had connected a handful of private fireplaces to the stadium for the use of important persons, and the Minister for Magic had invited Lucius Malfoy to use one, so they stepped out of the Manor’s drawing-room grate and into a panelled anteroom somewhere under the stands without so much as a smut on their robes. Lyra had heard that other people had walked for miles over wet fields at dawn, with a Portkey that looked like an old boot. She was sorry for them, in a general way.

Cornelius Fudge was waiting at the top of the stairs, in a bowler hat and a state of great excitement. He shook her father’s hand for a long time, and kissed her mother’s, and then turned his beaming face on Lyra and Draco.

“And these are the children! At Hogwarts, aren’t you? Both of you?”

“Lyra is,” said her mother. “Draco goes next year.”

“Well, well.” Fudge winked at Lyra, enormously. “Lucky things. You’re in for something rather special this year, you know. Very special indeed. But I mustn’t say.”

Draco, who had decided that this was a deliberate insult to anybody who would not be at Hogwarts until next year, glared at the Minister for Magic with such concentrated loathing that Fudge spent the next three flights of stairs trying to win him round with talk of Bulgarian Chasers.

Lyra hardly heard any of it.

*Especially next year. If students come from other schools.* Potter had said it to her under a cloister in May, out of breath, with a ring in his hand, and she had been too angry at the time to ask what on earth he meant by it. She had put it away to be wondered about later. Now the Minister for Magic was winking at her about something special at Hogwarts that he mustn’t say, and he was the Minister, and it was his business to know. Potter was fourteen, and it was not.

She had no time to go on with that, because they had reached the top box.

It was a small, beautifully placed box at the very top of the stands, exactly halfway between the goals, with two rows of purple and gilt chairs; and the front row was full of Weasleys.

Lyra stopped in the doorway.

There were a great many of them, in a row, red-headed and shabby, and Granger at the end in a jumper, and in the middle of them all, with his elbows on the rail and a pair of ordinary brass omnioculars round his neck, was Potter.

She had brought the good omnioculars, the gold ones, for the express purpose of finding him somewhere in the cheap seats on the far side of the stadium. He was sitting one row in front of her.

“Arthur,” said Fudge, with great heartiness. “You’ll know Lucius, of course. He’s made a most generous donation to St Mungo’s. I asked him along myself.”

Mr Weasley and her father greeted each other with the expressions of two men who had each been handed something unpleasant to hold. Her father led his family past the Weasleys to their seats, and as Lyra went by, Potter looked round.

He nodded to her. It was a perfectly civil nod, the kind one gave to somebody from school whose name one could just about remember, and then he turned back to the pitch.

That was what they had agreed. Not in front of people. Not anywhere anybody could see. Lyra had told him under the cloister in May exactly what she thought of the arrangement, and then walked off with his ring on her finger, which came to the same thing as agreeing. It was quite sensible, and she would have been extremely annoyed if he had done anything else.

She turned her head away sharply and went and sat down.

It was still a perfectly horrible nod.

Beside her, her mother arranged her robes, and looked at Lyra, and then, for a little longer, at the back of the row in front.
