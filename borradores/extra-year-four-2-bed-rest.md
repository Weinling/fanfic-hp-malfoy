# Year Four, Part 2: Bed Rest

Lyra Malfoy had a cold, and nobody was to know.

Harry had this from Crabbe, on a wet Tuesday in the second week of May. She stopped beside him in the corridor outside Charms. She looked at a point on the wall about a foot to the left of his ear.

“She’s in the hospital wing,” said Crabbe. “She says she’s gone home for a family matter. She says if you come up there she’ll have you killed.”

She went on to Charms. Harry stood there for a moment. Then he went up to Gryffindor Tower and got his cloak.

It was a long room, and it had rained so hard all morning that somebody had lit the lamps. There was only one bed with the curtains drawn. He stopped outside them, under the cloak. From inside came a thin high whistling sound, on and off, like a very small kettle. There was a pause. Then somebody sneezed. It was an enormous, wet, helpless sneeze, it went on longer than seemed likely, and it was followed by a voice he had some difficulty in recognising.

“*Bloody* thing.”

He put his head round the curtain.

She was sitting up against three pillows with a shawl round her. It was the first time he had seen her without a single pin in her hair. It had gone flat on one side. Her nose was the colour of a boiled ham, and her eyes were pink, and from each of her ears, in a slow and steady jet, there came a thin white plume of steam.

Harry took off the cloak.

For some time neither of them said anything. It went on being one of the best moments of his life.

“Ged oud,” said Malfoy. All the consonants had been filed off. The left ear whistled. “I bean it, Botter. I’ll curse you. Bobfrey gave be sobething, it’s the Bepper-Up. It’s dot funny.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You’re doing it with your *face!*”

“How long have you been steaming?”

“Dot one word. Dot to adybody. If you tell Weasley I’ll have your *eyes*.” She groped about on the blanket, without taking her gaze off him, and found a handkerchief, and looked at it, and put it down again with a shudder. “I’ve dever been ill. Malfoys don’t get colds. It’s a Buggle thing. I got it from Goyle, and Goyle got it from a *pasty*.”

He pulled up the chair. It was hard and rather low, and it put his chin about level with the edge of the mattress. He had a *Daily Prophet* in his pocket from breakfast. He unfolded it. She looked at it with hatred. She had been there for a day and a half, he could see, with nothing to read but the instructions on a bottle, and she had already done the bottle.

“I could read you something,” said Harry.

“You read like a boy walking on a frozen pond.”

“‘Ministry Confirms New Rules on Cauldron Thickness.’”

There was a silence. The left ear gave a small interested puff.

“Go on,” said Malfoy.

He went on. He did the Cauldron Thickness, and the Gobstones results. He read her a long complaint from a witch in Ottery St Catchpole about the Knight Bus. He got *Bagnold* wrong and she corrected him. He got it wrong again, and she told him how it was pronounced in the part of Wiltshire where they had known the family. He did the births and deaths in Professor Binns’s voice. She blew her nose. She had got down a little way in the pillows, and at some point her hand had come out from under the shawl. It lay on the blanket near the edge, not doing anything. He put his on top of it, as he had in the Three Broomsticks, and she looked at that and made no remark. It was very hot and rather damp. He did not think he had ever in his life held anything so disgusting.

The door at the far end opened. There were footsteps, brisk and rubber-soled, and Madam Pomfrey came down the ward with a tray. There was no time to do anything about it. She put back the curtain. She looked at the patient. She looked at the chair, at the boy in it with his mouth open and a newspaper on his knee, and at the blanket.

Nobody breathed.

“I see the bed is empty,” said Madam Pomfrey, to the air above his head. “I shall go on seeing that for about an hour. Miss Malfoy, you’ll drink this.”

She went away. Malfoy drank it. Her eyes watered. The steam came out in a rush, from both sides, and her hair lifted slightly at the temples.

“There’s a thing here about Puddlemere,” said Harry, when he could trust himself.

“Is it long?”

“Quite long.”

“Good,” she said, and shut her eyes.

Somebody coughed, behind him. He turned round. Greer Goyle had come in with a pile of Arithmancy notes and a plate. She looked at him, sitting there in the lamplight with the sports page. Her big soft face did not change. She walked round the end of the bed and set the plate on his knee, on top of Puddlemere. It was a slice of fruit cake about the size of a brick.

“You’ll want to keep your strength up,” said Goyle. “She’s horrible when she’s ill.”

She left the notes on the locker, and went away again.

He ate the cake. He read the piece about Puddlemere, in a low voice, right through to the league table. Then he read the weather. It rained on the window. He had got as far as the small advertisements when he noticed that nobody had corrected him for some time. He looked up. Her mouth had come open. Her head had gone over sideways on the pillow, with her flat hair across her cheek and her red nose in the air, and she was snoring, very slightly, on the in-breath. Her hand was still under his. From her left ear a thin white thread of steam went on rising, gently, towards the ceiling.

He ought to go. He had Potions at two.

He sat there. There was nothing in the world that needed doing. He slid down in the chair until his head was against the edge of the mattress, and he looked at the small wisp of steam for a while, going up, and coming apart.

“*Bagnold*,” he said, quietly, the wrong way, just to see.

She did not stir.

He read the small advertisements to himself. Somebody in Godalming had a Nimbus for sale.
