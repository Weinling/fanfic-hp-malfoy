# Chapter 6: Right Hand

“Lyra! Your *arm*! Let me see!”

Panther Parkinson had been waiting at the door of the Potions dungeon, and he fell into step beside her with the shining face of someone who has been waiting all morning to be useful. Lyra did not slow down. She had come down the dungeon steps with her right arm strapped across her chest in a great white sling, and while several of the Slytherins looked at it as if it were a medal, she found it ugly, awkward and humiliating, and she had been in a vile temper since breakfast. Crabbe and Goyle, who knew the signs, had not said a word to her all morning.

“No,” she said.

“Merlin! That overgrown pigeon! If it’s scarred you, I’ll—I’ll go down there tonight and pluck it. Bare. I mean it!”

“Parkinson.”

She said it quite quietly. He stopped.

“Madam Pomfrey has given me a salve, and there will be no scar, and nobody is plucking anything. My father has written to the Board, the Board has written to the Committee for the Disposal of Dangerous Creatures, and the Committee will, in due course, dispose of it.” She set down her bag with her left hand. “That is the seventh time I have said all that since breakfast. I have a strong feeling it should be the last.”

Parkinson went the colour of the Gryffindor banners and subsided, and she sat down.

Across the dungeon, the Gryffindors had their heads together. One of them detached herself from the group and came straight across the room towards the Slytherin side, and Lyra, seeing that it was Potter’s pet Mudblood, let out a long breath through her nose.

“And what can I do for you, Granger?”

“Write to your father. Tell him to drop it.” Granger was pink to the ears and holding her Potions book against her chest as though she meant to hit someone with it. “You insulted it. Hagrid stood there and *told* us not to, and you did it anyway, to its face. What did you think was going to happen?”

“I *spoke* to it, Granger.” Lyra crossed her ankles. “If a creature can take your arm off for being spoken to, the fault is not in the speaking.”

Lyra had been brought up to believe that pride was a thing wizards were entitled to and beasts were not, and she saw no reason to revise the arrangement on account of one hippogriff.

She took out her quill and examined the nib, which was how her mother dismissed tradesmen. “Take it up with your friend Potter. Dumbledore’s very fond of him. I’m sure there’s a string he can pull.”

“*Harry*—” Something shook in Granger’s voice. “Harry says if they sentence it, they sentence it. He *shrugged*. Over a cut that’s already healing! How can he say a thing like that?”

Lyra had expected to enjoy the morning very little, and found that she was enjoying it a great deal. The sling, the ache, Parkinson: all of it went somewhere else, and what was left was a small bright warmth just under the breastbone that she did not intend to show Granger for anything. She looked at her nib.

“Then Potter is wiser than his friends,” she said. “I shall write it down. It may never happen again.”

“It’s a *death sentence*!”

“Yes. That is what one gets for trying to kill a Malfoy. You might mention it to the others.”

It was a strange thing. Five minutes ago she had wanted the creature’s head on a plate. Now, for no reason she could put her finger on, she found she could picture it alive, somewhere a long way off, eating whatever hippogriffs ate, and the picture did not trouble her at all.

“Morning, all! Double Potions! Nobody look so pleased!”

Potter came through the door the way he always came through doors, as though somebody had announced him, and the dungeon tilted towards him the way rooms always did, as if he were the low end of the floor. Lyra had long ago been forced to admit that he could do it, and that he did it shamelessly. He came across to where Lyra and Granger were facing each other, making quite enough noise for three people.

“Hermione? What’s up?”

Granger gave him one look of pure, dry fury, and stalked back to the Gryffindor side of the room. Lyra felt her morning improve still further.

Potter rubbed the back of his neck. “Still not speaking to me? Ron?”

“Same as yesterday, mate,” said Weasley, from across the room. He looked from Potter to Granger and back, and then dragged a hand down his face. “Look. I know Hermione goes soft over anything with fur, and I know what that cat of hers has got planned for Scabbers. But Hagrid’s been crying into his boots since Friday. You could’ve said *something*.”

“I’m doing something, Ron.”

“Yeah, well, it doesn’t look like—” Weasley glanced at Lyra and then back at Potter, and something in his face settled. “Oh. *Right*. Fine. I’ll go and sit with her, then.”

“Cheers, mate.”

Weasley went off after Granger, and Potter was left standing in the middle of the dungeon on his own, looking round with tragic dignity.

“Abandoned,” he said. “And Snape’ll be in any second, and if I’m on my feet when he walks in he’ll have me for loitering.”

“Loitering,” said Snape’s voice, from the doorway, like cold air out of a cellar, “is ten points, Potter. Shall we begin there?”

Potter turned round. “Professor! We were just talking about you.”

“I have no doubt. Five more for the talking.”

“Fifteen? I’ve barely got through the door.”

“Twenty,” said Snape, “for the arithmetic. And if you are not on a stool by the time I reach my desk, it will be fifty.”

Potter sat down very quickly indeed. Twenty points in under a minute was, for Potter and Snape, roughly the equivalent of a polite good morning, and neither House so much as looked up.

The only unusual thing was the seat he had dropped into.

“Oops,” said Potter. “Look where I ended up. Hello, neighbour.”

It was the stool on Lyra’s right. She looked at him sidelong, very briefly, and said nothing at all.

They were making a Shrinking Solution. Lyra set her cauldron on its stand one-handed and then sat looking at the instructions on the board, considering. The daisy roots had to be cut into even slices and the shrivelfig peeled, and neither could be done with one hand by anybody who was not an octopus.

She raised her head. “Professor,” she said, and lifted the sling an inch, which said the rest.

A Gryffindor with a sling would have been told to brew with his teeth. Lyra had never been told anything of the kind in Snape’s classroom, in two years; she did not chatter and she did not ruin potions, and she had long since decided that being favoured was simply what being good looked like from the cheap seats.

Snape’s eyes swept the room and landed on the Gryffindor side. “Weasley. You will prepare Miss Malfoy’s ingredients as well as your own, and if I find a single—”

“Sir?”

It was a lazy, cheerful voice, and it cut him off in the middle of the sentence. Potter had lifted one hand and was waggling it.

“Save Ron the walk. I’m closer.”

He had her daisy roots across the bench before anybody could stop him and was slicing them, two sets at once, with the ease of a boy shelling peas. Snape’s lip curled, and he went to find somebody else to be disappointed in.

Lyra had always maintained, loudly, that Potter’s marks were Granger’s doing. It was harder to maintain at a range of eighteen inches.

The truth, which she had spent two years not looking at, was that Potter was good at Potions, and had made himself good at it to spite the one man in the castle who wanted him to fail. By Easter of first year Snape could no longer find anything to fault in his work. It had not made Snape like him. It had only made him furious in a different way.

Between his own steps he fed hers across the bench: the roots in neat coins, and then the shrivelfigs, peeled, in a row, like a hand of cards dealt face up.

“I’d have done them better,” said Lyra.

“Course you would. Left-handed and everything.”

She tipped the figs into her cauldron with her clumsy left hand and let the remark go past her.

The rat spleen and the leech juice were waiting for her when she looked up, measured and in order. She checked them against the book, because she was not about to take Potter’s word for anything, found nothing wrong, and resented it. In went the spleen. She took up the leech juice and squeezed.

Potter’s hand came over hers on the bottle, briefly, and stopped it.

She pulled away so violently she nearly upset the cauldron. Potter only tapped the book with one finger.

“Two drops. Not ten.”

“I can *count*, Potter. It’s my left hand that can’t.”

She shook the drops off her glove with great energy and went on with the potion. Then there was nothing left but to let it simmer, and she found herself frowning at her gloved left hand, wondering how one was supposed to take off a dragon-hide glove with no other hand to do it, when Potter, without looking up from his own cauldron, reached across and pinched the very tip of one glove finger.

All she had to do was draw her hand back. The glove came off like a skin. He wiped the leech juice from it, turned it the right way out, and handed it back.

It was, she had to admit, extremely good service. Her mother’s maid could not have done it better. Lyra let herself enjoy that for exactly as long as it took to remember who was doing it.

She took the glove. “If this is about the hippogriff,” she said, “you’re wasting your peeling.”

“What hippogriff?”

“Don’t. The Committee will sit, and it will be condemned, and that is all there is to it. You can fetch and carry for me until Christmas, and it won’t buy you a feather.”

It was all perfectly obvious. The sudden attentions, the open helpfulness, the cutting and the peeling and the gloves: every bit of it was for the hippogriff. Potter wanted her to call off the sentence, and this was his way of asking without having to ask.

She watched him as she said it. Though if he were to *ask*—properly, in words, with his pride on the bench between them—she supposed she might be open to an offer. She waited for it with some pleasure.

Potter looked at her as though she had started speaking Mermish.

“Hagrid’d be over the moon if you let it off. So would I, probably. But if they don’t?” He shrugged. “It had your arm open to the bone. People have got Azkaban for less.”

“What?”

That was not what he had said in the hospital wing. In the hospital wing, with her blood on his shirt, executing it had been “a bit much”. Lyra noticed the change, and noticed too that she did not much want to examine it, because whatever he meant by it, hearing it was pleasant. If he had meant it to reach her, he had sent it by way of Granger, who had not been in on the joke and was plainly hurt by it. Lyra added that to the growing list of things about Potter that ought to worry her more than they did. It was not like hearing it from Granger. When Granger had said it, the feeling had been bright and cool, like a window opening. This was closer and warmer and a little harder to breathe in.

Lyra gave a small cough. “Then what,” she said, and indicated the row of peeled figs, “is all *this*?”

“Um.” Potter thought about it. “Hobby?”

Lyra looked at him, and then at her cauldron, and decided the cauldron was better company.

Potter, meanwhile, had glanced round the room. While Snape was busy at the far end he picked up a sprig of something from his own bench, leaned across the aisle behind Neville Longbottom, and dropped it neatly into Longbottom’s cauldron. Then he caught Lyra watching him, and winked.

Lyra assumed, naturally, that he was sabotaging his own Housemate for the fun of it, and was fairly impressed. It was only at the end of the lesson, when Snape went down the Gryffindor bench with a face like thunder and stopped at Longbottom’s cauldron and could find nothing whatsoever to say about it, that she began to wonder. Longbottom was staring into his own potion as if somebody had swapped it.

She could not make Potter out at all. She had just watched him do something decent, quietly, for nobody’s benefit, with no audience but her, and she did not know where to put it. He looked, most of the time, as though he had nothing in his head but mischief, and she had very nearly started to believe it. So who had that been, in the empty classroom in May, with the diary on the desk and a voice like a shut door? She had told Lupin she had never been afraid of him. It had been true, more or less, at the time. And why, on Friday, had he been on his knees beside her before anybody else in the paddock had so much as moved? As if he had been watching for it. As if he had known.

It did not stop with Potions.

“Malfoy! I’ll take your bag!”

He was waiting outside Transfiguration the next morning, and outside Charms after lunch, and at the foot of the dungeon stairs the morning after that, and every time he took her bag off her shoulder before she could stop him and carried it to wherever she was going, chatting all the way about nothing at all, with an air of tremendous good cheer. Lyra tried walking faster. Potter was on the Quidditch team and walked faster still. She tried going round by the back stairs. He was waiting at the top of them.

On Thursday he started on her teeth. He had heard, he announced on the stairs up to the Charms corridor, loudly enough for most of the stairs to hear, that you were meant to brush for three whole minutes after every single meal. Granger’s mum and dad were dentists and swore by it. And since she obviously couldn’t manage it left-handed, he would be very happy to do it for her. A Hufflepuff fourth-year laughed so hard she missed a step.

Lyra did not answer him. She went down to the common room, sat in the chair nearest the fire with her bandaged arm in her lap, and thought about it the way she would have thought about a Quidditch match.

Potter wanted something. He did not do anything for nothing, whatever he said about hobbies. And he had chosen a weapon that only worked for as long as she let it. Every one of his performances depended on her sitting there with one useless arm, unable to do a single thing for herself, while the whole school watched him do it for her. She could go on being followed round the castle for another week and a half, until Madam Pomfrey signed her off, and be laughed at for every day of it. Or she could take the weapon away.

That night, in the dormitory, she slipped the sling off and tried the arm. It ached when she lifted anything heavier than a quill, and the salve had a few days’ more work to do. It would serve. If she was honest, which she was only ever in the dark, the sling had not been strictly necessary for some days. She had kept it on because people were nicer to a girl in a sling, and because it was the one visible mark the thing had left on her. Potter had probably noticed that too.

On Saturday, at dinner, Potter crossed the Great Hall from the Gryffindor table to the Slytherin one, which no Gryffindor had done in living memory, and the whole table went silent. He had brought his own knife and fork.

“Chops,” he said, and sat down opposite her. “Can’t do chops one-handed. Nobody can. Pass it here.”

Lyra looked at him. Then, in no particular hurry, she began to unwind the bandage from her right arm, round and round, until it lay coiled on the table beside her goblet and the arm underneath was smooth and pale and entirely unmarked. She picked up her own knife in her right hand, cut a small, neat piece of lamb, and ate it.

“That will be all, Potter,” she said, in precisely the voice her mother used for a footman who had lingered. “You may go.”

The Slytherin table erupted. Someone at the far end actually applauded. At the staff table, Professor Snape had set down his goblet and was regarding the scene with the face of a man who has found a Gryffindor in his soup. Half the Ravenclaws were laughing too, and Potter, sitting there with a knife and fork and nothing to use them on, looked for one glorious second as though he had genuinely not seen it coming.

Then he grinned, laid the cutlery down neatly beside her plate, and stood.

“Shame,” he said. “I’d bought a toothbrush.”

Lyra could not tell, then or for a long time afterwards, whether he meant it.

That evening in the common room, Marcus Flint came and threw himself into the armchair opposite hers with the expression of a man who has just watched a Quaffle go through his own hoops.

“What did you go and do that for?”

“Do what?”

“Take the bandage off. In front of the whole school.” He jabbed a thick finger at her arm. “I only thought of it at lunch. I was going to see Hooch tomorrow and get our match against Gryffindor swapped. Our Seeker’s injured, can’t possibly play, so Gryffindor plays Hufflepuff first instead. They’ve spent a month getting ready for us, and they’ve got nothing ready for Diggory. And now the whole Great Hall’s watched you carve up your dinner with your right hand.”

Lyra looked at him.

She thought about Potter outside Transfiguration, and Potter on the Charms stairs, and Potter at the Slytherin table with his knife and fork and that one glorious second of surprise. She had been so pleased with that second. She had taken his weapon off him in front of the whole school. And in doing so she had taken the bandage off, in front of the whole school, exactly as if somebody had arranged it.

It was absurd. Flint had only thought of it at lunch; he had said so. Nobody could have known what Flint was going to do before Flint did.

And yet.

If Potter had simply asked, she thought, with a sudden, fierce satisfaction, she could have saved him a great deal of trouble. She had no intention of hiding behind a sling to put off a match with him. She did not want the match put back. She wanted to play it, and she wanted to win it, properly, in front of everybody, with both arms.

“I don’t need a postponement, Marcus,” she said. “I need a Snitch.”

Flint opened his mouth, looked at her face, and shut it again.

Her father came up to the school at the end of the month, with two members of the Board of Governors and a thin, damp-looking wizard from the Committee for the Disposal of Dangerous Creatures, to settle the matter of the hippogriff. He sent for her before he went anywhere else.

Lyra found him in an empty classroom off the entrance hall, the Committee man hovering by the door. Her father came across the room in three strides, took her by both shoulders and looked her up and down as though he expected to find pieces missing.

“Lyra. My treasure. Your arm—”

“Is perfectly all right.” She held it up and turned it in the light to show him. “Not a mark. Madam Pomfrey is very good.”

“Thank Merlin.” He let out a long breath, and then his face hardened into the expression he wore for the Ministry. “Well, you needn’t worry any further. The Committee is quite of my mind. That animal will not see Christmas. And as for that oaf Hagrid…”

“Don’t.”

Her father stopped.

Lyra had been thinking about this for three weeks, ever since the night in the hospital wing when she had written to him in a cold fury with her left hand, and every day since she had liked the letter less. She had sworn, in July, sitting at her desk with Pobble’s fire roaring behind her, that she would keep her father quiet and out of sight and well away from anything that might bring the wrong kind of eyes onto the family. And then, with her very first chance, she had gone and handed him a war. Lucius Malfoy against Hagrid. Lucius Malfoy against Dumbledore, again, in the pages of the *Prophet*, three months after they had thrown him off the Board. It was the stupidest thing she had done all year, and she had done it because her arm hurt.

That was one reason. She was perfectly aware there was another, and it was standing at the bottom of the dungeon stairs every morning with her bag over its shoulder, and she declined to look at it.

“No executions,” she said. “I don’t want months of it in the *Prophet*: the Malfoy girl and the hippogriff, will it live, won’t it. I’m tired of it already. Put it somewhere I shall never have to see it again.”

“Lyra.” Her father’s voice cooled by several degrees. “That creature drew your blood in front of two Houses. Families like ours do not let such things pass. I believe I have explained this to you before.”

“You have,” said Lyra. “You told me we can’t afford to be kind.” She folded her hands in her lap. “But we can’t afford to be in the *Prophet* either, Father. Not this year. They’ve only just put you off the Board. If you go after Hagrid now, it’s you against Dumbledore again, on the front page, and everyone who ever wondered about the Chamber starts wondering out loud. Over a hippogriff.” She paused. “And if they kill it, it’s the first thing anybody will remember about me for the next five years. The Malfoy girl who had the gamekeeper’s pet put down because it scratched her. I’d much rather they remembered that I was gracious about it.”

Her father said nothing at all for some time.

“The animal, then,” he said at last. “But Hagrid will be formally reprimanded by the Board. In writing, on his record. I will not have it said that a Malfoy was injured in a lesson and nobody answered for it.”

Lyra considered this, and decided it was cheap at the price. A man who has been talked out of most of what he came for ought to be allowed to win something.

“That seems fair,” she said.

Her father studied her face as if it were a contract he had signed without reading quite carefully enough.

“I hear,” he said at last, slowly, “that it was the Potter boy who carried you up to the hospital wing.”

She had not known he knew. She should have. Somebody always wrote to her father.

“Somebody had to,” said Lyra. “Hagrid was busy with his chicken.”

Her father’s face did something complicated and then, by an effort of will, did nothing at all. He kissed her forehead and went off down the corridor with the Board and the Committee man in tow, and Lyra, standing at the classroom door, heard him remark to the ceiling, “Thirteen is a great deal of trouble.” It sounded like a joke. Lyra, who had sworn in July to keep her father calm and incurious, did not think it was one.

Because the injured party asked it, the Committee for the Disposal of Dangerous Creatures did not sentence Buckbeak to death. It ordered him confined instead: removed from lessons, kept apart from the other hippogriffs in a paddock behind Hagrid’s hut, and never again to be ridden by anyone. Hagrid received a letter of reprimand from the Board of Governors, which he was said to have read twice, blown his nose on, and put away in a drawer. It was a great deal less than she had asked for in September, and she found, to her surprise, that she liked it better.

She watched her father leave from the top of the entrance steps that afternoon, a tall figure in black crossing the grounds towards the gates with his cane flashing in the thin autumn sun.

She was not the only one watching. Potter was standing at the foot of the marble staircase with his hands in his pockets, looking out through the open doors at her father’s back, and he was not smiling. His face had gone very still and very cold. Then he heard her on the steps, and turned, and became himself again so quickly that she might have imagined it.

“Heard about Buckbeak,” he said.

“I can’t think what you mean.”

“Hagrid told me. He’s been crying since lunch. The good kind.” Potter looked at her as though she were a puzzle that had just come out differently from the way he had worked it. “I honestly didn’t think you’d do it.”

It was the second time in a fortnight that she had seen Potter caught off guard, and Lyra found that she liked it a great deal more than she ought to.

“Don’t flatter yourself,” she said. “It had nothing to do with you.”

“Obviously,” said Potter. “Oh, and Malfoy? He’s going to send you a rock cake. Don’t eat it.”

She went down to the dungeons feeling, for once, that she had come out of a conversation with Potter ahead. It was only later, lying awake in the dormitory, that she remembered the look on his face before he had seen her. She knew perfectly well what her father had done last year, and to whom. She had simply never seen, until today, what Potter thought of it.
