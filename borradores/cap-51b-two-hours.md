# Chapter: Two Hours

There was a smell of baking in the passage behind the kitchens, and it was the only thing about the evening that was doing him any good.

Harry sat on the floor with his back to the wall and his knees drawn up. He had been there since seven o’clock. It was a low stone corridor with a barrel-shaped roof, lit by two torches, and if you went along it past the bowl of fruit you came to a door of old black oak with a brass plate worn too smooth to read. Things happened behind that door. There was a thud, every so often, and somebody saying “Again,” and now and then a flash went under it and lit up the toes of his shoes.

He had given away a thousand Galleons that afternoon. He had seen it coming, the way you see a Bludger, and he had walked down the marble staircase with the bag in his hand, in front of the school. He had a Galleon in his turn-up. It had rolled there. He had not been able to bring himself to take it out.

It had been a stupid thing to do. Hermione had said so, at dinner, in two words, passing him the salt. It had not been for Diggory, whatever he told people. He wanted to get through that door, because there was a very small boy in Slytherin with a piece of paper against his chest and a corridor on the seventh floor in his head, and nobody was going to ask Harry Potter what he wanted with a first-year. He needed a room to put him in. He had looked for the way to do that, and that had been the way. So he had used the money, and the Tournament, and what he had done to Cedric in a hedge. It was very like him. He had thought of that on the stairs.

And she’d told on Hopkins, and he had put her against a wall. He had that as well. He’d had it for four days.

“Is it a queue?” said somebody.

Neville was standing over him with a pot. He looked along the passage at the black door, and down at Harry, with his round mild anxious face.

“No,” said Harry. “I’m just sitting here.”

“Oh,” said Neville.

He thought about that. Then he turned round, lowered himself carefully down the wall, and sat, with his legs out in front of him and the pot on his knee. The grey thing in it had got a good deal larger since September, and it had put out a sort of knob. Neither of them said anything. Neville told the plant that it was Tuesday, in a low voice. It was the most restful thing that had happened to Harry for a fortnight.

“What are you sitting here for?” said Harry, after a while.

Neville went pink. “I’ve been wanting to ask,” he said. “About that. In there. Only they’re all big, and they all know what they’re doing, and I thought if I just stayed about for a bit, somebody might come out, and I could—” He looked at the plant. “I’ve been coming down since Friday. I don’t usually get very far along.”

“Since Friday?”

“Since the sausage,” said Neville.

The door opened. It opened about a foot, and a good deal of noise came out, and a long thin boy, walking rather carefully, who shut it behind him with his left hand. He had a towel round his neck. He saw the two of them on the floor, and stopped. His face went slowly red.

“Sorry,” said Wayne Hopkins. “Am I in the way?”

Nobody had asked him to be sorry. Harry looked at his right hand. It was hanging at his side, in a clean bandage, and the fingers were curled in a way that fingers don’t. There was a smell of murtlap. He thought of a small jar, a quarter full, and he thought of somebody’s finger, pushing it across a table an inch at a time. He got his own right hand into his pocket.

“No,” said Harry.

“Only I’m supposed to go and sit down. Diggory says. I keep dropping my wand.” He stood there with his long mild spotty face, looking from one of them to the other, as though he thought one of them might like to tell him something. He seemed to be trying to think of a civil remark. “That’s a nice plant,” he said.

Neville looked up at him as if he had been handed the Order of Merlin.

There was a heavy, unhurried tread in the passage. It was coming from the direction of the fruit bowl. Something large and round came round the bend, in Slytherin robes with the sleeves pushed up, and flour to the elbows. It was holding a covered plate out in front of it with both hands, and it stopped. Harry had seen a good deal of Greer Goyle over the years, in the way that you see a sideboard. She looked at him on the floor. She looked at Neville. Her big soft face had no expression on it to speak of.

She walked past them both and stopped in front of Hopkins, and held out the plate.

“It’s fish pie,” said Goyle. “You don’t need a knife.”

He looked at it. All the colour that had come into his face went out, and came in again. He took it, in his left hand, and it tipped, and she put out one floury finger and steadied it.

“I’m not—” said Hopkins. “You don’t have to— Sorry, I don’t—”

“I know,” said Goyle.

She took her finger away. It seemed to be all she had come to say. She turned round, and on the way back she stopped, and looked down at Harry from a considerable height, with her slow, thorough, unsurprised eyes. She had brought a slice of cake once, to a chair by a bed, and put it on his knee. He had a good memory for that kind of thing.

“You’ll want to keep your strength up,” said Goyle.

She went away along the passage. They listened to her go.

It was just after nine when the door opened again, wide this time, and people started to come out in twos and threes, not talking much. They were mostly large. They stepped over his feet. Fred said, “Evening, Croesus,” and George shook his head, more in sorrow. Cho Chang went by, and did not look at him. Then there was nobody; and the torchlight fell in through the doorway across a long strip of worn boards, and Cedric Diggory was standing on it, rolling up a mat.

He finished. He put the mat against the wall. He came to the door and looked down.

“Two hours,” said Diggory.

“About that.”

“I can count.” He had his wand behind his ear and his sleeves rolled, and there was sweat on the white line in his hair. He looked rather tired, and he looked at Harry with the same pleasant, well-kept face that he had been looking at him with since June; only it seemed to Harry that somebody had been sitting up late behind it. “What was that this afternoon, Potter?”

“It’s yours. I told you.”

“I know what you told me. I’m asking what it was.”

He had an answer for that. It was short and rather good. He looked at it. There was a smell of fish pie, and he had been on a stone floor for two hours, and he was so tired of being good at this.

“I don’t know,” said Harry. “I can’t give you the other thing.”

For some time Diggory didn’t say anything. Neville had stopped breathing. He was holding his pot very still, in the hope of not being noticed.

“I said sixth years and seventh,” said Diggory. “In September. I told myself it was on account of the O.W.L.s.” He took the wand from behind his ear, and looked at it. “I cut up a boy’s sausage on Friday. I’ve known him since he was eleven; he’s never given anybody a moment’s trouble in his life. I didn’t know. He was in that office for three nights, and he’s in my House, and I’d got a list, with a nice clean line under the fifth year, so there’d be nobody in the room that I’d have to teach to hold a wand.” He didn’t raise his voice. “So I’ve stopped doing that. Anybody who’s been in her office. Anybody who asks. Longbottom, are you asking?”

Neville opened his mouth. A small sound came out.

“Tuesday and Thursday. Bring a clean shirt. Leave the plant.” He looked back at Harry. “That’s most of them. Not you.”

“I know.”

“I’ve got a room full of people who are going to meet the Dark Lord’s friends one of these days, and I can get them as far as a Shield. I can’t do a Patronus. I’ve tried. I get a sort of fog. Granger says you’ve got one with a tail.” His jaw moved. “So you’ll come in on Thursday and you’ll teach that, and you’ll stand at the front, where I can see you. If you turn your back on anybody in this room, Potter, for any reason, I’ll know. And I’ll have the Galleon that’s in your turn-up. It’s the school’s.”

Harry looked down. He looked up. There was nothing on Diggory’s face at all, not a thing; it was, he thought, perhaps the best joke that anybody had ever made at his expense.

“Right,” said Harry.

“I’ll go in front when we leave, as well,” said Diggory, and he went back and picked up his mat.

It took him until Wednesday to get Draco alone.

He got him after Charms, in the corridor by the suit of armour with the dent, while Urquhart’s little brother had gone back for a shoe. He had rather been hoping that it would be difficult. It was a very small boy, with a bow tie and a large bag and a tooth missing, who looked up and saw who it was. His whole face went perfectly white, and then it went perfectly red. He had been told about Harry Potter. He’d had it from his father and his sister; you could see it all going on behind his eyes. And then, as Harry stood there, none of that seemed to weigh anything. He just looked at the scar. Nobody had looked at it like that since a boy with a camera.

“There’s a club,” said Harry.

He had thought about how he would do it. He said it all in about a minute. It was very easy. He knew just what to say to someone who was eleven, and small, and whom nobody had yet picked for anything; he could have written it out. He felt in his pocket for the coin, and put it into a hot, slightly sticky hand, and closed the fingers on it. He said that thing about going anywhere at night. *You’re to tell me first.*

“Why?” said Draco.

It came out in a sort of squeak. He was holding the coin in both hands, against his shirt. He had grey eyes, very clear, and he looked up with them, and for a moment it was like being looked at across a corridor by somebody else, a good deal taller.

“Because I think you’d be good at it,” said Harry. “And I’d go with you. If you got lost.”

He had seen a boy with that face in a robe shop, once. He’d had it for seven years; he had seen it sneer and go grey and sit in a bathroom, crying. He had seen it at the end of a table, with a snake going past. It had never in all that time looked at him as though he had hung the moon.

Draco went on looking. Then he grinned, with the gap in it.

“Everybody thinks I get lost,” said Draco.

He ran off down the corridor with his bag banging against his legs. He always ran. Harry stood there a moment. He had got what he came for. He looked at his hand, the one that had closed those fingers, and he thought that of all the things that he had done with it, that had been about the most thoroughly competent.

He liked him. That was the awkward part. He hadn’t allowed for that.

“It isn’t the *charm* I’m interested in,” said Hermione, “it’s the principle. Look. You put a Protean on the master, and then every other one takes its form from that, so when you change the numbers on this, they all change. It’s completely silent. It’s not meant to be possible at our level. It’s a seventh-year charm, it’s in Vablatsky, and I got it wrong eleven times. And the point is that you can’t tell. You could have one in your pocket in front of Umbridge. It’s a *Galleon*.”

It was the Thursday, in the long room. It had a low roof and a smell of cold dust and something frying, and somebody a long time ago had painted a white line down the middle of the boards. There were thirty-one people in it. They had been standing about in their shirtsleeves, not looking at each other, Gryffindors and Hufflepuffs and a tall stray Ravenclaw or two; and Hermione had got up on a bench, with a sack, at Diggory’s request, to explain the coins. She had been explaining them for six minutes. She had gone pink. Her hair was coming down and she had a Galleon in each hand. Harry had seen her look at a great many things, and he thought that this might be the happiest he had ever seen her without somebody being wrong.

“Nobody’s got the faintest idea what you’re talking about,” said Ron. He said it from the floor, where he was sitting with his long legs out. He had a look on his face that Harry had seen him give the Chudley Cannons, in the few weeks of the year when they weren’t actually losing. “She did that,” he told Dean Thomas, with a jerk of his head. “By herself. Eleven times.”

“Will you marry me?” said Fred.

“It’s only a Protean Charm,” said Hermione, and dropped one of the Galleons. It rolled, and a very small boy in a bow tie, who had been standing by himself next to the wall trying to look as though he did this every Thursday, got to it first.

The room had gone rather quiet. Fred had stopped smiling. Thirty people were looking at a bow tie, and working out whose.

“Potter,” said Diggory, not loudly.

“You said anybody who asks,” said Harry.

“I did.” Diggory looked down the room for some time at a very small boy with a coin held against his shirt. “Can you keep your mouth shut?”

“Yes,” said Draco. It came out as a squeak, and everybody heard it.

“Then he’s yours,” said Diggory to Harry, “and so is whatever comes of it. Bring a clean shirt.”

Then somebody coughed, and it was Diggory, and Harry had to go and stand at the front.

He didn’t know how to do this. It was absurd. He had done it before, in a room a good deal larger, with a lot of mirrors; he had been fifteen. He stood on the white line, with his back to the wall, and they all looked at him. There was a Hufflepuff girl with a plait and a glove. He could see Ron. He told them what it was for. He told them about the wand movement, and the words. It came to about a minute.

“It runs on a memory,” said Harry. “That’s all. That’s the whole of it. You need the happiest thing you’ve got. Not something you think ought to be. Not your birthday, unless it was. You’ve got to go and look for it and find one that’s real, and stand in it. If it’s not good enough, it won’t come. So you’d better go and have a think, and try it. You don’t have to tell anybody.”

“Show them,” said Diggory, from the wall.

He had known that was coming. He put up his wand and went to look, and it was all there where he had left it: a canvas roof, and a dragon somewhere on the other side of it, and a hand laid over his. It had a wrist on the end of it.

“Later,” said Harry. “They’ll only copy.”

It wasn’t much of an answer. Diggory looked at him for rather longer than it deserved, and let it go.

They told each other at once. It had not occurred to him that they would. He ought to have known. It was a cold room, and there wasn’t anything else to do with your hands, and within five minutes the whole length of it was full of people standing about in twos and threes, with their wands drooping, muttering and looking embarrassed and starting to laugh. Dean’s was a day at West Ham. Seamus’s had a bonfire in it, and his mother’s face, and had to be told twice. Fred and George had one between them and would not say what it was, and it was plainly illegal. A big seventh-year Hufflepuff he had never spoken to said that he had once won a marrow competition, and went red, and looked ready to fight anybody who laughed. Nobody did. Susan Bones’s was her aunt, at Christmas, doing a very bad impression of Madam Pince.

Neville stood by himself with his eyes shut. A wisp came out of his wand, about the size of a breath on a cold day. He looked at it with his mouth open.

“What was that?” said Harry.

“It’s when it flowered,” said Neville, in a whisper. “She gives me things, when we go. My mum. They’re only rubbish, she doesn’t— I put one in a pot. I was six. I didn’t know it wasn’t a seed.” He stopped. “It was only the once,” he said.

Harry didn’t say anything. He had done this in the other life, and he hadn’t asked. He’d gone along the line and said *Good, try again*. He stood there beside Neville Longbottom in a cold room, and he felt something go through the bottom of his chest, and he said, “Do it again.”

Ron’s was the hoops. He didn’t say so; he had his eyes shut and his tongue out and his elbow in. Hermione was going to do it from a book, and was explaining this to Ginny. Ginny had her wand down. She was leaning against the wall with her arms folded, watching everybody, and she wasn’t trying.

“Got one?” said Harry.

She looked at him. She had got a very direct way of looking; she got it from the Quaffle.

“I’ve got one,” said Ginny. “I’m choosing. Some of them have got holes in.”

She said it lightly. She pushed herself off the wall and went away along the line, to tell Draco Malfoy that he was holding his wand like a fish.

He got to the end of the room. He had told himself that he would stop there, and go back to the front, where he could be seen. There was a long thin boy on the bench under the window, sitting by himself, as you sit when you have been told to rest. He had his wand in his left hand, and he was looking at it. He looked up, and started to get to his feet, and Harry shook his head.

“I can’t get it to do anything,” said Wayne Hopkins. “I’m ever so sorry. It’s not the wrong hand, Diggory says that doesn’t matter. It’s just I can’t find one.”

“One what?”

“A good one.” His light eyes went to the bandage, and came away from it. He seemed to feel that he had said something rude. “I’ve got quite a lot. I keep getting them out, and they’re all right. I had a very nice time at my nan’s. Only they’ve all got a sort of mark on them now. I look at them, and I think, well, you didn’t know, did you? I don’t know why. It’s stupid. It’s only a hand.”

He looked at it. Then, as if it might be some use to somebody, he felt in his robes with his left hand and got out a small new notebook, and showed Harry the page. There were two columns of something that might have been written by a spider in a high wind.

“I’ve started it again,” said Hopkins. “There’s ten now. Nobody can read it. I’m ever so sorry. I thought that might be quite good.”

Down the room, somebody laughed, and a thin bright thread of silver went up to the ceiling and vanished, and a lot of people cheered.

Harry stood there. He had taught this. He’d had a hundred ways of doing it. He looked at that careful bandage, tied by someone who had done it very neatly; and at the fingers. He knew where she had been sitting. He had got her by the wrist. His hand was in his pocket, and it had closed.

“Sorry,” said Hopkins again, quite quietly, to the wand.
