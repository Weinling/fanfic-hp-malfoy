# Chapter 92: Twenty-Three

They held it in the Hufflepuff corridor, by the barrels, because Slytherin had nowhere that would have done.

Her mother had come for her on the Tuesday, and nobody from the school would be going to the north. So on the Thursday, at about four in the afternoon, with the snow coming down past the high barred windows, Harry came along the passage by the kitchens on his way to nowhere, and found it full.

There must have been eighty of them. They were standing along both walls, not saying very much. Somebody had brought a chair and put it by the pear. On the chair was a plate.

Hopkins had done that. He had carried it up from the kitchens himself, with his left hand and his forearm, and set it down, and stepped back. It was a slice of fish pie, gone a bit cold. He stood looking at that, long and mild, with his bad hand against his chest, and he didn’t appear to be going to say anything. There was a house-elf beside the chair, and another, and then you noticed there were a good many, in a row, along the skirting, with their ears down. They had brought a very small loaf.

Nobody had organised anything. They just came up, as you’d go up for a Sorting. A second-year put down a bun. Nott stood there for some time with his long dull careful face, and took a chess piece out of his pocket, and looked at it, and put it back. He went and stood beside a thickset girl who wasn’t looking at him; and after a while, without turning her head, she moved over by about an inch.

There was a sort of hiccup, by the wall.

Harry looked round, with everybody else. Hermione Granger was standing with her back to the stone, with a bag of woolly hats held against her chest in both arms and her chin up, and she was crying. It was coming out of her in great tearing ugly gulps, with her mouth square and her nose running, like a child of about six, and she made no attempt to do anything about it; she stood there in front of half the school and howled. Harry had known her since she was eleven, in two lives, and he had never heard that noise. “She *said*,” Hermione was saying. “She said there was too much sugar in the— I was going to— I’d got a *rota*—” Nobody had been expecting it. Goyle had been a Slytherin. Ginny went over, without any hurry, and took one hand off the bag, and held it.

Crabbe went last.

She had been standing at the far end, and they made room for her. It took her some time to come; she walked as though she was carrying something, slowly, with her big plain pale face looking straight in front of her. She stopped at the chair, put her hand into the pocket of her robes, and brought out a piece of greaseproof paper, folded small, and laid it on the seat. She went on. There was a toffee paper, and a stub of pencil, and a bit of string, and a recipe on the back of a page of Arithmancy, in a big slow round hand. She got out a long thin sheet with figures on it, *Eight Galleons a quarter*, and an estimate from a man in Tutshill for a slab of Welsh marble. She emptied one pocket and then the other. She turned them inside out.

She didn’t cry. She looked up. She looked along the wall, slow and careful and thorough, at a person who was standing by herself, a little apart, with a good three feet of stone on either side of her that nobody had liked to fill.

He’d been trying not to. He looked.

She was in black, with her hair up, the colour of the snow on the sill, and she stood very straight with her hands held together in front of her, in a pair of old grey woollen gloves that didn’t fit. One thumb was a good deal too long; he could see that from here. There was nothing on her face at all, and there hadn’t been since Sunday. He’d looked at it at ten past eight on four mornings, as you’d look at the clock.

Crabbe’s eyes rested on her for a while. Then they moved away.

His pocket had got warm.

He didn’t take it in, at first. It was his left, with the notebook in it, and the third one; he’d got so that he hardly noticed it. There was a small round spot of heat, against his thigh. It got hotter. He put his hand down, and his fingers closed on a Galleon.

It nearly burnt him.

It was the turn of the passage, past the fruit bowl, where the stairs went down to the old storerooms. Nobody came there. There was one torch, and a smell of onions, and he came round the corner, with the coin in his fist and his wand out, and for a moment he thought there was no one there.

Something hiccupped.

A very small boy was sitting on the bottom step, with his knees together and his bag on them, and his scarf wound round something in his lap, with both his hands clamped on it. He was in his clean grey uniform and his hair was flat. It was the tie that stopped Harry’s heart. It had slipped round under his left ear and come partly undone, with one end hanging, and he hadn’t noticed. He looked up. His face was perfectly white and quite composed, and it was shaking, all over, in small quick regular jerks.

“You said,” said Draco Malfoy.

*Hic*.

“You said. If anybody ever asked me to go anywhere. You said I had to tell you first.” It was a clear small careful treble. It stopped for a moment, and jerked, and went on. “I wasn’t going to. I thought about it. I thought I’d see. Only then I looked. So I’m telling you. I’ve told you now.”

He got down on one knee. He couldn’t have said how. “Draco. All right. It’s all right. Who asked you?”

A hand came out of the scarf. It held out a sheet of paper, thick and cream-coloured and faintly ribbed, folded once. He took that, and he tilted it to the torch, and there were two lines on it, in a small upright clear good hand, the hand of a boy who has won prizes.

*There is a pale box at the bottom of your sister’s trunk, in the left boot. She is tired. Take it from her tonight, and bring it to the seventh floor. Everybody thinks you get lost. You would do.*

He read it. He read it twice. There was a roaring noise, somewhere, and it seemed to be a long way off.

“When did you get this?”

“This morning. It was on my plate.” *Hic*. “Nobody saw. I was so—” He stopped. Something had started to happen to his mouth, and he did something to it, with his teeth. “He said I would do. He said so again. I went at lunchtime. I know all her hiding places, she thinks I don’t. It was locked. I’ve always had a key. It was where he said. She’d put it back very neatly. It had a card in it. I didn’t want to look. I was told not to, in the summer. But it’s not— It’s not mine now. She took it. So I thought it was all right.”

He unwound the scarf.

It was about the size of a prayer book, very clean, made of some pale smooth wood like the inside of an almond. He held it out in both hands, at the full length of his short arms, and his fingers had left damp marks on it.

Harry took it from him. It hardly weighed anything. He laid his wand down on the step. He lifted the lid. He could smell chocolate. Under that there was something else, very faint and sweet, like the peel of an orange on a winter morning; and he had been shown a lot of things in the green underwater light of a certain dungeon, he’d been taught to stand over a thing, and breathe in, and name what it was. His whole skin had gone cold. *To the Headmaster*, said a card, *with the compliments of the Sixth Year*.

They lay in two layers, in pleated paper. Each one had a small dark curl on it. They were very beautifully done.

“There were twenty-four,” said Draco.

Harry looked up.

“I counted. In July. I gave it to her and I told her that. I count things.” The pale eyes were fixed on the box. They were enormous. There was no expression in them whatever, and the tears had started to run out of them, quite steadily, without any help. “There’s twelve in each. It’s very exact. I always count. So I counted now. You can look.”

He looked. In the corner of the top layer, on the left, there was a small square of pleated paper, with nothing on it but a brown smear.

One gap.

*Her heart*, somebody had said.

He stayed there, on one knee. The torch was hissing. 

“She gave me pastry,” said Draco.

It was very low. He could hardly hear it. The small clean shaking face had lifted, and it was looking at him, and it wasn’t twelve. It wasn’t any age. He had seen that look, on a child, in a war.

“I used to go at six. She let me hit the dough. She never said anything. She used to send me a tin. She sent me one every holiday, and I’ve still got the— I’ve got twenty-one. They had orange in. She said I could have the end of the— Is that— Harry, is that—”

He couldn’t get any further. It got stuck. His mouth stayed open. *Hic*, he said.

Harry put out his hands without thinking about it. He took hold of the end of that bow tie, or whatever you’d call it, in his cold stiff fingers, and drew it round, and pulled it straight, and tucked in the end. It was a very bad job. He got his arm round a small hard narrow pair of shoulders, like a bird’s, that went rigid and stayed rigid for about a second. Something butted him under the chin. There was a thin high dry sound, that he’d heard from a boy of eleven, in another life, in a wood.

“You told me,” said Harry. His voice was very strange. “You did it right. You did exactly right. D’you hear me? You told me first. You don’t have to go anywhere. You’re not going to the seventh floor, you’re not going anywhere, you’re going to go with Neville. You know Neville. You’re going to go to the greenhouse, with Neville, now, and stay there till somebody comes. I’ll fix it. I’ll fix it, I swear. Give me that.”

He took the sheet of paper. He had got his coin in his hand, and he pressed. Very quickly and rather clumsily he made it say what you’d make it say; somewhere at the far end of the passage, by a chair with a slice of pie on it, a boy with a crooked jaw would be feeling in his pocket.

His ring had started to burn.

He’d have known without looking. It had been blue for thirty-four days, and it went through his finger like a hot wire. He held it up in the torchlight, and it was the colour of a fresh cut, and it was pulling, up and to the left. It didn’t go down at the earth, this time, or over a hedge. She was in the building.

*Second floor*, it said.

He’d always known. He had known since he was sixteen. He’d known that it would be there.

The sign said *OUT OF ORDER*. The door was open by about a hand.

He could hear the water from the end of the corridor. A tap was running, or a good many. It was nearly dark, with the snow-light coming in at the high frosted windows, grey and blue on the wet floor, and it smelt of old drains. All along one wall the long cracked mirror gave him back a thin boy in black, with a box. The ghost had stopped crying. He noticed it. There wasn’t any sound at all but the water, and something else.

She was at the sinks.

She was on the floor. He didn’t see her, for a moment, and then he did. She had got down on the wet stone, at the foot of the big round basin with the snake on the tap, with her back against it and her legs anyhow. She’d lost a shoe. Her hair had come down. He had seen that in a gale, and he’d seen it in a green fire. He’d never seen it like that, in wet ropes, stuck to her face. She was holding on to something, with her arms wrapped round it. It was a pair of grey gloves. She had got them pressed against her mouth.

She was crying. It wasn’t anything he had ever heard. Her mouth was wide open against the wool, with her whole face gone into it and her eyes shut, and it came up out of her with no shape, no breath, no end; it was what you’d hear from an animal in a trap, if the trap had been there for a very long time. It was the first time since Sunday, and nobody in the world had seen it. She had her left arm clamped against her ribs, and she was rocking, a very little, backward and forward on the stone, and saying one word, over and over. He couldn’t make it out. It might have been a name. It might have been *nice*.

He’d stopped in the door.

Six months. He stood there with his hand on the wet wood. He had the whole of it ready, he’d come up two flights of stairs with it in his mouth. He looked at the top of her head. There was a white line where her hair parted. He hadn’t known that a person could get so small. His knees had started to go. Something enormous had come up in his throat, and he took a step; he had his free hand out, it was out of his pocket, he didn’t care. He was going to go down on that floor. He was going to go down on his knees in that water and get hold of her and he’d never in this world let go.

Then he felt the corner of the box, under his arm.

*Twenty-four*.

He stopped. He stood quite still. He looked at the box for some time.

There was a basin about a yard from her head. He walked over, with his boots making a noise in the wet, and she heard that: the sound stopped, as if someone had cut it with a knife, and her shoulders came up. He didn’t look. He set the pale wooden box down on the edge of the sink, very gently, squared it with the rim, took off the lid and laid it beside it. He stood back.

“Twenty-three,” said Harry.

It wasn’t loud. It went round the walls and came back.

She lifted her head.

He hadn’t been ready for her eyes. They were swollen nearly shut, and there was nothing left of her face but that. She looked at his boots, and up, and at the box, and he saw it reach her, slowly, from a long way off. She didn’t scream, or start. She got one hand down on the stone, and her knee, and she began to get up. It took a long time. She pulled herself up the basin an inch at a time, with her hair in her mouth, and she stood there, swaying, on one shoe, with her left arm pressed in against her side, and God knows how, she got her chin up. It was shaking so much that she could hardly keep it there. She looked at him, out of what was left.

“Draco,” she said. It was a dreadful voice. There wasn’t anything of it. “Where’s—”

“He’s safe. He gave it to me. He told me first.” It had begun. He could feel it. It was coming up, cold, from under his feet. “It wasn’t her heart.”

She didn’t say anything.

“It wasn’t her *heart*. Was it? I’ve been looking at you for five days. You stood at the table. *Poor Goyle*. I heard. I thought— I don’t know what I thought. On the stairs. I thought you’d— She made you a *sandwich!* She made you a sandwich every day for six months!” His voice had cracked. It went up, and it broke; he heard it go; and something ran down the side of his nose, and it was wet. He was crying. He hadn’t known. It wasn’t what you’d have called noble. “You let me think— You sat there! I’ve been— I said *Please come*, I did that to you, I’ve wanted to cut my tongue out; and you’ve got this, you had this in a *boot*, all the time, and I’ve been— Who are you? Who *are* you? I asked you! I stood there and I asked you who you were talking to!”

Her lips had moved. It might have been *Stop*. Her eyes had gone very wide, and they’d gone to the wall, the door, the empty stalls. Her right hand had started to come up, palm out. She was shaking her head. She shook it, a very small quick shake. *No*. He’d seen her do that, across a room, once, in a dream.

He didn’t care. He’d stopped caring. He couldn’t have stopped it for a thousand Galleons. He took a step towards her, with his finger out, and it was shaking, and he’d never in his life been so glad to say anything.

“I know what it is! I know what’s on your arm! I watched him do that to you. I saw you butter that toast. I’ve known since August. I know whose you are, I know what you’ve been doing every Sunday, you’re not a *clerk*. That box was for Dum—”

Her wand was in her hand.

He never saw it come. It had been in her sleeve, and she’d been holding on to a basin, and it was there, level, at the end of her arm, a foot and a half from his chest. Everything else about her was shaking, and there wasn’t any tremor in it at all. Her eyes were on his. She’d stopped crying. He had seen that face looking up from a pink carpet, about a foot from his, and he had been smiling.

*You put a stop to me*, it said. *Mid-sentence*.

“*Cru*—” said Lyra.

He heard it. He heard the first half of the word go out on the wet air.

So did she.

He would see that till he died. He saw her hear what she was saying. It went across her face from the hairline, like something being wiped, and her teeth came together on the rest; she bit it clean through, and he actually heard them click. There was blood on her lip. Her eyes had gone huge and blind and appalled, and they had gone to her own hand, hanging at the end of her arm as if it had been left by somebody else, with the wand in it, pointing at his heart. A breath went into her, with a thin high whooping noise, like a child’s.

She took it down.

She took it down, and it was the hardest thing that anybody had ever done. It took the whole of her. Her arm shook; it came down an inch, and another, like someone lifting a house, until she had got it nearly to her side. Half of that word did nothing. It wanted meaning, to the end, and she had bitten off the end. She had stopped herself, in the middle, with her teeth, in the worst hour of her life, and she stood there with her empty face turned up to him and her mouth bleeding, and she had got her hand down.

And his arm had come up.

He hadn’t lifted it. He felt it go. He looked. It rose from the shoulder, very smooth and level, with no weight in it at all. It had the wand in it. He didn’t know when. It knew this room. It had a taste like a coin. It came up out of the floor of him, up a long dark groove that had been waiting there since a corridor when he was eleven. He knew what it was. He knew exactly what it was. He saw her wand go down.

*No*, he thought. *She’s stopped. She’s stopped, look at her, she’s stopped*—

*Mine*, it said. Very quietly, behind his right ear. It was so pleased. For an instant, God help him, so was he.

He tried. He’d say that for himself. He would say that to anybody, in a very long life, and it wouldn’t help. He threw his whole weight against it. She had looked up, a yard away, and he saw her eyes go to the end of his arm, and she didn’t lift a finger. She had taken a good look at that, some time ago, in a wardrobe, and she knew what it was. He got his mouth open. He had the whole of his breath, the only thing that was still his, and he tried to get it out, the one word, to get her out from in front of it; he screamed at her, with his throat tearing, from the bottom of two lives.

“*MOVE!*”

And the other came out on the same breath.

“*Sec*—”

He had got it wrong, about the noise. He’d thought there would be one.

There wasn’t a flash, and she didn’t cry out. She made a very small sound of surprise, like somebody who has been told something interesting, and her head went back a little. A line had appeared on the black of her robe, from the left side of her neck down across her arm, as neat as the crease in a letter; he’d have sworn that it was a thread. Then the line had a good deal of width. Then she was sitting down in the water, with her back against the basin and her legs folding under her, as if somebody had told her she could. She looked as if she was going to ask him something.

The water had started to change colour.

He’d dropped his wand. No. He had it. He didn’t know how he’d got there. He was on his knees. He had crossed the floor, there was a lot of water, and it was warm; it had got into his sleeves. He’d got his hands on the place. *Don’t*, he was saying. *Don’t, don’t, don’t, no, no, please, I didn’t, no*— It came up between his fingers. There was so much. Nobody had told him. There had been a side of pork. He pressed, and it came up round the edges of his hands, in time with something. He got his wand. He’d got it. He knew this. It had taken him thirteen weeks. *You will not be in a state to sing*. You held it an inch above; against the direction of the wound. There were three notes and a fourth. He opened his mouth.

It came out as a sort of croak. He tried again. He knelt in the red water of the girls’ bathroom on the second floor, with the tears running into his mouth, and he sang, in a cracked high tuneless shaking voice, the song he had learnt for a leg of mutton. His hand was jumping. It closed for about an inch, like a badly sewn pocket, and it came undone. *Again*, somebody said. *The patient has by now bled to death*. If you stopped to breathe it came undone. Her sleeve had been cut through from the shoulder, and it fell open along her arm, heavy and wet, and he dragged it back, to get at the place, with his red slippery fingers, and he looked.

He’d never seen it.

It was very thin, and rather pretty, greenish-grey, like a line of engraving. It lay along the inside of her forearm in two long slow curves, and the wound had gone across it, a little above the elbow, without so much as a scratch. It ran down by the blue veins to the wrist, to a small flat head lying on her pulse, with one eye, the size of a grain of rice. It was open.

It was looking at him.

He’d stopped singing. He hadn’t meant to.

There was a movement under his hand. Her eyes had come round, very pale, from a long way back. He put down his head to hear her. There was blood on her teeth, from where she’d bitten through a word. Her right hand had got out of the water, God knows how; it came up off the stone by about two inches, with the fingers dragging, and it went by his knee, and it touched the back of his right hand, lying on her arm, with the white letters on it, wet and red.

She took hold of that. There wasn’t any strength in it at all. She pulled.

He let her. He didn’t know how you stopped her. She drew his hand along her forearm, an inch and an inch, down the two slow curves, and it left a mark. She brought it to the place. She pushed his thumb down, on the inside of her left wrist, on the thin cold skin, on top of the small flat head. He felt her pulse, right under the ball of his thumb. It was so light. It was going very fast, and it kept missing.

She looked at him. There was something at the corner of her mouth. It was very nearly insolent.

“Hold it,” said Lyra.

He couldn’t see. He held it as you’d hold an egg, or a lit match, as he had held it on a hill, in the snow, with her teeth chattering, a hundred years ago. *Until I tell you*. He put his forehead down on her arm, with his other hand on the wound and his wand in it, and he started again, from the top, three notes and a fourth, with his whole body shaking. It closed for a bit, and it stayed. *Please*, he sang, there weren’t any words, it was just the sound. *Please. Please. Come. Please.*

Something moved under his thumb.

It wasn’t her pulse. It was smaller. He felt it through the skin: a very slight, slow, drowsy sort of shifting, like a child turning over. He raised his head. He looked. He took his thumb away, by about a quarter of an inch, and it was the most extraordinary thing.

It was closing.

The lid had come down. There hadn’t been a lid. A thin grey skin had come from nowhere, from the top and the bottom of that small round eye, and it was drawing across, very gradually, as you’d see it in somebody who has been kept up far too late and has been told that they may go to bed. It stopped halfway, and drooped. It looked at him, with the last dull bit of tea-coloured light, not seeing him at all; and it went out. There was a small flat grey-green head, lying on a girl’s wrist, as peaceful as a stone.

He heard her breathe in.

It was a very long breath, with a catch in it. He had seen that face do everything a face could do, across a chessboard and across a Great Hall, and he hadn’t seen this since a Wednesday in June, with a cake. All the bones had let go. She had felt it stop. Something that had been standing behind a door, for a hundred and forty-one days, wasn’t there, and every line of her had started, slowly, to come down. She looked up at him, with her eyes full, and there was somebody in them, right up at the front, all of her, looking straight into his face from about a foot away. Oh, God. There she was. *There you are*. She was so tired. She was smiling.

“It’s asleep,” said Lyra.

It wasn’t much more than the shape of it. It was the voice from the wind. He’d never heard that voice. He’d only seen it, once, on a very bad day for flying.

“What? What’s— Don’t talk. Don’t talk, I’ve got it, it’s closing, look, hold on—”

“Harry.” It had a kind of wonder in it. “*Harry*. I can… It’s asleep. Listen. Listen to me. It hears.” Her fingers moved on his. “It’s always heard. Every word. That’s all it’s for. In the room. That’s why. All of it. Oh, your stupid *face*. I’ve been telling you… I told you… You said *What?*”

He stared at her. He was still singing; it was coming out of him by itself. *It hears*. Somewhere, a very long way down, something had started to fall.

*I SAID YOUR FLYING’S A DISGRACE!*

“Oh,” said Harry.

He hadn’t any breath, and it stopped the song, and he got it going again. The wound had started to creep shut under his wand, an inch at a time, ragged and pale, and it held; he could feel her pulse steady and strengthen, under his thumb. It was going to work. He was going to save her. *Oh, thank God*, he thought, who had never prayed. *Thank you. Thank you*. He bent over it, sobbing, and he sang.

Her eyes had changed.

She was looking at his hand, at the wand going along her arm, and the line of the wound closing up behind it, and then down at her own wrist. He saw the smile stop. It stayed where it was, and something came up behind it, from a long way off. Her eyebrows drew together, very slightly, in a small tired frown. She looked at the sealed grey lid, and at the wound. She’d always been so quick. Her lips had started to move, without any sound, and she got her tongue round it.

“It only sleeps—” said Lyra.

The door hit the wall.

He came through it like something thrown. The black robes were flying out behind him. He’d lost all his colour; he had his left hand clamped round his own right wrist, as hard as it would go, and there was smoke coming from between the fingers, a thin grey curl of it, with a stink of burning. Harry had never seen him run. He stopped dead, in the red water, with his boots in it, and he looked at the floor. He looked at her, and at the boy on his knees, and at the wand; and he looked along that long ragged half-closed line. The black eyes came up to Harry’s, and there was nothing in the world that you could have called an expression in them.

“That,” said Severus Snape, “is *mine*.”

He hit Harry in the chest with his forearm, and Harry went over, and lay on his side on the stone with his arm stretched out and his fingers still round her wrist. Snape had gone down on both knees, with his thin black wand out. He drew one breath, through his nose. And the song came out of him, low and harsh and rusty, up and down, with no gap in it at all, as it had come in another room in another life, and it was the most beautiful thing Harry had ever heard. The ragged edges stirred, and drew together, as a thing draws together that remembers what it was. It went along her arm and up her shoulder, with the wand going before it, a line of hard white light. He didn’t stop to breathe. He went over it three times.

It was closed.

There was a long raised seam, as thick as a bootlace, from the side of her neck to the middle of her arm. Her eyes had shut. She was breathing. She was the colour of the basin; but it went in and out, slow and shallow, and quite regular, and her lips had come together, with that very small frown still between her brows.

Nobody spoke. The snow-light had nearly gone. There was just the wet dark floor, and the long mirror.

He got up on one elbow, which took some time; he couldn’t feel his legs. His right hand was red to the wrist, and it was lying on her arm. He hadn’t let go. He didn’t think he’d be able to.

He felt it, then.

Her pulse had got stronger. It was going along under the ball of his thumb, quite evenly, as it should. She’d been so nearly gone, and she was coming back, a little at a time, like the tide coming up a beach. He had cut her from the neck to the elbow; and he thought, with a great dull stupid surge of joy, that she was going to live.

Something moved, under his thumb.

It was about the size of a grain of rice. It gave a small drowsy shift, like someone who has been asleep, and has heard a noise. He looked down. He moved his thumb.

The thin grey skin had started to draw back.

He couldn’t do anything. He couldn’t take his eyes away. It slid up, and it slid down, very gently, in no sort of hurry at all. There was a line of dull brown, and a slit, and it widened. It was the colour of weak tea, quite round, and perfectly awake. It lay on the pulse of the only person he had ever come for, and it opened all the way.

It looked at him.
