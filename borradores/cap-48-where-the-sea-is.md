# Chapter 48: Where the Sea Is

“Again,” said Professor Snape.

It was the fourth Thursday. The fire in his office had burned down to a red seam, and the jars along the walls had gone dark, and Lyra was sitting in the hard chair in front of his desk with her hands in her lap and her head aching as though somebody had been tapping on the inside of it with a spoon.

Somebody had.

“*Legilimens.*”

She let him in.

That was the whole art of it, he said, and it was the hardest thing she had ever been asked to do. Everything in her wanted to shut the door. She had been shutting it, without knowing, since she was eleven; it was as natural to her as putting up a hand to a blow. Instead, every Thursday, she had to stand aside, and hold it open, and let a cold hand come into her head and walk about in it as if it lived there.

She had furnished a room for it. She had worked on the room all week. A breakfast table at the Manor, with the light coming in sideways and her father reading the financial pages. A Quidditch match, last year, the wind in her hair. Draco, at the Sorting, with the Hat over his eyes. A boy she had quarrelled with, in a corridor on a train, and her own voice saying *sit down, Potter*, and a small mean pleasure at having said it, which was true, and which she had polished up a little, to make it shine. An Arithmancy test she was worried about. A dress her mother had promised her for Christmas. All of it real. All of it chosen.

The hand walked about in the room. It picked things up and put them down. It looked at the dress for a long time, which she thought was a little unnecessary.

Then it stopped.

*There*, said nothing, inside her head, in Snape’s voice. *What is that?*

She knew what it was before she looked. In the corner of the room, where she had not put anything, where there should have been only the dark: a little point of light. A candle. Held up in a corridor she had never meant to show anybody, by somebody small, and bare-footed, and frightened.

She put it out.

She did not slam the door. She had learned that much. She simply reached into the corner of her own furnished room and pinched the wick, as one pinches a candle at bedtime, and it was gone, and the dark was only dark; and the hand stood looking at the place where it had been for a moment longer, and then withdrew.

Snape sat back in his chair. He was breathing a little hard. So was she.

“Better,” he said.

She waited.

“There was a candle. In a corridor I did not recognise. You put it out. Neatly.” He looked at her with no expression whatsoever. “Furniture, Miss Malfoy, does not move. If there is a light in a room, a Legilimens will look at it, because it is the only thing in the room that is alive. Next time, do not let it in at all. Or if you must, give it a reason to be there that he will find dull.”

“Yes, sir.”

“I do not ask what it was.” He picked up a quill, and looked at it, and put it down. “I should prefer not to know. Thursday.”

She went up the dungeon steps with her head ringing and her hands quite cold, and thought about a man who had looked into her and found her brother in the dark, and had not asked.

She stopped pushing the jar on the last Wednesday of his detentions.

She had pushed it for nine days, an inch at a time, across Professor Umbridge’s desk, without looking up. On the Wednesday the hour ended and he put down the quill, and he took off the bandage to look at what was under it, and she saw what he had been doing to it, with his left hand, in a dormitory, by himself.

“Give me that,” said Lyra.

She came round the desk. She pulled the little pink chair out from under the window, and sat down on it, knee to knee with him, and took his right hand in both of hers and turned it to the light.

He had held hers like that once, in the brambles behind the Shack, to stop it shaking. She did not suppose that he remembered. She took the stopper out of the jar with her teeth.

“It’s fine,” said Potter.

“It is not fine. It is a disgrace. Who taught you to bandage? I have seen better work on a ham.” She tipped the yellow stuff on to her fingers, and laid them on the back of his hand, on the line and the three small words beneath it, and felt him breathe in through his nose. She did not look up. She was so angry that she could feel it in her teeth. “Hold still.”

The kittens frisked. She worked along from the knuckles to the wrist, a very little at a time. His hand was cold, and a good deal bigger than it had been, and it lay in hers without doing anything at all.

“I love you,” said Potter.

Her fingers stopped.

It was a quarter past six on a Wednesday, in Dolores Umbridge’s office. There was blood on her cuff. He had said it in a perfectly ordinary voice, rather hoarse, to the top of her head, as one might remark that it had come on to rain.

She went on with what she was doing.

“Hold still,” said Lyra.

“I was only—”

“I know what you were only. I read it. I am aware of the position.”

“It’s different out loud.”

“It is a great deal worse out loud. There’s a kitten looking.” She tore the clean bandage with her teeth. She was not at all sure what was happening to her face, and she kept it bent over his hand. “That is the worst-timed thing I have ever heard anybody say. You have no conception of when to say anything. You never have. Is that all, or would you like to tell me something else while I’m holding your hand in the office of a woman who is at this moment having tea with the Minister?”

“That’s all.”

“Good.”

She tied it off, rather hard. Then she held on to it for a moment longer than the knot required, and she gave it back to him, like something he had dropped.

“Thank you,” said Potter.

“Don’t you dare,” said Lyra.

The note was in her prefect’s pigeonhole on a Wednesday in the middle of October, between a list of rounds from Diggory and a reminder from Filch about Dungbombs, and it was folded into a very small square and had nothing written on the outside at all.

She recognised the small, untidy hand. The letters were in ink this time.

*Third floor. The knight. Ten o’clock. Please.*

She read it twice. The second time she read only the last word, which he had written smaller than the others, and pressed harder, as though he had not been sure it would stay on the paper.

He had never written *please* to her in his life. He had never needed to; he had always been the one who knew what was coming, and she had always been the one who came.

She burnt it in the common-room fire, and went to her rounds, and at ten o’clock she was on the third floor.

⁂

The classroom behind the tapestry of the knight asleep on his horse was as it had always been: desks, dust, a window on the lake, the moon. She had called him here in June and told him to swear he would not lie to her.

He was sitting on a desk by the window with his feet on a chair, and he got up when she came in.

He had taken the bandage off. That was the first thing she saw, before his face, before anything: his right hand, at his side, in the moonlight, and on the back of it the line Umbridge had cut there, healed now to a raised silvery white, *I must not tell lies*, from the knuckles to the wrist; and underneath it, smaller, three more words, that the murtlap had closed but not taken away.

She looked at them. She could not help it. He let her.

“Thank you for coming,” said Potter.

“You said please.”

“Yeah.” He looked at the floor, and then back at her. “I didn’t know what else would work.”

“Nothing else would have. Say what you want, Potter. I have rounds at eleven.”

⁂

He told her standing up, by the window, with his back to the lake and the moonlight on the side of his face, and he told it badly, as he told everything that mattered, in pieces, out of order, going back for things.

His scar had opened. That was the first piece. Not the way it had opened in their first year, in a corridor, with Quirrell walking past; worse. Since the summer, since about the beginning of July, he had been dreaming, and the dreams were not his. He was somewhere else in them. He was somebody else.

“I’m in a house,” said Potter. “Big. Old. Cold, all the time, even in July. There’s a long room with windows down one side, very tall ones, and the sea outside them. I can hear it. It never stops. There’s a fireplace at one end that you could stand up in, with two dogs on either side of it, stone ones, sitting up, with their mouths open.”

Lyra did not move.

“And there are people. Coming and going. On their knees, mostly. I can’t see their faces properly; I’m—I’m looking down at them, and they keep their heads down. Except one.” He frowned, as if at something he had been trying to see for months. “An old man. White hair, long, and a cane, silver at the top. When the rest of them kneel, he doesn’t. He stands. He’s allowed to, I think. And I don’t know who he is.” He looked at her. “I don’t know who any of it is. I don’t know where the house is. There wasn’t any house like that, before. There wasn’t any old man.”

*Before.* He did not mean last year. She knew what he meant.

“And in August, I went to a place where I knew there was something. Something of his. Under a floor. I’ve known it was there since I was eleven, and I left it, because somebody told me to wait, and I went in August and it was gone. Somebody had come and taken it out, weeks before. Somebody who knew exactly where to look.” His hand closed at his side, the one with the words on it. “Somebody’s going round collecting his things, Lyra. And I don’t know who, and I don’t know where they’re taking them, and I don’t know where *he* is. I don’t know anything any more.”

He said her name in the middle of it as if it had got out by itself.

“I need you to tell me what you know. Whatever it is. I know you know something. You lied to me in this room, before the Third Task. You said so after. I’m not asking you what about. I’m asking you to tell me anyway.”

⁂

She stood by the door with her hands at her sides.

*When we are all together in the north.* She had stood between those stone hounds at seven, in her best dress, and put her hand in the mouth of the left-hand one because nobody was looking. Afterwards she had been told never to mention where she had been. Not even to Crabbe.

Potter had stood in that room in his sleep, looking down at people on their knees. She knew whose eyes he was looking out of.

She looked down at the scars on his hand.

She had said, in June, that she was on the other side of this, all the way. She had meant it. She still meant it; she was Lucius Malfoy’s daughter, and the house he was asking about was her family’s house, and there were people in it whom she loved.

But he was not asking her for her family. He was asking her for *him*. For the thing with no nose, in a long room by the sea, that the people she loved were on their knees to.

“It isn’t grim,” said Lyra. Her voice came out quite level. “The house. You said it as if it were grim. It isn’t. It’s Georgian. The windows were put in by my great-great-grandfather, who liked to watch the weather.”

Potter went very still.

“It’s on the coast of Northumberland. North of Alnwick. Not in any register in this country; my father saw to that, a long time ago, and I imagine his father saw to it before him. I have been there once. I was seven. We went by Portkey, from the study, and I never saw the road, so I can’t tell you how to get there and I shouldn’t, if I could.” She held his eyes. “In June, in this room, after you had sworn, Karkaroff had said to me, that same evening, on his ship, that he had done everything the letter asked. And that when they were all together *in the north*, he hoped he would be remembered.” She stopped. “That was what I didn’t tell you. That was the lie.”

The moonlight lay on the floor between them.

He did not say thank you. She was more grateful for that than she could have told him.

“The old man,” he said, after a long time. “With the cane.”

“I don’t know who he is.”

It was true. She heard it go into the room, true, and was glad of it.

“There are a great many old men with canes who would be allowed to stand.”

⁂

She had meant to stop there. The house was her family’s, and she had given him the place and the name. She ought to turn round and go to her rounds.

He said, “And whoever’s collecting—”

*Collecting some old things of his*, said her father’s voice, in a study, in July, at the door, with his hand on it, as if he had one more present in his pocket. *That were left with friends.*

And a boy in his pyjamas on the seventh floor at midnight, with a candle, and a sheet of old cream paper folded small against his chest, walking past a bare wall three times. *You won’t tell. Him. The gentleman.*

She kept her face exactly as it was. She had been learning to, on Thursday evenings, in a dungeon office with the jars along the walls: a room, furnished. A face with nothing behind it but what was expected.

“—you don’t know anything about that,” said Potter. “Do you.”

“No.”

It went out into the room, perfectly smooth, perfectly level, and lay there.

Potter looked at her.

She held her face arranged until he looked away. She could not tell whether he believed her.

“Right,” said Potter quietly.

He had said that once before, in this room, in June, to a lie.

⁂

He put his hand into the pocket of his robes and took something out and held it out to her, on his palm, the palm of the hand with the words on the back of it.

It was a ring. Plain silver. Hers.

“I’m not giving it back,” said Potter, before she could say anything. “Not like that. You don’t have to wear it for me.” He was speaking fast. “Keep it in your pocket. If anything happens, put it on, or hold it against your skin. It won’t tell me anything about you unless you touch it. But if mine goes red, I’ll know. And I’ll come. That’s all it’s for.”

“You can’t come to Wiltshire, Potter.”

“Watch me.”

She held out her hand, and he tipped the ring into it.

It lay in her palm, cool against her bare skin, and then warm; and while she looked at it, it changed colour.

She had braced for pink. Instead it went a deep, unhappy blue, like the sky over the lake a few minutes after the sun has gone. She had spent a summer trying not to think anything that might come out blue. She had not imagined it would look like this.

Neither of them said what it was. She thought he knew. She thought, from his face, that he had known for some months.

She closed her hand on it.

“Rounds. I am late.”

She went to the door. At the door she stopped, as she always stopped at doors in that room, and did not turn round.

“Potter. Whatever you do with it. Don’t go there yourself.”

“I won’t.”

She stood with her hand on the door for about as long as it took to know that he was lying.

Then she came back across the room. She had hold of him by the front of his robes before he had got his mouth shut, and she kissed him as she would have slammed something, and he made a noise in his throat and his bandaged hand came up and stopped an inch from her hair and stayed there. It was the first time since June that she had gone back for anything.

“Liar,” said Lyra, against his collar.

She let go of him, and went.

“Sixty,” said Potter, behind her, not very steadily.

At breakfast the next morning Draco was telling Urquhart’s little brother about a corridor on the seventh floor where, he said, if you knew the trick, a door came out of the wall.

“The gentleman wrote it all out for me,” he added, importantly. Potter was passing the end of the table on his way to the doors.

“You’ve found it, then,” said Urquhart’s little brother, impressed.

“Not *yet*,” said Draco, with dignity. “But I know the trick.”

He looked down the table, and saw Lyra looking at him, and went red, and changed the subject to Quidditch with great speed.

Lyra ate her toast. She had given Potter the north and kept Draco’s list from him.

At the end of the table Draco was waving his spoon, with his tie on crooked and the list folded against his heart. She took out her book of rounds and moved that evening’s visit to the seventh floor forward by half an hour.
