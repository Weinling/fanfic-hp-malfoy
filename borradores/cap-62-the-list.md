# Chapter 62: The List

“What would you do,” said Lyra, “about a drawer?”

Potter looked up from the fender. It was a Monday, on the timetable, the day after the crossword. He had been trying for some time to balance a sugar quill on his nose.

“What sort of drawer?”

“A locked one. Locked by a person of limited imagination, with a very great opinion of herself, and a taste for ribbon.”

The quill fell off. He caught it. She watched him not ask; it went across his face in stages, like somebody carrying a wardrobe past a series of windows. He had got rather good at that. *Nothing moves without asking.* It had occurred to her that if one was going to be a person who had broken a rule, one might at least be seen keeping it about something small.

“I’m asking,” said Lyra. “You may observe me doing so. I want to open a drawer in an office, and I want it to shut again afterwards as though nothing had happened, and I don’t propose to tell you why.”

He took the quill out of his mouth. It began at one corner. There had been none of it since the day before, and she found she had been watching for it, as one watches for the post.

“Fetch the tea caddy,” said Potter.

It took twenty minutes. It was a nasty little Gryffindor sort of charm that he had learnt from somebody who was dead, and it needed one to be rude to the lock under one’s breath. She made him do it twice on the tea caddy. He lay on the rug afterwards with his arms behind his head, and he did not say anything else about it at all. She could have kissed him for that; and on consideration she did.

The notebook was in the bottom left-hand drawer, under a box of lace-edged envelopes, tied up in pink.

She had seen it go in. She had been seeing it go in, one way and another, since November. It was the Saturday. Goyle had remarked at breakfast, to her egg, that the kitchens had been told to send up no tray. Zabini had undertaken, for a consideration he did not name, to have a question about the Squad’s expenses that would keep Filch on the stairs until eleven. Professor Umbridge had been called to the Minister, and she had asked Lyra, in her little breathless way, whether she would be a dear and water the African violets. So she stood in that dreadful room with the kittens looking on and a small brass can in her left hand. She was rude to the lock. It gave a shocked click.

It was an ordinary exercise book with a blue cover. *W. Hopkins, 5th Yr, History of Magic* had been ruled out on the front. She untied the ribbon, and she stood and read it through by the window. He wrote a careful round hand, like somebody crossing a stream on stones. *Potter, H. Since 6th Sept. Back of right hand. Won’t say how many lines. Bones, S. Twice. Left hand, she writes with her left. Macmillan, E.* There were nine of them, in that hand, and they stopped halfway down a page. After that somebody else had gone on. There was a different ink, and a small upright rounded script with little circles for the dots. Umbridge had kept it up. She had added five more, in date order, with the number of lines beside each and a neat tick; she had been keeping it, it appeared, for pleasure.

The last name on the list was *Hopkins, W. Three evenings. Most satisfactory. Assisted by Miss M.*

Lyra read that line. It was very nicely written. She looked at it until a kitten behind her began to wash its ear.

The quill was in the same drawer, in a long box, on a little velvet bed. She did not touch it with her hand. She took it out in a lace-edged envelope. She put the can down, and she tore the last page out of the back of the notebook, along the stitching, quite slowly. She folded it in four. There was no reason why anybody should have to read about Miss M. It had nothing to do with the case.

She watered the violets. She was thorough about it. There was no sense in being careless.

“I need something written,” said Lyra.

Crabbe was by the window of the dormitory, with the green light on her pale square face. She had not turned round. She had stopped doing that. She got up when Lyra came in, and she went on with what she was doing, and if there was a seat free she took another one. She said “Good morning.” It was all perfectly civil. It had been going on for four and a half months, and Lyra had found that one could get used to it, in the way that one can get used to a cold room.

“Not in my hand. It can’t be in anybody’s that the Ministry’s ever seen. You’ve got the plainest writing in the school, Vanessa. It looks like a laundry list. It’s four lines.”

She held out the paper. She had thought about what to say. She had thought about it on the stairs. Crabbe looked at the paper, and then she took it, by the corner; and she read it standing up.

*To Madam Bones, Department of Magical Law Enforcement. Your niece’s left hand. Ask her to take off her glove. The rest is enclosed. Ask what the quill is for.*

It took her some time. Crabbe read everything slowly. Lyra watched her get to the end, and go back, and read the word *niece* again. Then she walked across to her own bed, and sat down on it, and pulled out the box from underneath, and found a pen. She wrote it out. She blotted it. She held it out to Lyra at the full length of her arm.

“It’s not going to give him his hand back,” said Crabbe.

“No.”

“I want that understood.”

“Yes,” said Lyra.

For a moment the pale eyes looked at her: slow, careful, thorough. She could not read them at all. She had been able to read that face since she was eleven. It came to her that she did not know, now, what Crabbe thought about anything; that she had not known since November; that a very great deal seemed to have been going on behind that face without her.

“This isn’t anything,” said Crabbe, and she went back to the window.

There remained the matter of the post.

It was six o’clock on the Monday morning, and still dark, and Goyle was sitting on the edge of her bed in the vast flannel nightgown, doing up her shoes. Lyra stood in front of her with a package in her hand, sealed, with nothing on the outside. It was the first time that she had asked her for anything since a Tuesday in March. She had thought about that as well.

Goyle looked at the package. She did not take it. She looked up, and her big soft face had nothing much on it at this hour.

“Is it a nice one?” said Goyle.

There were a number of things that one could say. Lyra looked at them. It had a boy’s hand in it, in a blue exercise book, and a line that she had torn off the end.

“It’s the nicest I ever wrote,” said Lyra.

Goyle considered that, for about as long as it took to tie a bow. Then she held out both hands. She took it as she had once taken a tin, as though it might go off, and she put it in the pocket of her dressing gown, and went away up the stairs, one at a time, towards the Owlery.

Madam Bones came to breakfast on the Thursday.

It was the last day of term before Easter. The doors of the Great Hall opened at twenty to nine, while the post was still coming down, and a square grey-haired witch with a monocle walked in, and two tall Aurors behind her. She walked straight up the middle of the room. She did not hurry. She carried a blue exercise book under her arm and a long lace-edged envelope in her hand. It went quiet from the doors inward, table by table, like a tide going out.

Professor Umbridge had a piece of toast. She had been nibbling it, with her little finger out. She looked up, and her wide face spread into a smile of the most delighted surprise.

“*Amelia!*” she said. “Why, how perfectly—”

“Dolores.”

She had stopped at the foot of the staff table. Professor Dumbledore had got to his feet. He did not say anything. He stood with his long fingers resting on the cloth, and he looked, as far as one could see behind the half-moon spectacles, like a man listening to a piece of music that he had been waiting for a long time to hear.

“I have here a blood quill,” said Madam Bones. She had a hard flat voice that carried to the back of the Hall without appearing to try. “It’s a Dark instrument, and it has been on the Ministry’s proscribed list since 1809. I have it from an unsigned source. I’ve also got a notebook, which seems to be a list of pupils at this school. I should tell you that I came up on Tuesday evening to see my niece.” She laid the exercise book down on the table, between the marmalade and the Minister’s appointee. She turned round, and she looked out across the four long tables, with the monocle flashing. “It’s in two hands. The first says W. Hopkins. Is Mr Hopkins in the room?”

Nobody moved.

Lyra had her cup in her hand. She had thought about this as well. She had thought about it in some detail, and in everything she had thought, he had stayed where he was. Why should he not? He was the sort of boy who said sorry to tablecloths. He had been sitting at that table for four and a half months with his right hand under it; he had been Umbridge’s for three evenings. He would keep his head down. Madam Bones would read the names aloud, and that would be that. All she had needed was for the thing to be done.

There was a small scraping noise.

Somebody at the Hufflepuff table had pushed back the bench. He got up, rather slowly, and he had to steady himself on the shoulder of the person next to him, who was Cedric Diggory, and who looked at him and took his hand away from the knife. He was long and mild and spotty. His tie was done up very well. He came out from behind the table. He walked up the side of the Hall, with six hundred people looking at him, with his right hand held against his chest as if it were something that he was carrying for someone else. It took a long time. He stopped in front of the square grey witch, and he went scarlet to the ears.

“Sorry,” said Wayne Hopkins. “That’s me.”

Madam Bones looked down at him for a moment. Her face did not alter. She took the notebook, and held it out.

“Can you read this for me, Mr Hopkins? I’ve left my other glasses in the office.”

He took it with his left hand. He had some trouble with the page. Everybody in the Great Hall could see that he had some trouble. Umbridge had stopped smiling. She said, “Now, *really*, dear—” and one of the tall Aurors moved half a step, and she did not go on.

“Potter, H.,” read Hopkins.

It was a thin, careful voice, and it shook. He cleared his throat.

“Since the sixth of September.”

Down the Hall, at the Gryffindor table, somebody stood up.

He did not say anything. He just got up, with his hair on end and his toast left on his plate, and stood there, with his hands at his sides. He did not look at the staff table, or the Slytherins, or at anybody.

“Bones, S.”

There was a sound from the Hufflepuffs. A sturdy girl with a plait had risen to her feet. She had started to cry, and she was holding up a hand with a glove on it. “Macmillan, E.” He stood. “Thomas, D.” “Abbott, H.” They were getting up all over the room. It went on. They stood up one at a time, here and there along the benches, a third-year, a big Ravenclaw, a very small boy who had to be helped over the seat, and each of them stayed where they were. No one had told them to. He read out the nine in his own hand. He turned the page, and he went on, in that small shaking stubborn voice, with what had been written by somebody else. There were thirteen. By the time he had got to the bottom of the sheet there was no other sound at all but the owls.

He stopped. He looked at the page. He looked at the place at the bottom where it ended, and at the torn edge of the paper in the stitching, and a slight puzzled line came between his eyebrows.

“And you, Mr Hopkins?” said Madam Bones. “I don’t see your own name.”

Lyra put down her cup.

It made no noise at all on the saucer. She saw him stand there, with his thumb on the torn place. He raised his head. He looked, in a mild, tired, slightly frowning way, along the length of the room; it went over the Ravenclaws; he had very light eyes. She had never known what colour they were. It went over Montague and Parkinson, and it came to the end of the Slytherin table, and it stopped. He looked at her.

It may have been a second. He did not do anything with his face.

“I’d like to be on it, please,” said Wayne Hopkins. “It’s just been left off.”

He held the notebook up, in his left hand, for Madam Bones to take. Then he turned round, carefully, and stood beside the others, with his back to the staff table and his bad hand against his chest.

She sat with her hands in her lap. He could not know. He could not possibly know. It was in her pocket at this moment. He had only looked. Anybody might look.

Goyle pushed the toast rack an inch towards her. She did not see it.

They took Professor Umbridge out by the middle of the Hall. She went quite quietly. Her little bow had slipped. She had her handbag held up in front of her in both hands, and an Auror at either elbow; and it was some time before anybody in that enormous silence understood that she was saying something, in a high, puzzled, reasonable voice, over and over, to nobody, to all of them. It was all a misunderstanding. There were proper channels. The Minister would be most— At the end of the Slytherin table she stopped. She looked down. The small round eyes had gone quite wide and wet, and there was a crumb on her lip.

“Miss Malfoy,” said Professor Umbridge. “Tell them, dear. You saw. You were there.” Her soft damp hand had come out, and it rested for a moment on the edge of the table. “But you’re a *good* girl.”

Lyra looked up.

There were fourteen people standing in the Great Hall, and one of them had turned his head. A monocle had turned as well.

“Yes, Professor,” said Lyra.

He picked her up.

He had been waiting behind the door. She had not got it properly shut. It was a quarter past ten, on the last night of term. She had come straight up from Professor Snape, who had said nothing whatever about the morning, and had gone through her head twice as hard as usual. It was not on any timetable; and he took her round the middle and lifted her clean off the bald rug, and went round twice with her, knocking over the tea caddy, and put her down in the chair, and fell on top of the arm of it, laughing. She had never heard him make such a noise. He looked about nine.

“*Fourteen!*” said Potter. “Did you *see*? He got *up!* Hopkins! I thought I was going to be sick. The whole— Did you see McGonagall? Did you see *Filch?* He’s gone and taken all her decrees down himself. He’s got them in a wheelbarrow. Seamus says she’ll get five years. Dumbledore’s given the whole school a holiday on Saturday. It *is* a holiday on Saturday.”

“I can’t imagine what you’re talking about.”

“A drawer,” said Potter.

“I was curious about drawers.”

“‘A person of limited imagination.’” He had got his breath. He had pushed his glasses up, and he was looking at her, with his whole face. She had seen him look at a dragon, and at a Cup. She had seen him look at the sea. She had not been looked at like that by anybody, she thought, ever; not on a platform; not on her birthday. It went into her somewhere under the collarbone like hot tea. “It was you. You sent that. You got a Malfoy’s daughter’s hand on the Ministry’s own— You did that for them. You did it for *him*. Oh, you absolute— Do you know what Hermione said? She went white. She put a tick. She said ‘*Somebody* had to’, and then she went off and sat by herself in the owlery for an hour.”

“You have ink on your nose.”

“I love you,” said Potter. “I know, *hold still*. I love you, I love you. I don’t care. Look at you. You’re the most frightening person I’ve ever met in my life.”

He kissed her. He got most of her left eyebrow, and then he got it right. He had her by the front of her robes, with both fists, and his forehead came down against hers; he was out of breath and he was shaking with it. She found that she was holding on to his ears. There was a hard square of folded paper in her pocket. *Most satisfactory. Assisted by.* She could feel its edges. She had thought it would have stopped being there. It seemed that one could sit in a chair with that in one’s pocket, and be looked at like this; that the one thing did not do anything at all to the other. She had supposed there would be some sort of arrangement. *He has a pie on Tuesdays.* She wanted to go on being what he was looking at. She shut her eyes, and turned her face into his collar, and stayed there.

“*Mine*,” said Potter.

He said it to the top of her head, very low. He had said it a good many times by now. It had been a joke; it was a word for the armchair, for the last of Goyle’s cake; she had taken ten points for it. It was not one now. She could hear that. His hands had stopped shaking. There was something at the bottom of his voice that she had heard once, on a landing, and it did not go with the laughing. It frightened her. She noted that, and she noted that she did not mind.

She ought to have said something. There was any number of things that one could say.

“Yes,” said Lyra.

It was true. That was the thing that was the matter with it. Nobody in her life had ever been so sure of her as that; and she sat there in the firelight, in the most comfortable chair in the world, with a page in her pocket, and she let him be.

The last of the Squad’s badges went into Filch’s wheelbarrow. *How careless of you to lose her*, her father wrote, by return, and that was all he had to say about it.

She saw Hopkins once more before the train. It was in the Entrance Hall, among the trunks. Diggory was doing up his tie for him, in full view of everybody, with no expression whatever, and saying something; and a long thin boy with a bad hand was nodding, and he had something held out in his left. She could not see what it was. It might have been a Chocolate Frog card, with a crease in it.

He was trying to say thank you. She could tell, even at that distance, by the set of his ears.

She waited until they had gone by, and she went out to the carriages.
