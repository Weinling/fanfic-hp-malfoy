Draco had witnessed something he simply could not believe.

"My sister... is being blackmailed."

A clandestine meeting between Carina Malfoy and Harry Potter. The two had exchanged words in a rather sharp tone, and then—for reasons he couldn't fathom—had suddenly seized each other's faces and kissed. For Draco, hidden away in the luggage compartment, it had been a sight shocking enough to stop his heart.

Draco was young, but as befitted a child of a pureblood family, he knew a great many things.

Harry Potter was the Malfoy family's sworn enemy. Kissing was something mothers and fathers did. And the two of them had kissed.

"My sister and Harry Potter... becoming like Mum and Dad?"

What utter nonsense. Draco shook his head vigorously. Sworn enemies were people who fought each other; married couples were people who loved each other. This was a contradiction.

He felt a small flicker of pride at having recalled such a difficult word as contradiction, then shook his head again.

"It must be blackmail. There's no other explanation!"

Draco didn't particularly like his sister.

The age gap between them was barely anything—only four years—yet she treated him like a small child. It also irritated him that she would receive some precious gift and then lend it to Draco as though she were doing him a great favour. And through it all, she was forever putting on airs.

Once he got to Hogwarts, he would crush her utterly—academically, on the Quidditch pitch, any way he could—and wring a full surrender from her. Draco had sworn this to himself long before he ever set foot in the school.

And yet, however much she annoyed him, she was his sister—a Malfoy by name.

If he helped her now, she'd owe him a debt. She might stop looking down on him. She might even be impressed, might see him in a new light.

With a faint swell of self-satisfaction—and just the smallest, most reluctant thread of concern for his sister—Draco made his way toward the Great Hall.

A peculiar undercurrent had settled over Hogwarts' Great Hall.

Several students clutched copies of The Quibbler to their chests, whispering to one another as though the magazine contained some closely guarded secret. Others seemed far more curious about whatever was tucked inside their classmates' robes than about the Sorting ceremony that was about to begin.

The Slytherins, meanwhile, stood apart from this atmosphere entirely. Only a handful of them had read The Quibbler, and those who had knew perfectly well that the contents were rubbish—yet for reasons of their own, they couldn't say so aloud, and their faces were tight with the effort of keeping quiet.

Ginny Weasley slid into the seat beside Harry, looking rather pleased with herself.

"See? I told you people would read it if it was entertaining enough. Not bad, right?"

Over the summer, Harry had done everything he could to get the truth out. But the Daily Prophet operated squarely under the Ministry's thumb, running one unflattering story about Dumbledore after another. And Harry, a minor who couldn't Apparate, had precious little room to manoeuvre.

It was Ginny who had suggested a particular magazine as a means of spreading the truth.

That magazine was The Quibbler.

It was a gossip rag that ran, without a hint of irony, articles claiming that Sirius Black was the lead vocalist of a famous band. Even Harry, who could usually manage a smile at most things, had gone stone-faced the first time he'd seen it.

He had read through the magazine carefully, made Sirius actually sing something, and arrived at the firm conclusion that The Quibbler was rubbish that didn't bother to verify a single fact. If Ginny hadn't been watching, he might well have tossed it into the fireplace as a meal for an Ashwinder.

But Ginny had been relentless.

"It's not the most rigorous publication, I'll grant you that. But no matter how absurd the content—if knowing something makes you feel a little bit special, people will believe it."

And this was the result.

The article had run in a thoroughly unreliable magazine, yet its reach had been remarkable. Harry wasn't even sure whether running the same piece in the Daily Prophet would have produced a similar effect.

Those who wanted to believe, believed. Those who didn't, didn't. That was partly down to The Quibbler's reputation for printing nonsense in the first place.

Either way, it had planted a seed of doubt in people's minds, exactly as intended—so Harry couldn't complain too loudly.

"I feel rather like I've become Professor Trelawney. It's not a pleasant sensation."

He'd felt this way before. In Divination, watching students hang on every word of Trelawney's absurd pronouncements—that was precisely the feeling. How many hours had he suffered through her rambling about Venus this and Mars that, week after week?

If genuine prophecies hadn't occasionally surfaced amid all the fabricated ones, Harry might have stormed out like Hermione. Remarkably, it was Trelawney—intermittent prophet that she was—who kept him sitting through a class he otherwise despised.
