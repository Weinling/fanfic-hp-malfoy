# Chapter 36: Below Stairs

“Dumbledore sent for me,” said Potter, on the fifth-floor landing. “Last night.”

It was the first Tuesday in March. Moody had gone down past them a moment before without a word, the blue eye turning backwards in his head to keep them in it, as a man keeps an eye on two coins he suspects of being forged; Lyra had been getting that look twice a week since January, and had stopped minding it in February, more or less.

Lyra did not stop climbing. “What for?”

“Moody. Sort of.” He was quiet for half a flight. The portraits on that stretch of staircase had given up leaning out to watch them by the end of January. “He wanted to know how sure I’d been. About the trunk. Before I went in.”

“And what did you say?”

“I said completely.”

“And?”

Potter shifted the bag on his shoulder.

“He said, *That is what troubles me.*” He said it in a fair imitation of the voice, gentle and unhurried, and it was not funny. “And then he gave me a lemon drop and sent me to bed.”

Lyra climbed two more steps.

He had told her. She had not asked. She did not say so, in case he stopped.

“Well,” said Lyra. “He’s quite right. It *is* troubling. You’re very rarely completely anything. You’re usually only completely wrong.”

“Thanks.”

“It was a lemon drop, I suppose, and not a Toffee Éclair.”

“It’s always lemon drops.”

“That,” said Lyra, “is the most troubling thing you’ve told me.”

He laughed, and they went on up to the seventh floor, and at the door of the Arithmancy classroom he stopped, as he had stopped every morning since January, and did not go in, and did not quite go away either.

“Divination,” said Lyra. “North Tower. You’ll be late.”

“I’m always late.” He did not move. “Lyra.”

“What?”

“Nothing,” said Potter. “See you at lunch.”

He went off down the corridor with his hands in his pockets, and Lyra went in to Arithmancy and sat down in her usual place by the window, and opened her book at the right page, and sat with her quill in her hand until Professor Vector had to say her name twice.

The Hogsmeade weekend was the second Saturday in March, and it was the first time they had ever walked down the drive together in daylight with nobody under anything.

It was not, Lyra decided, what she had imagined. She had imagined it a great many times, in the summer, behind her bed-hangings, and in every version they had been alone. In none of them had Weasley been there, walking on Potter’s other side with his hands in his pockets, complaining about the Chudley Cannons, and Granger behind them reading a book as she walked and somehow never falling into a puddle; and in none of them had half the school been coming down the drive behind them in a long, cheerful, staring procession, with Parkinson somewhere in the middle of it telling anybody who would listen that it was all for her father.

She did not mind as much as she would have expected. She minded Weasley, a little.

The Three Broomsticks was as hot and crowded and loud as it had been the first time, in her third year, when she had looked for him in it and not found him. Today he went to the bar for four butterbeers and came back with them, and sat down next to her at a table in the middle of the room where everybody could see, and the whole pub watched him do it. A table of Ravenclaws by the window had stopped talking altogether. Two Slytherin sixth-years at the bar were staring at her over their tankards.

Lyra took her butterbeer, and looked at the table of Ravenclaws until they started talking again.

“You paid for these,” she said to Potter.

“Yes.”

“All four.”

“Yes.”

“That puts me in your debt,” said Lyra. “I shall pay for the next ones.”

“You can’t,” said Weasley at once. “It’s Harry’s round. And then it’s Hermione’s round, and then it’s my round, and then it’s yours. That’s how it goes.”

“That is not how it goes. That is a system designed by a Weasley so that a Weasley never has to pay for anything until everybody has gone home.”

Weasley went red. “It is *not*—”

Granger, without looking up from her book, said, “She’s got you there, Ron,” and turned a page.

“Whose side are you on?”

“Nobody’s. I’m reading.”

They argued about it for ten minutes, and then about the Chudley Cannons for another ten, and then about whether Krum would have caught the Snitch at the World Cup if Lynch had not hit the ground first. It was not an argument; Lyra had been in the top box and Weasley had been in the row in front of her, and they both knew perfectly well what had happened; and somewhere in the middle of it Lyra realised that she was enjoying herself. She had quarrelled with Weasley at intervals for nearly four years, on staircases and in the grounds and on the gravel outside the doors, and every one of those quarrels had been meant. This one was not. They were both doing it because it was what they did, like two old men on a village bench who would be lost without the other one to be wrong.

She thought of saying so, and decided he would take it badly, and said instead that the Chudley Cannons had not won a match since the reign of Queen Victoria, which he took worse.

Potter did not say much. He sat between them with his butterbeer and looked from one to the other, and every so often he put his head down on his arms on the table and laughed until his shoulders shook, and once, when Weasley said something so stupid about the Wimbourne Wasps that even Granger looked up, Potter’s hand found Lyra’s under the table and held on to it, as though he needed something to keep him upright.

She let him.

When they came out it had begun, very lightly, to rain, the fine silvery rain of March that hardly seemed to fall, and the High Street was full of students going from Honeydukes to Zonko’s and back with their collars up. Granger had gone ahead with her book over her head. Weasley had stopped to look in the window of Dervish and Banges.

They were outside the post office. Lyra had stopped to pull her hood up, and Potter had stopped because she had, and he looked at her, in the rain, with drops on his glasses and the High Street going past on either side of them; and then he bent his head and kissed her.

It was very short, in the middle of Hogsmeade on a wet Saturday afternoon, between the post office and a shop that sold quills.

“Oh, *honestly*,” said Weasley, from the window of Dervish and Banges.

Lyra stepped back, and straightened her hood.

“Not,” she said, with great dignity, “in front of the post office.”

“Right,” said Potter. “Sorry.” He did not look sorry. “Where, then?”

Lyra walked on up the street without answering, with her hood up and her face perfectly composed, and did not look back to see whether he was following, because she could hear that he was.

“How,” said Lyra, “does one get into the kitchens?”

Granger looked up from her book. They were in the library, on the Sunday afternoon, at the table by the window, which Lyra had begun to sit at without asking and Granger had begun to let her; there was a pile of Arithmancy between them and nobody else within three shelves.

“Why?”

Lyra put her hand into the pocket of her robes, and took out a small badge that said S.P.E.W., and pinned it, carefully and straight, to the front of her jumper.

Granger stared at it.

“Society business,” said Lyra.

There was a pause in which Granger appeared to try out several faces and reject all of them.

“There’s a corridor,” she said at last, slowly, “down the stairs past the Great Hall, on the right. The one that goes towards Hufflepuff. There’s a painting of a bowl of fruit. You tickle the pear.”

“I tickle the pear.”

“It giggles,” said Granger. “I’m only warning you.”

“Of course it does,” said Lyra, and unpinned the badge, and put it away.

⁂

The pear giggled.

It squirmed under her finger, and giggled, and turned into a large green door-handle, and Lyra opened the door and went down three steps into the biggest kitchen she had ever seen.

It lay directly under the Great Hall, as she ought to have guessed, and it was the same size: a vast high room with a stone ceiling, and heaps of copper pots and pans gleaming along the walls, and an enormous fireplace at the far end, and four long wooden tables standing exactly where the four House tables stood above, so that she understood at once how the food came up. And everywhere there were house-elves. There must have been a hundred. They were cleaning and stirring and scrubbing and carrying, and every single one of them stopped what it was doing when the door opened, and turned its great eyes on her, and bowed, all together, so low that their noses touched the flagstones.

Then they rushed at her with trays.

She took a cup of tea, since it was simpler than refusing, and said, “Thank you,” to the elf who gave it to her, as she said it at home to Pobble. It seemed to astonish the elf very much. At the edge of her eye, something moved. A very small elf in a tea-cosy hat and two odd socks had been carrying a stack of plates past the end of the Ravenclaw table; it had seen her, and dropped behind a cauldron as though it had been shot, plates and all, and a pair of huge green eyes looked out at her over the rim.

She knew those eyes. She had known them all her life, at the end of the long table at the Manor, until the summer a boy had tricked her father into handing over a sock.

She did not call to him. She looked away, and let him stay behind his cauldron, and drank her tea.

“The elf of Mr Crouch’s,” she said to the one with the tray. “Is she here?”

The elf’s ears went down. A ripple of something went through the others, and several of them looked at the fireplace, and then quickly away from it, as people look away from a guest who has drunk too much at a wedding.

“Winky is not herself, miss,” whispered the elf.

Winky was on a low stool beside the fire, with her back to the room. She was wearing a little blouse and skirt that had once been blue, and had a stain down the front of them now, and a hat with holes for her ears; and she had a bottle of butterbeer in her lap, and was swaying, very slightly, with her eyes on the flames.

Lyra took another stool, and set it down beside her, and sat on it.

Behind her, the whole kitchen made a small, scandalised sound.

“You’re Mr Crouch’s elf,” said Lyra.

Winky turned her head. Her eyes were the size of tennis balls and swollen nearly shut, and when she saw who it was, they filled up again at once and ran over.

“Winky is *not* Mr Crouch’s elf! Winky is not anybody’s elf! Winky is being given *clothes*—” and she wailed, and put her face into her stained little skirt, and rocked.

Granger, Lyra thought, would have patted her. Granger would have told her that it was wonderful to be free, and that she ought to ask for wages, and that she had rights; and Winky would have howled the castle down.

Lyra did not pat her.

“Sit up, Winky,” she said.

She said it in her mother’s voice: the voice for the drawing room when something had been spilt, quiet and certain and not unkind. It went through Winky like a bell. The elf sat up on her stool, with her hands in her lap and her ears up, and stopped crying in the middle of a sob, as she must have stopped a thousand times before in another house, for another voice.

“That’s better. Drink your butterbeer, if you must. And tell me about Mr Crouch.”

Winky drank, and hiccupped, and told her. It came out in pieces, and most of the pieces were the same piece over and over: that Winky had kept her master’s secrets, that Winky had always kept them, that Winky was a good elf and would never tell, never, not if they gave her a hundred sets of clothes. That Master was not well. That Master was not eating; Winky knew he was not eating, because who was there to make his tea now, who was there to see that he ate a little bit of something when he came home so tired?

“He is not well, miss,” said Winky. “Winky knows. Winky is always knowing when Master is not well.”

“What’s the matter with him?”

But Winky only shook her head, and drank, and said it again.

Then she said something else.

“Winky was *minding the seat*.” She said it to the fire, in a hurt, bewildered voice, as though it were the thing she had turned over most. “At the Quidditch. In the top box. Winky was minding it so good, miss. Winky is not liking heights, Winky was putting her hands over her eyes the whole time, and still Winky was minding it, and Master is giving Winky *clothes*—”

Lyra held her cup quite steady.

There had been an elf in the top box. She remembered it now, as one remembers a face from a crowd: at the far end of their own row, on a gilt chair, bolt upright, with both hands over its eyes, minding the empty chair beside it for a master too important to arrive on time. She had thought it very poor form. And at the first Irish goal, the empty chair had creaked, as though somebody had sat down in it to watch.

She had not thought about it since August. She thought about it now, and could not make it mean anything.

“Whose seat was it, Winky?”

Winky’s eyes went wide, and then narrow, and she clutched her bottle to her chest.

“Winky is not saying. Winky is keeping her master’s secrets. Winky is a *good elf*—” and her hand came up, quite fast, to strike herself across the head with the bottle, as an elf will.

“Don’t,” said Lyra sharply, and Winky’s hand stopped in the air.

They sat for a while by the fire. The kitchen had gone back to its work around them, very quietly, as though there were somebody ill in the house.

“You want to go back to him,” said Lyra.

Winky looked at her.

“Winky is wanting to *stay*, miss,” she whispered. “Winky was only ever wanting to stay.”

Lyra looked at the fire.

She knew a house where an elf had cried for a week in the scullery because she was sure she would be sent away. She knew what it was to want to stay in a house, even a house with something wrong in it. She had never heard anybody say it so plainly.

“A house is a house,” said Lyra at last. “Even when the master is a fool.”

Winky put her bottle down on the hearth, and folded her hands, and looked up at her with her drowned eyes as though nobody had said anything sensible to her in seven months.

When Lyra went back up the three steps and out past the pear, there was an éclair in a napkin in her hand that the elf with the tray had pressed on her at the door, and a secret of Mr Crouch’s in her head that she did not know what to do with; and behind her, from behind a cauldron at the end of the Ravenclaw table, a pair of green eyes watched her all the way out.

She went back to the kitchens once more, in the first week of April, and came away with a raw steak wrapped in brown paper, which the elves gave her with an air of having always known she would ask.

She took it down the lawn on a bright, cold morning to the pumpkin patch behind Hagrid’s cabin, where the hippogriff was tethered, and stopped at the proper distance, and bowed. Buckbeak looked at her with one fierce orange eye. Then he bent his scaly knees and bowed back, and she went up to him and unwrapped the paper and held it out on the flat of her hand, and he took it as delicately as a cat, and ate it, and allowed her to touch his neck.

It had been on the books for fourteen months. She walked back up the lawn lighter by a steak.

Hagrid was at his window. She saw the curtain move. He did not come out, and she did not go to the door, and they left it at that; but when she looked back from the top of the slope, the curtain had been pulled right back, and he was still there.

That week she finished the letter to Professor Lupin, which had said *Dear Professor Lupin* and nothing else since November. It came to a page, about Buckbeak, mostly, and the weather, and she signed it *Yours sincerely*, exactly as he had, and thought he would understand that perfectly.

Draco’s letter came on the Saturday.

It was short, for Draco, and almost all of it was about the Falmouth Falcons, who had lost to Puddlemere on a disgraceful decision by the referee, and about a broom he had seen in a catalogue that Father said he was too young for and that he was not too young for, and would she please write to Father and say so. At the bottom, squeezed in where he had run out of room:

*The gentleman has gone abroad for a few weeks, to see some old friends. He says he has not seen them in a very long time and it is high time he did. He left me his chess set to practise on while he is away, the one with the pieces that argue, and he says when he comes back he will beat me in twelve moves instead of nine, so I am practising very hard.*

Lyra laughed at the twelve moves, a little late.

She wrote back to him that night, a whole page, to tell him that she would write to Father about the broom when the Falcons had won a match, and that if he lost in twelve moves he was not trying, and that he should keep his queen at home until the gentleman had brought his out.
